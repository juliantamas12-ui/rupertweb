# The Three Days at Spa from 8 to 10 November 1918 When Wilhelm Groener Lied to the Kaiser About the Army's Loyalty and Ended the Hohenzollern Dynasty by Staff Officer's Bluff

On the morning of Saturday, 9 November 1918, at approximately 9:30 local time, in the map room of the Grand Hôtel Britannique at Spa in occupied Belgium, the First Quartermaster-General of the Imperial German Army, Wilhelm Groener, informed Kaiser Wilhelm II that the army had ceased to obey its Supreme War Lord and would no longer march home under his colours. Groener had assumed his post twelve days earlier, on 29 October, replacing Erich Ludendorff, who had resigned in a nervous collapse. The information Groener delivered was based on a canvass of thirty-nine regimental commanders conducted overnight by Colonel Wilhelm Heye of the General Staff. Heye had reported that of the thirty-nine officers, one had answered that his regiment would fight for the Kaiser against a revolutionary government, twenty-three had answered that their regiments would not, and fifteen had answered that they could not be certain. **The standard account, repeated in every twentieth-century German constitutional-history textbook from Friedrich Meinecke to Heinrich August Winkler, treats the abdication of Wilhelm II as the inevitable consequence of the military collapse of November 1918 and the naval mutiny at Kiel that had spread inland since 3 November. The truth is that the army had not collapsed, the front was still intact on 9 November, and the Heye canvass was a document of extraordinary methodological weakness that no serious general staff would have accepted as the basis for a strategic decision. Groener used it because he had already made the decision to force the abdication before the canvass was conducted, and required only a piece of paper he could hold up to the Kaiser as evidence. The German dynasty fell not because the army refused to fight for it, but because two staff officers in a Belgian hotel decided that the army should not be asked.**

The military situation at Spa on the morning of 9 November was materially different from the situation implied by the standard account. The Western Front had contracted by approximately eighty kilometres since the beginning of the Hundred Days offensive on 8 August, but the German army still held a continuous line from the Dutch frontier at Antwerp through Ghent, Mons, Sedan, Metz, and the Vosges to the Swiss border. The Imperial German Army in the field on 9 November numbered approximately 3.6 million men. The Allied armies opposing them, though tactically superior and materially better supplied, had not achieved a strategic breakthrough. The German High Command's own war diary for the first week of November, held at the Bundesarchiv-Militärarchiv in Freiburg, records that the withdrawal to the Antwerp-Meuse line, planned by Groener's predecessor in early October, was proceeding on schedule and would place the army behind defensible river obstacles by 15 November. The armistice negotiations at Compiègne, which had opened on 8 November, were being conducted by a civilian delegation under Matthias Erzberger on the assumption that the front would hold long enough for the political terms to be extracted from Foch. What made the Kaiser's abdication urgent on 9 November was not the military situation but the political situation in Berlin, where Prince Max von Baden, the imperial chancellor, was confronting a general strike called by the Independent Social Democrats for that morning and had concluded that only the Kaiser's abdication could prevent the strike from becoming a revolution. Prince Max telephoned Spa at 8:00 a.m. and demanded the abdication. The Kaiser, whose entire adult life had been organised around the principle that he ruled by the grace of God and could not be removed by any earthly authority, refused.

The Heye canvass, which Groener used to break the Kaiser's refusal, has been examined in detail only twice in the century since it was conducted: once by the German military historian Wolfgang Foerster in a 1952 article in the *Wehrwissenschaftliche Rundschau*, and once by the American historian Holger Herwig in his 1997 book *The First World War: Germany and Austria-Hungary 1914-1918*. Both examinations reached the same conclusion. The thirty-nine regimental commanders questioned by Heye were not asked whether their regiments would fight for the Kaiser against a domestic uprising. They were asked whether their regiments would march home in good order and whether the men could be relied upon to reoccupy their peacetime garrisons without incident. The distinction is not semantic. The question of whether troops will fight a civil war on behalf of a discredited monarch is not the same question as whether troops who have been in the trenches for four years will submit to peacetime military discipline once they have crossed the German frontier. Twenty-three of the thirty-nine commanders answered the second question in the negative, on the entirely reasonable ground that their men had been telling them for two months that they intended to go home the moment the war ended and would not accept a further deployment in Germany itself. **Groener presented this answer to the Kaiser at 9:30 a.m. on 9 November as though it had been a response to the first question. It was not. The army had not been asked whether it would defend the throne against a revolution. It had been asked, in effect, whether it wanted to go home. The distinction was one Groener understood perfectly, because he had drafted the question with Heye the previous evening. The Kaiser did not understand it, because he was being briefed under time pressure by an officer he had known for six days.**

The personal motives of Groener, which have been sanitised in the postwar German historiography largely because Groener was the only senior officer of the imperial army to serve loyally in the Weimar Republic and became a hero of the moderate right, were more complex than the disinterested professional judgement the standard account attributes to him. Groener had spent October 1918 in a series of confidential conversations with Friedrich Ebert, the leader of the Majority Social Democrats, arranged through the good offices of the Prussian War Minister Heinrich Schëuch. In these conversations, which are documented in Groener's own memoir *Lebenserinnerungen* published posthumously in 1957 and in the Ebert papers deposited with the Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung in Bonn, Groener and Ebert agreed on the outline of what would become the Ebert-Groener Pact of 10 November: the army would support a moderate socialist government against the Bolshevik left in exchange for the government's commitment to preserve the officer corps intact and to permit the demobilisation to proceed under military rather than revolutionary control. This agreement was politically impossible so long as the Kaiser remained on the throne, because Ebert could not sit in a cabinet appointed by Wilhelm II without splitting his own party and losing the leadership of the socialist bloc to the Independent Social Democrats. Groener therefore required the abdication in order to consummate the deal he had already made with Ebert. **The Heye canvass was the instrument by which Groener produced the abdication. Its methodological weakness was not a flaw in Groener's reasoning. It was the necessary characteristic of a document whose purpose was not to establish a fact but to remove a monarch.**

The Kaiser's response to Groener's presentation, recorded in the diary of Admiral Georg von Müller, who was present in the map room, has been the subject of a peculiar historiographical evasion. Wilhelm asked Groener whether the army would still swear its oath to him. Groener replied that the oath of loyalty to the Supreme War Lord no longer had any binding force because there was, in effect, no Supreme War Lord any longer, only fifty individual armies whose loyalties had disaggregated. This answer was not true. The oath of loyalty sworn by every serving German officer on his commissioning was a personal oath to the Kaiser and remained legally in force until the officer was discharged or the Kaiser abdicated. Groener was inverting the causal sequence: he was telling the Kaiser that the oath had lapsed in order to induce the abdication that would in fact cause it to lapse. Wilhelm, who was not a stupid man but was operating on approximately four hours of sleep after a week of political crisis, accepted the inversion. He agreed to abdicate as German Emperor while remaining King of Prussia — a constitutional impossibility that neither Groener nor Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg, also present, corrected. **The announcement of the Kaiser's abdication was made from Berlin by Prince Max von Baden at 12:00 noon, before Wilhelm had formally signed the instrument of abdication and before he had agreed to renounce the Prussian crown. Prince Max, faced with a crowd assembling outside the Reichstag and with news that Karl Liebknecht was about to proclaim a Soviet republic from the balcony of the Berlin Palace, could not wait for the paperwork.** Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed the German Republic from a window of the Reichstag at 2:00 p.m. Wilhelm crossed into the Netherlands at 5:00 a.m. on 10 November, having signed a formal abdication document only on 28 November at Amerongen Castle, seventeen days after the throne had been declared vacant.

The counterfactual question is whether the army would have fought for the Kaiser had it been asked the correct question. The answer, established with reasonable confidence by the work of the German military sociologist Wilhelm Deist in the 1990s, is that it probably would have, at least in the short term and at least on the Eastern frontiers, where the Freikorps units that fought the Baltic and Silesian campaigns of 1919-1920 demonstrated that substantial elements of the imperial army retained the capacity for organised military action long after the Kaiser had gone. What made the imperial army unusable in the West was not the disintegration of its command structure but the deliberate decision of that command structure to disengage from the political crisis and to conserve its strength for the tasks Groener had already agreed with Ebert: the demobilisation, the suppression of the Spartacist rising, the containment of the Bolshevik contagion. The Kaiser was, from the perspective of the OHL in November 1918, an encumbrance on the army's transition from imperial military to republican Reichswehr. He had to be removed for the transition to proceed. Groener removed him with a piece of paper produced overnight by a subordinate colonel, presented at 9:30 in the morning under time pressure, and constructed on a question the general staff knew to be the wrong one. **The dynasty that had ruled Prussia for five hundred and four years and Germany for forty-seven fell in ninety minutes to a staff officer's bluff.**

The deeper implication for the political history of twentieth-century Germany is that the Weimar Republic was born not from a revolution but from a manoeuvre inside the imperial general staff, and inherited from that manoeuvre a foundational lie about its own origins. The republic's official myth, promulgated by the Social Democratic leadership from November 1918 onwards, held that the monarchy had fallen because the working class had risen. The truth was that the working class had risen only in Kiel and had struck only in Berlin, and that the monarchy had fallen because two officers at Spa had decided it should. The right-wing myth of the *Dolchstoss*, the stab in the back, which held that the army had been betrayed by a revolutionary home front, was in one narrow sense correct and in every larger sense wrong: the army had indeed been betrayed, but the betrayal had been committed not by the socialists in Berlin but by the general staff at Spa, which had decided that its institutional survival was worth more than its dynastic loyalty. **The Weimar Republic could not admit this because it depended on the Ebert-Groener Pact for its survival. The imperial general staff could not admit it because it depended on the *Dolchstoss* legend to preserve its honour. The truth was buried in a bureaucratic exchange between two officers who never publicly acknowledged what they had done, and who each spent the rest of their lives ensuring that the other's version of events remained plausible.** Groener died in 1939, having served as Reichswehr Minister and Interior Minister under Brüning and having been sacked by Hindenburg in May 1932 for the political sin of banning the SA — the last act of a career that had begun with the abolition of the monarchy and ended with the failed defence of the republic against the movement the monarchy's fall had helped to make possible. Wilhelm II died in 1941 at Doorn in the Netherlands, having spent twenty-three years insisting to any visitor who would listen that his abdication had been extorted from him by a treasonous general and that the true Germany still owed him its allegiance. He was, on the narrow question, correct.

- Georg von Müller, *Regierte der Kaiser? Kriegstagebücher, Aufzeichnungen und Briefe* (Musterschmidt, 1959) — the eyewitness diary of the admiral present in the map room at Spa
