# The Eleven Days at the End of May and Beginning of June 1967 in Tel Aviv When Levi Eshkol Lost Control of the Israeli Cabinet to Moshe Dayan and the IDF General Staff, and Discovered That the Prime Minister of Israel No Longer Decided When Israel Went to War

**Date:** 27 July 2026
**Category:** History

At 09:15 on the morning of Thursday, 1 June 1967, in the office of the Prime Minister on Kaplan Street in Tel Aviv, Levi Eshkol — sixty-one years old, born Levi Shkolnik in the town of Oratovo in the Kiev Governorate of the Russian Empire, a founder of the Deganya settlements, a lifetime Mapai apparatchik, and Prime Minister of Israel for three years and eleven days — signed a document appointing Moshe Dayan as Minister of Defence. The portfolio had been held by Eshkol himself since the resignation of David Ben-Gurion in 1963. By signing the document, Eshkol formally surrendered the ministerial authority over the Israel Defence Forces that he had inherited from Ben-Gurion. Substantively, he was ratifying a transfer of decision-making authority that had already taken place. **Between 22 May, when Gamal Abdel Nasser closed the Strait of Tiran to Israeli shipping, and 1 June, when Dayan entered the Ministry of Defence, the constitutional principle that the Israeli cabinet decided when Israel went to war had been broken. Eshkol did not know it had been broken, because the mechanism by which it was broken did not require his participation and did not produce a document he could sign or refuse to sign. It was broken by the General Staff of the IDF, in coordination with a faction of the cabinet led by Menachem Begin, through the sustained public and private repudiation of the Prime Minister's authority over a period of eleven days.** War began on the morning of 5 June. The IDF's opening operation, the destruction of the Egyptian Air Force on the ground at eleven airbases in a single hundred-and-seventy-minute strike between 07:45 and 10:35, had been planned in operational detail by Ezer Weizman and Mordechai Hod since 1963 and had been rehearsed continuously through the spring of 1967. Eshkol did not authorise the strike in the sense in which a Prime Minister of the United Kingdom would have authorised an equivalent operation. He was informed of it, accepted the inevitability of it, and voted for it in the cabinet meeting of the evening of 4 June. The chain of causation ran through the General Staff and terminated in the cabinet as a formal ratification of a decision the cabinet did not make.

The conventional Israeli account of the Six Day War, which was consolidated in the historiography by Michael Oren's *Six Days of War* in 2002 and which remains the mainstream Anglophone version, treats the events of late May and early June 1967 as a case of extended deliberation by a democratic government under existential pressure, culminating in a lawful cabinet decision to launch a pre-emptive strike. The account is not fabricated. Cabinet meetings did occur, minutes were kept, and votes were taken. **But the account misdescribes the mechanism. The cabinet did not deliberate its way to war. The cabinet was compelled to war by a coalition of the General Staff, the political opposition, and the Israeli press — a coalition that formed against Eshkol in the second week of the crisis and forced upon him, first, the surrender of the defence portfolio, and second, the acceptance of an operational plan whose timing was determined by the IDF and not by him.** The precipitating event was Eshkol's radio broadcast of the evening of 28 May, in which he attempted to explain to the Israeli public that diplomatic efforts were continuing and that a decision on war would await the return of the Foreign Minister, Abba Eban, from Washington. Eshkol, who was a native Yiddish speaker and who had never been comfortable with public Hebrew oratory, stumbled twice over words in the prepared text, corrected himself, and continued. The stumbles were audible on the broadcast. The next morning, the entire Israeli political culture — the Knesset opposition, the General Staff, the reserve officer corps mobilised on the Sinai frontier, the editors of *Ha'aretz* and *Yedioth* and *Ma'ariv*, and the wives of officers holding public rallies in Tel Aviv — turned on him. The pretext was that he had appeared indecisive to the nation at the moment of maximum danger. The substance was that a Prime Minister of Ashkenazi Yiddish origins, of the pre-state Mapai generation, was not a figure who could be entrusted with the war that the Israeli military class had been preparing since Sinai in 1956 and that it now believed was necessary and imminent.

The pressure on Eshkol over the following four days was of an intensity and coordination that has few parallels in the history of parliamentary democracies. General Ariel Sharon, commanding a divisional task force in the Negev, was quoted in the press as having said that if the government did not act, the army would act without it. General Uzi Narkiss of Central Command told a cabinet delegation that "the road to Jerusalem is open and closes tomorrow." Yitzhak Rabin, the Chief of Staff, entered a period of nervous collapse on 23 May that removed him from effective duty for forty-eight hours and left the operational planning in the hands of the Deputy Chief of Staff, Ezer Weizman, who was Dayan's brother-in-law and political ally, and who had been Chief of the Air Force from 1958 to 1966 and had personally designed the pre-emptive strike doctrine that would be executed on 5 June. **The General Staff was not a neutral instrument awaiting political direction. It was a political actor with its own preferred outcome, its own operational plan for producing that outcome, its own conception of the timing on which the plan depended, and its own communication channels — through press briefings, through reserve mobilisation, through cabinet testimony — for imposing that timing on the political level.** The demand for Dayan's appointment to the Ministry of Defence came from Begin's Gahal faction and from the National Religious Party, but its substantive advocacy came from the General Staff, which knew that Dayan — Chief of Staff from 1953 to 1958, former commander of the 1956 Sinai campaign, and the political figure most closely identified with the doctrine of Israeli offensive pre-emption — would authorise the operation on the timeline the Staff had determined. Eshkol resisted the appointment for four days. He offered the portfolio to Yigal Allon of the Ahdut HaAvoda faction as an alternative. Allon was rejected by Begin and by the General Staff as insufficiently identified with the offensive doctrine. On the evening of 31 May, in a cabinet meeting that lasted seven hours, Eshkol capitulated. Dayan was appointed. The war began four days later.

The strategic consequences of the eleven-day sequence, which propagated through Israeli political culture for the next four decades, are worth stating precisely. **The Six Day War was won, at the operational level, in the first three hours of 5 June, when the Egyptian Air Force ceased to exist as a functioning force and the Israeli victory in the ground campaign became a matter of exploitation rather than decision. But the political precedent set in the eleven days before the war was of a different character and had a longer half-life. The precedent was that the General Staff of the IDF, acting in coordination with the political right and with a mobilised civil society, could compel a sitting Prime Minister to surrender operational control of Israeli grand strategy at a moment of the Staff's choosing.** The precedent was applied again in October 1973, when Dayan — by then Minister of Defence in Golda Meir's government — failed to detect the Egyptian-Syrian preparations for the Yom Kippur War and produced the strategic surprise that nearly destroyed Israel; the failure was in significant part the consequence of a defence establishment that had, since 1967, operated with a degree of autonomy from cabinet oversight that made the necessary intelligence-political synthesis impossible. The precedent was applied in 1982, when Ariel Sharon — as Defence Minister in Menachem Begin's government — exceeded the scope of the cabinet's Lebanon authorisation and drove the IDF to Beirut in an operation whose political objectives Begin himself had not been fully briefed on. It was applied in 1996, when the intelligence services publicly repudiated the Shimon Peres government during the Israeli election campaign and contributed to the return of Benjamin Netanyahu. **The Israeli constitutional pattern, in which the military and intelligence establishments operate as autonomous political actors capable of shaping the terms on which elected governments make national security decisions, was fixed in the eleven days before the Six Day War. It has not been undone.**

Eshkol died on 26 February 1969, twenty months after the war he had not decided to fight. His obituaries in the Israeli press treated him as a transitional figure between Ben-Gurion and Golda Meir, a decent man of limited political capacity whose Yiddish inflection and pre-state Mapai instincts had been overtaken by the confidence of the sabra generation. This assessment was not wrong at the level of personality. **It was wrong at the level of causation. Eshkol did not fail to lead Israel in June 1967 because he was personally inadequate to the moment. He failed to lead Israel because the office of Prime Minister of Israel, as it was constituted in 1967, no longer had the authority to lead the country into a war of the kind the General Staff had decided to fight. The office survived. The authority did not.** The subsequent history of Israeli civil-military relations is, in a real sense, the history of Prime Ministers rediscovering this fact and either accepting it, as Meir accepted it in 1973, or attempting to overcome it, as Rabin attempted to overcome it in the Oslo period between 1993 and his assassination in November 1995, or exploiting it, as Netanyahu has exploited it in the four separate premierships that constitute his political career.

The cabinet records of the meetings of 22 May to 4 June 1967 were declassified progressively between 1997 and 2017 by the Israel State Archives and are available in the collection *Israel Documents of the Six Day War* (Israel State Archives, 2017). The most complete reconstruction of the internal Israeli decision-making sequence remains Ami Gluska's *The Israeli Military and the Origins of the 1967 War* (Routledge, 2007), which draws on the archives of the IDF General Staff and of the cabinet secretariat. The eleven days remain, in Israeli political memory, a period whose full character is understood but not openly acknowledged. The country continues to operate on the constitutional principle that the cabinet decides. The country continues to be governed on the operational principle that in a real crisis, the General Staff decides and the cabinet ratifies. The gap between the two principles opened in the last week of May 1967 and has not been closed.
