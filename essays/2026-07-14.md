# The Six Weeks in October and November 1517 When Martin Luther Wrote Ninety-Five Theses for an Academic Debate and an Anonymous Printer in Nuremberg Made the Reformation by Publishing Them Without His Permission

On the afternoon of Wednesday, 31 October 1517, an Augustinian friar and professor of biblical theology at the young University of Wittenberg posted a Latin document titled *Disputatio pro declaratione virtutis indulgentiarum* — ninety-five propositions on the doctrine of indulgences — on the north door of the Castle Church, which functioned as the university's notice board. He simultaneously mailed copies to Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz, who was licensing the sale of indulgences Luther objected to, and to a few academic correspondents. The document was in Latin, addressed to fellow theologians, and framed as a proposal for a scholarly disputation of the sort that occurred every few weeks in every major European university. Luther expected, at most, a heated exchange within the Faculty of Theology and a possible summons to explain himself to his religious superiors. He did not expect a movement. He did not want a movement. And he certainly did not know, when he posted the theses, that the document was already, by its nature, uncontainable — because Latin university disputations were routinely reprinted in the cities where they excited interest, and because the specific network of printers who serviced the German academic market had, over the previous thirty years, developed a business model that treated documents like Luther's as raw material for a mass audience that no theologian had ever previously imagined existed.

**The standard narrative treats the Ninety-Five Theses as the founding act of the Protestant Reformation and Luther as the intentional author of a religious revolution. Both claims are wrong. The document was an academic proposal that no one in Wittenberg read as revolutionary; the revolution was manufactured, between roughly 15 November and 25 December 1517, by an anonymous printer in Nuremberg — almost certainly Hieronymus Höltzel, though the attribution is disputed — who obtained a copy of the theses without Luther's knowledge, translated the theological Latin into a form accessible to the literate laity, and printed it in quarto pamphlet format for sale at the Frankfurt book fair.** By the time Luther learned that his theses were being read in Nuremberg, Basel, and Leipzig, they had already ceased to be his property. By the time he understood that the readers included tradesmen, minor nobles, town councillors, and parish priests rather than only theologians, the Reformation was six weeks old and the man usually credited as its author had been the last person in Germany to notice it was happening.

To see why this matters, one has to understand what a Latin university disputation actually was in early sixteenth-century Germany and what indulgences actually were. The disputation was a formal academic exercise, governed by strict conventions, in which a proponent proposed a series of numbered theses and a respondent — usually a colleague or advanced student — was invited to argue against them in public. The theses did not necessarily represent the proponent's settled views; they were provocations designed to elicit argument, and the more sharply they were worded the more effective they were as pedagogical instruments. Luther's ninety-five theses were sharp. Thesis 27 — "They preach vanity who say that as soon as the penny clinks in the coffer, the soul flies out of purgatory" — was quotable in a way that most Latin theology was not. Thesis 86, which asked why the Pope, "whose wealth today is greater than the wealth of the richest Crassus," did not build St Peter's Basilica with his own money rather than that of the poor, was politically incendiary in a way Luther would later claim he had not intended. But sharpness was the genre. Luther was doing what German university theologians did. What he was not doing, and what no one who read the theses in Latin in Wittenberg thought he was doing, was declaring war on the Roman Church.

The indulgence controversy that occasioned the theses was itself narrower than the mythology suggests. Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz, a twenty-seven-year-old Hohenzollern prince who already held two bishoprics in violation of canon law, had purchased a dispensation from Pope Leo X to hold the third — the archbishopric of Mainz, the wealthiest ecclesiastical office in Germany — for 21,000 ducats, a sum he had borrowed from the Fugger banking house of Augsburg. To repay the Fuggers, Albrecht had arranged with the Pope to sell a special indulgence throughout his territories, with half the proceeds going to the construction of St Peter's in Rome and half to the Fuggers' repayment. The Dominican preacher Johann Tetzel, appointed as the operation's principal salesman, had crossed into Saxony in the summer of 1517 with a marketing pitch — the coffer-and-penny formulation Luther would attack in thesis 27 — that Luther's parishioners had begun bringing back to Wittenberg for their confessor's opinion. Luther's objection was, at bottom, pastoral. His parishioners were purchasing pieces of paper in the belief that they were purchasing salvation, and he thought this was theologically incoherent and morally corrupting. The ninety-five theses were his attempt to force a public disputation of the doctrinal basis on which the sale rested. The intended audience was Albrecht and the theological faculty. The intended outcome was a reform of indulgence practice, not a schism.

**What broke this containment was the technology of print, and specifically the fact that the German print industry in 1517 had developed a specific commercial appetite for exactly the kind of document Luther had produced — short, controversial, in a name that meant something to educated readers — and had built a distribution network capable of moving such documents across the empire in weeks rather than months.** By 1517 there were approximately 200 print shops operating in the Holy Roman Empire, concentrated in a dozen imperial cities: Nuremberg, Augsburg, Basel, Cologne, Leipzig, Strasbourg, Wittenberg itself. Most of these shops survived on a mix of commissioned religious and legal works, but the profitable margin of the industry came from opportunistic pamphlets — three to eight pages, quarto format, printed in runs of 500 to 1,500 copies, sold at prices a skilled artisan could afford, and marketed through the Frankfurt fair to booksellers who redistributed them across the German-speaking world. The market for such pamphlets was expanding rapidly. Literacy rates in the imperial cities had reached perhaps 30 per cent among adult males; the vernacular reading public was large enough to sustain a commercial ecology in which printers, not authors, made the crucial decisions about what got distributed. And the decision-making calculus of a printer looking at a Latin document was simple: was this something a bookseller in Augsburg or Basel would pay for? Would the pamphlet sell 500 copies at three or four pfennigs each? A document with the political sharpness of Luther's theses — an obscure Saxon monk attacking the Pope's finances by name — was an unusually attractive commercial prospect.

The reconstruction of what happened next is possible because bibliographers have identified three separate first editions of the theses printed in late 1517, each in a different city, each without Luther's authorisation. The earliest — traditionally attributed to Höltzel in Nuremberg, though Wolfgang Stöckel of Leipzig has also been proposed — appeared as a broadside, a single large sheet suitable for public posting. This was followed almost immediately by two quarto pamphlet editions: one from Basel, printed by Adam Petri, and one from Leipzig. All three editions appear to derive from copies of the theses that Luther had mailed to correspondents or that had circulated in manuscript through the German university network in the two weeks after 31 October. None of them credits Luther by consultation; the earliest surviving letter from Luther expressing surprise at the reprinting is dated 11 November 1517, addressed to Albrecht himself, and contains the crucial phrase: "It was never my intention to publish them." By the time Luther wrote that sentence, at least one printed edition was already in circulation, and probably two. By the end of November, copies had reached Erfurt, Frankfurt, Mainz, and Augsburg. By 25 December — the traditional dating of the pamphlet editions' arrival at the Frankfurt fair — the theses were the most talked-about document in Germany.

What the anonymous printer of the first edition did that Luther had not done, and could not have done, was to transform the document from a scholarly proposal into a public manifesto. The transformation was not linguistic — the first printed editions were in Luther's original Latin — but structural. By printing the theses as a stand-alone pamphlet, physically separated from the university context that had originally framed them, the printer stripped them of the interpretive apparatus that would have signalled to readers "this is a proposal for academic debate, not a statement of doctrinal position." A pamphlet is not a disputation. A pamphlet is a claim. **The moment the ninety-five theses became a pamphlet, they became propositions asserted by their author against the Church, rather than provocations proposed by a theologian for academic argument, and Luther lost the ability to control what the document meant.** He would spend the next three years — through the encounters with Cardinal Cajetan at Augsburg in October 1518, with Johann Eck at Leipzig in July 1519, and finally at the Diet of Worms in April 1521 — negotiating a public position that had been forced on him by print rather than chosen by him theologically. His views hardened under the pressure. By the time the Diet of Worms convened, Luther held positions — on papal authority, on the sacraments, on the priesthood of all believers — that were considerably more radical than anything visible in the ninety-five theses. He had, in a sense, been made a Reformer by having to defend the theses in the form in which the printers had distributed them, rather than in the form in which he had written them.

**The evidence that Luther experienced this as a loss of authorship, rather than as a strategic success, is substantial.** In a letter to his mentor Johann von Staupitz in March 1518, Luther wrote that the theses had been "scattered abroad much more widely than I had wished" and complained that the printers had produced them "without my consent." In his preface to the *Explanations of the Ninety-Five Theses*, published in August 1518, he attempted to reassert authorial control by providing an official gloss on each thesis — an implicit acknowledgment that the pamphlet editions had allowed the theses to be read in ways he had not intended and could no longer prevent. In the *Table Talk* recorded by his students in the 1530s and 1540s, Luther returned repeatedly to the story of 1517, and the version he told was always one of surprise: he had not expected the theses to be read outside Wittenberg, he had not expected them to be translated into German (as they were, by an anonymous hand, in January 1518), and he had not expected them to make him famous. In the version of the story that Luther told about himself, agency belonged to the printer, not the theologian. Modern Reformation scholarship — Bernd Moeller, Mark Edwards, Andrew Pettegree — has confirmed his version of events, though the confirmation has struggled to displace the older narrative of Luther as intentional author of his own revolution because that narrative is dramaturgically more satisfying.

**The larger point is that the Reformation, considered as a communications event, was made possible by a specific configuration of technology, commerce, and legal permissiveness that had assembled itself in the German lands over the previous half-century without anyone in particular having designed it.** Printing itself had been possible since Gutenberg's press of the 1450s, but it took roughly fifty years for the industry to develop the specific business model — cheap quarto pamphlets, aggressive redistribution through fairs, tolerance for anonymous or unauthorised reprints — that made possible the mass diffusion of a single document across a linguistic community in a matter of weeks. The imperial cities lacked, and would continue to lack until the Reichstag legislation of the 1520s, an effective mechanism for censoring print in advance of publication; the loose federal structure of the Holy Roman Empire meant that a printer in one jurisdiction could produce material forbidden in another, and that authorial control over printed material was legally and practically almost non-existent. The Latin scholarly world had, at the same time, expanded beyond the medieval university system into a wider republic of letters whose members corresponded across confessional and political boundaries and shared documents freely. Luther's theses landed in this environment as a spark in a room full of gunpowder that no one had known was there — including Luther. Had he written the same document twenty years earlier, before the pamphlet industry had matured, it would have remained a Saxon academic controversy. Had he written it twenty years later, after the Catholic authorities had developed the Index and the Protestant princes had developed their own censorship regimes, it might have been suppressed before Frankfurt. He wrote it in the ten-year window when the German print market had matured beyond the ability of any authority to control it and before any authority had understood that this was the case.

There is a lesson in this that historians of technology understand better than historians of ideas: revolutionary events are made by the intersection of a message with a medium that is prepared to carry it, and the medium is often more decisive than the message. Luther's theology was not new — much of it was inherited from Wycliffe, Hus, and the German mystical tradition, and none of it was as theologically inventive as the parallel work being done at the same moment by Erasmus in Basel. What was new in 1517 was the printer. **The Reformation was not the achievement of a heroic individual conscience against the corruption of the Roman Church; it was the first mass-media event in European history, and the man usually named as its author was, in the crucial six weeks between 31 October and mid-December 1517, its passenger rather than its driver.** The anonymous printer of the Nuremberg broadside — a person whose name we may never know with certainty, but who was pursuing no theological agenda beyond the desire to sell pamphlets at the Frankfurt fair — did more to make the Reformation happen than Luther did in the same six weeks. Luther supplied the raw material; the printer supplied the event.

The subsequent history of European Christianity, and by extension of European politics, of the wars of religion, of the settlement of Westphalia, of the confessional divisions that would shape the continent until 1945 and in some ways still do, depended on this obscure commercial decision by a Nuremberg tradesman in the second week of November 1517. He calculated that a Latin pamphlet by a Saxon monk attacking the Pope's financial arrangements would sell 500 copies. It sold considerably more than 500 copies. The margin between the two figures is the space in which the modern world was made.
