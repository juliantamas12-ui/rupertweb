# The Three Hours on 16 July 1979 in Baghdad When Saddam Hussein Read 66 Names from a List in the Khuld Hall and Made the Ba'ath Party Personal Property

On the morning of Wednesday, 18 July 1979, in the Khuld Conference Hall of the Republican Palace in Baghdad, Saddam Hussein convened an extraordinary session of the Iraqi Regional Command of the Ba'ath Party. He had become President of the Republic six days earlier, on 16 July, following the resignation of his cousin and patron Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr, which had been announced on state television the previous evening as a decision taken on grounds of ill health. Al-Bakr was not ill. He had been forced to resign at gunpoint in the early hours of 16 July by Saddam personally, in a meeting at the presidential residence at which the terms of the transfer — full powers, no successor council, no consultative role for al-Bakr — had been dictated and the resignation letter signed in al-Bakr's own hand for the cameras of the following evening. The Khuld Hall session of 18 July, which was filmed by the state broadcaster Iraqi Television and which has survived in a sixty-eight-minute recording that circulated on VHS through the 1980s and on YouTube since 2006, was the second act. It was the act in which Saddam established that the Iraqi state, the Ba'ath Party, and the lives of the men assembled in the room were now his personal property, to be disposed of at his discretion, on the basis of a list he held in his hand. **The standard account treats the Khuld Hall session as a purge of rivals who had plotted against him. The truth is that the plot did not exist. Saddam invented it, in collaboration with his security chief Barzan al-Tikriti and his cousin Adnan Khairallah, in the seventy-two hours between 14 and 17 July, for the specific purpose of staging a public ritual in which the entire Ba'ath leadership would be made complicit in the murders of its own members, and would therefore be bound to him by the shared knowledge that they had voted, on camera, for the executions of men they knew to be innocent.**

The mechanics of the session matter because they were choreographed with a precision that has been obscured by the sheer theatrical horror of the recording. The Regional Command convened at approximately 10:00. There were sixty-eight men in the hall, seated in rows of folding chairs facing the dais. Saddam entered at 10:15, in a white suit, smoking a Cohiba cigar. He spoke for forty minutes. He announced that a conspiracy against the party had been uncovered, that the conspirators had confessed, and that the names would now be read. He then sat down and lit a second cigar. Muhyi Abdul Hussein Mashhadi, the Secretary-General of the Revolutionary Command Council, was brought to the podium. Mashhadi had been arrested five days earlier and tortured at the Hakimiya prison in central Baghdad by Barzan al-Tikriti's interrogators. He read a confession that named sixty-six members of the assembled Regional Command as participants in a Syrian-backed plot to overthrow the government. As each name was read, the man named was escorted from the hall by security officers. The remaining members of the Command applauded each removal. Saddam smoked. The session lasted three hours. By 13:30, sixty-six men had been removed. Twenty-two of them were executed within seven days, by firing squads composed of the surviving members of the Regional Command, who were required to participate personally in the killings of their former colleagues. The remaining forty-four were imprisoned or exiled. **The forty-two surviving members of the Command who had not been named on the list were not the lucky ones. They were the targets. The session had been designed to bind them, by the act of pulling triggers at executions they knew to be staged, to the regime that Saddam had now established. The murders were not the purpose. The murders were the instrument by which the survivors were converted into accomplices.**

The cruelty of the design has been described by Saddam's biographers — Said Aburish, Con Coughlin, Efraim Karsh — as evidence of his pathological character. The cruelty was, in fact, a political instrument of considerable sophistication. The Iraqi Ba'ath Party in July 1979 was not a vehicle of personal rule. It was a collective leadership of approximately one hundred senior cadres, most of whom had joined the party in the 1950s, who had participated in the 1968 coup that brought al-Bakr to power, and who regarded Saddam — eleven years younger than al-Bakr, formally only Vice-President — as a powerful but constrained figure within a system of institutional checks. The Regional Command, the Revolutionary Command Council, and the cabinet were overlapping bodies whose membership was renewed by internal party elections and whose decisions required collective approval. Saddam had spent the decade from 1969 to 1979 systematically colonising the security apparatus, the party organisation, and the economic ministries with relatives from the al-Bu Nasir clan of Tikrit and with personal clients. By July 1979 he controlled the means of coercion. He did not yet control the legitimacy of the institutions. The Khuld Hall session was the conversion of the first into the second. **He could have killed his enemies in private. He chose to kill them in public, with the participation of the men he was not killing, because the political problem he was solving was not the elimination of rivals but the destruction of the principle that the Ba'ath Party was a collective body whose members owed loyalty to each other and to the institution rather than to him.**

The instrument worked. Within eighteen months, the Ba'ath Party had ceased to function as a political organisation in the sense it had retained until 1979. The Regional Command continued to meet, but its decisions were dictated by Saddam in advance and ratified by acclamation. The Revolutionary Command Council was reduced to a rubber stamp. The cabinet became an administrative apparatus whose ministers served at Saddam's pleasure and whose dismissals — frequent through the 1980s — were announced without explanation. The internal elections that had renewed the party's senior cadres through the 1970s were suspended. The party congresses, which had met every four years, were not convened between 1982 and 2001. **The Ba'ath Party as a collective body died in the Khuld Hall on 18 July 1979. What survived under the name was a personal patronage network with a flag.**

The deeper question is what made the design possible. The Iraqi state inherited by the Ba'ath in 1968 had been built, since the British mandate of 1920, on a series of institutional checks — parliamentary, military, tribal, clerical — that had prevented any single faction from monopolising power for more than a few years at a time. The five governments of the 1920s, the four military coups of the 1930s and 1940s, the Hashemite monarchy, the Qasim regime of 1958–63, the Arif brothers of 1963–68 had each been constrained by countervailing centres of authority. The Ba'ath in 1968 had begun the process of dismantling those checks. The Khuld Hall session completed it. It completed it because Saddam had understood, by July 1979, what his predecessors had not understood: that the instruments of state coercion in modern Iraq — the Mukhabarat security service, the Special Republican Guard, the Amn al-Amm internal security — were sufficiently developed to permit the elimination of any institutional rival, provided the elimination was carried out fast enough that no counter-mobilisation could be organised. **The window for institutional resistance in post-1968 Iraq was approximately seventy-two hours. Saddam used it. His predecessors had not.**

There is a final dimension to the session that has been systematically misread by Western historians and correctly understood by the Iraqis who survived it. The forty-two men in the hall who applauded the removal of their colleagues, who voted for the executions, who participated personally in the firing squads of the following week, were not cowards. They were, with very few exceptions, intelligent men who had spent their adult lives in clandestine political organisations and who understood what was being demanded of them. They participated because they calculated, correctly, that participation was the price of survival. **What they did not calculate, and what Saddam had calculated, was that the participation would bind them to him not for the duration of the immediate crisis but for the duration of his life. The men who pulled triggers in late July 1979 could not, in 1980 or 1985 or 1990, denounce a regime they had murdered to defend. The complicity was the cage. The cage held for twenty-four years.** The regime that emerged from the Khuld Hall lasted until the morning of 9 April 2003, when the statue of Saddam in Firdos Square was pulled down by United States Marines. The forty-two surviving applauders of 18 July 1979 had, by then, either died of natural causes, fled into exile, or — in fourteen documented cases — been executed by Saddam himself in subsequent purges between 1982 and 1991. The Ba'ath Party that had been collectively murdered in the Khuld Hall did not survive the regime it had been forced to construct. **Saddam Hussein had spent three hours on a Wednesday morning in July 1979 converting a political party into a personal possession. The conversion held until he was dragged from a spider hole near Tikrit on 13 December 2003. The men he had bound to him in the Khuld Hall had, by then, ceased to exist as a political force. They had been consumed by the instrument they had agreed, on camera, to wield.**
