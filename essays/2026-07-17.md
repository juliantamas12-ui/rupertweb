# The Night of 28 February 1986 on Sveavägen When Olof Palme Was Shot Walking Home From the Cinema and Swedish Social Democracy Lost the Confidence in Permanent Power That It Would Never Recover

At 23:21 on the evening of Friday, 28 February 1986, on the corner of Sveavägen and Tunnelgatan in central Stockholm, a man in a dark overcoat drew a .357 Magnum revolver, fired two shots at point-blank range into the back of Prime Minister Olof Palme — who was walking home from the Grand Cinema on Sveavägen with his wife Lisbet after seeing *Bröderna Mozart*, without security, at his own insistence — and disappeared up the concrete steps of Tunnelgatan into the winter dark. Palme was dead on arrival at Sabbatsberg hospital forty minutes later. Lisbet, grazed by the second bullet, survived. The killer has never been convicted. The murder weapon has never been recovered. The Swedish state, thirty-nine years and one closed investigation later, has produced a single official theory — that the assassin was Stig Engström, a graphic designer at the insurance firm Skandia who worked in the building across the street from the shooting and who died in 2000 — but has never proven it in court, and the file remains one of the two or three great unsolved political murders of the twentieth century, alongside the Kennedy assassination and the disappearance of Raoul Wallenberg in Budapest in 1945.

**The historical significance of the Palme assassination is not its unsolved character but the specific effect it had on the country in which it took place.** Sweden in February 1986 was the most confident social democracy on earth. The Swedish Social Democratic Workers' Party — the SAP — had governed the country for forty-four of the previous fifty-four years. It had constructed the most comprehensive welfare state in the industrialised world, negotiated the wage-setting system that produced two decades of nearly full employment, positioned Sweden as the leading neutral voice in the Cold War, and made Stockholm the diplomatic capital of the non-aligned movement. Palme personally had made the country a global voice — condemning American bombing in Vietnam from the streets of Stockholm in 1968, chairing the Independent Commission on Disarmament and Security Issues, brokering back-channel negotiations between Iran and Iraq in the war then in its sixth year. He had enemies. He had many enemies. The CIA had a file on him. The South African security service BOSS had a file on him. The Iranian regime had a file on him. The Iraqi regime had a file on him. Swedish right-wing paramilitary groups had files on him. Yugoslav Ustaša émigrés had files on him. Kurdish PKK operatives had files on him. The murder investigation — which spent thirty-four years and cost the equivalent of 800 million kronor — would examine each of these files in turn, and find in each of them motive, means, and, in several cases, opportunity, and would prove none of them.

The immediate operational failure was Swedish. Palme had dismissed his security detail that evening because it was Friday and he wanted to walk home with his wife. The Stockholm police, notified of the shooting at 23:23, deployed to the wrong intersection — the emergency dispatcher had misheard "Sveavägen" as "Sveavägen 44" rather than "Sveavägen and Tunnelgatan," and the first patrol arrived seven minutes late. The initial crime scene was contaminated by paramedics and onlookers within twenty minutes. The two shell casings recovered from the pavement — which forensic analysis established had come from a Smith & Wesson .357 Magnum revolver, model 66 or 686, loaded with metal-jacketed Winchester Western ammunition — were the only physical evidence of the killer's presence. The murder weapon was never found. No fingerprints were recovered. The witnesses, of whom there were more than twenty within a hundred metres of the scene, produced descriptions of the assassin that ranged from a slim man of 175 centimetres to a heavyset man of 190 centimetres, wearing a coat that was variously described as blue, black, brown, or grey. **The investigation began, in other words, at a disadvantage from which no investigation ever fully recovers.**

The first suspect was Christer Pettersson — a small-time criminal, alcoholic, and former psychiatric patient with a conviction for a 1970 knife murder — who was identified by Lisbet Palme in a police line-up thirty months after the shooting. Pettersson was convicted of the murder in July 1989, sentenced to life imprisonment, and acquitted on appeal three months later on the grounds that the line-up had been contaminated: Lisbet had been shown a police photograph of Pettersson before the identification and Pettersson had been the only man in the line-up displaying obvious signs of intoxication, which had been the primary characteristic Lisbet had used to identify her husband's killer. The acquittal — which the prosecutor Anders Helin appealed unsuccessfully to the Supreme Court in 1998 — collapsed the investigation's most substantial theory. Pettersson died in 2004 of a brain haemorrhage after falling down stairs while drunk. Whether he was the killer is one of the questions the Swedish state has never resolved. **The prosecutorial verdict of June 2020, delivered by Krister Petersson (no relation), was that Stig Engström had been the assassin — but Engström had died in 2000, could not be charged, and the case was closed without a trial.** The Palme family rejected the finding. Swedish public opinion rejected the finding. Independent researchers rejected the finding. The finding closed the case without resolving it.

The point about the failed investigation is not that it failed. The point is what its failure did to Sweden. **The Palme assassination broke the Swedish state's confidence in its own competence at a moment when that confidence was the operating principle of Swedish politics.** For forty years, the SAP had governed on the premise that the Swedish state could do difficult things — construct a welfare system, negotiate wage agreements, mediate international disputes, project moral authority — because the state was uniquely well-run. The state's inability to solve the murder of its own prime minister punctured that premise. If the Stockholm police could not find the man who had shot the prime minister on a Stockholm street corner in front of twenty witnesses, what else could they not do? The question, once asked, could not be un-asked, and it began to be asked about every other Swedish institution in the years that followed.

The economic crisis of 1991-93 finished the job that the Palme assassination had begun. The Swedish housing market collapsed in the autumn of 1990 following the deregulation of the credit market in 1985. Swedish banks — Nordbanken and Gota Bank in particular — accumulated losses that required a state rescue in September 1992 of approximately 65 billion kronor, roughly 4 per cent of GDP. The krona, defended by the Riksbank at an overnight interest rate that reached 500 per cent on 16 September 1992, was floated on 19 November 1992 and immediately devalued by 20 per cent. Unemployment, which had averaged 2.1 per cent through the 1980s, reached 12.1 per cent in 1993. The SAP, which had governed for forty-four of the previous fifty-four years, lost the September 1991 election to a four-party centre-right coalition under Carl Bildt, and although it returned to power in 1994 under Ingvar Carlsson, the terms of Swedish political competition had changed. Sweden joined the European Union in January 1995. It privatised state assets. It reduced the maximum marginal income tax rate from 87 per cent to 57 per cent between 1990 and 1995. **The Swedish model, in the specific form it had assumed under Palme, ceased to exist between February 1986 and January 1995.**

Whether the assassination caused this trajectory or merely coincided with it is a question historians will continue to argue. What is clear is that Palme's personal role in the SAP's ability to project confidence had been indispensable. He was the last Swedish prime minister who genuinely believed that Sweden could serve as a global moral referee, and the last who genuinely believed that the Swedish welfare state could indefinitely combine full employment with rising real wages and expanding social provision. His successor Ingvar Carlsson was a competent administrator without any comparable ideological ambition. The SAP under Carlsson, and then under Göran Persson from 1996 to 2006, managed the Swedish state's transition into a different kind of country — smaller state share of GDP, EU membership, tighter fiscal rules, an inflation-targeting central bank — without ever articulating the transition as an ideological project. The Swedish social democracy that had governed the country from 1932 to 1976 and from 1982 to 1991 continued to exist as an electoral coalition after 1994 but ceased to exist as a governing philosophy. The Sweden Democrats — the far-right nationalist party that has now become the second-largest force in the Riksdag — emerged from the political space that the SAP vacated. Palme's Sweden ended on Sveavägen, and the Sweden that replaced it has, in its confused and defensive way, been searching for a replacement philosophy ever since.

**There is one further point worth registering.** The investigation into Palme's murder generated a category of Swedish political psychology that did not previously exist: the conspiratorial imagination. Sweden had been, before 1986, a country that trusted its institutions to an unusual degree — a country in which citizens presumed, absent evidence to the contrary, that the state was competent and honest. The Palme investigation destroyed that presumption. Every Swedish political controversy since 1986 — the sinking of the ferry *Estonia* in 1994, the murder of foreign minister Anna Lindh in September 2003, the botched response to the 2004 Boxing Day tsunami that killed 543 Swedish citizens in Thailand — has been shadowed by a suspicion that the state is hiding something. The suspicion is usually wrong. But it is a Swedish habit now, and it did not exist before 28 February 1986.

The historical geometry is this. Political assassinations in stable democracies have effects out of proportion to the individuals killed. **The murders that matter are the murders that puncture a governing assumption.** Kennedy's murder in 1963 did not change the Cold War, but it changed American citizens' relationship to their own government in ways that were still being worked out thirty years later. Palme's murder in 1986 did not change the trajectory of the Swedish welfare state — that trajectory was already turning — but it accelerated the change and gave it a specific psychological character. Sweden became, over the following decade, a country that had lost the ability to imagine itself as unusually good at government. The Sveavägen and Tunnelgatan intersection is now marked by a small brass plaque set into the pavement. Tourists photograph it. Swedes walk past it. The concrete steps up which the killer disappeared are unchanged. The city has decided, by the accumulated weight of small municipal decisions, to remember the murder without solving it. That is what the closure of an assassination file looks like when the country in which the assassination took place has decided, without ever saying so, that the identity of the killer matters less than the fact that the killing was possible.
