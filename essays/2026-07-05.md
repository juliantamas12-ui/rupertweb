# The Six Hours on 25 April 1974 in Lisbon When a Song on Rádio Renascença Triggered a Military Coup That No One in the Caetano Government Had Detected and Ended the Portuguese Empire in a Single Night by Broadcast Signal

At 00:20 on Thursday, 25 April 1974, the Catholic radio station Rádio Renascença, broadcasting from studios on the Rua Ivens in central Lisbon, played a song by the folk singer José Afonso titled *Grândola, Vila Morena*. The song had been banned by the Portuguese state since its release in 1971 for its explicit celebration of popular fraternity in the Alentejo village of Grândola, and its inclusion in the station's playlist had been arranged three days earlier through the personal intervention of the assistant programme director, Manuel Tomás, who had received the instruction from a Captain of Cavalry named Vasco Lourenço via an intermediary in the Communist-affiliated Metalworkers' Union. The song was the second of two coded signals. The first, broadcast at 22:55 the previous evening on Rádio Emissores Associados, had been the sentimental ballad *E Depois do Adeus* by Paulo de Carvalho, which had represented Portugal at the Eurovision Song Contest three weeks earlier. Between the two signals, four hundred officers of the Movimento das Forças Armadas, none of whom held a rank above Major, had moved units from garrisons at Santarém, Mafra, Vendas Novas, Tancos and Beja along five converging axes toward the Portuguese capital. By 06:00, they had captured the Ministry of the Interior, the Salazar Bridge, the airport, the two television stations, and the barracks of the Republican National Guard on the Largo do Carmo, into which the Prime Minister Marcelo Caetano had retreated at 04:20 in the belief that its walls would protect him. By 18:00, Caetano had surrendered to Brigadier General António de Spínola on condition that power be transferred to a general rather than to a captain, and the Estado Novo — the dictatorship that Antonio Salazar had built in 1933 and that had governed Portugal without interruption for forty-one years — had ceased to exist. The Portuguese African empire, comprising Angola, Mozambique, Guinea-Bissau, Cape Verde, São Tomé and Príncipe, and East Timor, would be liquidated in the eighteen months that followed. Approximately one million Portuguese settlers would be repatriated. The last European colonial empire on the African continent, which had been established in 1482 with the construction of the fortress at Elmina on the Gold Coast, was terminated by a folk song played on a Catholic radio station at twenty minutes past midnight.

**The standard account of the Carnation Revolution, repeated in every survey of twentieth-century Portugal from A. H. de Oliveira Marques to Kenneth Maxwell, treats the coup as the outcome of a long ideological maturation within the Portuguese officer corps, provoked by the unwinnable colonial wars in Angola, Mozambique and Guinea, and culminating in a broadly popular uprising that mobilised the Lisbon population against a decaying regime. The truth is more precise and considerably more contingent. The MFA had approximately six hundred active conspirators out of an officer corps of approximately nine thousand. It had no support in the Navy High Command, no support in the Air Force General Staff, no support in the GNR (the paramilitary Republican National Guard commanded by General Alfredo Reis), and no confirmed support in the Lisbon garrison itself. It had four hundred junior officers, five hundred conscripts who had been informed of the operation less than six hours before it commenced, and two radio signals. The Caetano government possessed the DGS secret police, the Legião Portuguesa militia, three infantry regiments in the capital, an armoured squadron in Ajuda, a naval frigate on the Tagus, and a functioning telecommunications system. The government did not use any of these assets, because it did not know until approximately 04:30 that a coup was in progress. The MFA had achieved operational surprise not through superior intelligence discipline, but because the Caetano regime's own intelligence services had concluded, in a report dated 22 April and initialled by DGS Director Silva Pais, that no coup was possible before the annual promotion cycle of September 1974. The report was correct about the political and military balance and wrong about the timing, because the MFA had understood something that the DGS had not: that the operation did not require political and military advantage. It required only that the government be unable to reach a decision within six hours.**

The mechanics of the operation, reconstructed from the memoirs of Otelo Saraiva de Carvalho — the thirty-seven-year-old Major who wrote the operational plan — and from the interrogation transcripts of DGS officers preserved in the Torre do Tombo national archive, reveal a level of amateurism that in any other configuration would have produced a catastrophic failure. Otelo's plan, drafted in three weeks in a rented apartment on the Alameda Afonso Henriques and coded on a single sheet of graph paper that he carried folded in his uniform pocket, allocated units on the basis of officers he personally trusted rather than on the basis of tactical necessity. The Escola Prática de Cavalaria at Santarém, commanded by Captain Salgueiro Maia, was assigned the capture of central Lisbon because Otelo had studied with Maia at the Academia Militar, not because cavalry was the correct instrument for urban assault. The regiment at Beja, three hundred kilometres from the capital, was included in the operation because its commanding officer had been Otelo's brother-in-law, notwithstanding that the unit could not reach Lisbon in time to participate in any decisive action. The Air Force units at Sintra and Tancos, whose F-86 Sabre jets could have destroyed the Santarém column on the road, were not neutralised in advance but were simply expected not to intervene, on the basis that no one had told them to. The naval frigate NRP *Almirante Gago Coutinho*, moored in the Tagus with a full complement of 4-inch guns, was similarly unassigned. Otelo's plan against the Navy consisted of the single sentence: *"Confia-se em que a Marinha não intervirá"* — "It is trusted that the Navy will not intervene." **The Navy did not intervene. It did not intervene because the Chief of the Naval Staff, Admiral Pinheiro de Azevedo, was on holiday in the Algarve, could not be reached by telephone between 01:00 and 07:00 because the MFA had cut the trunk cables between Lisbon and Faro at 00:45, and had left standing orders that the fleet was not to move without his personal authorisation. The Portuguese Navy did not defend the Portuguese state on the morning of 25 April 1974 because its commander was asleep in a hotel room in Praia da Rocha and because his subordinates followed his procedures rather than their instincts. A regime that could be destroyed by an admiral's holiday was a regime that had already destroyed itself.**

The Caetano government's failure to respond within the six-hour window between the *Grândola* broadcast and the arrival of Maia's column at the Terreiro do Paço was not a failure of information. The DGS duty officer in Lisbon received his first credible report of unusual military movement at 01:12, from a police captain at the Vila Franca de Xira crossing on the Tagus who had observed twelve armoured personnel carriers heading south. The report was logged, transmitted to DGS headquarters at the Rua António Maria Cardoso, and there placed by the duty officer, Inspector José Serra Bandeira, into the overnight tray for morning consideration. Serra Bandeira did not wake Silva Pais because the standing instructions of the DGS required exceptional intelligence to be verified through two independent sources before the director was disturbed at night, and no second source had yet reported. The second source arrived at 02:47, when a member of the Legião Portuguesa telephoned to report that units of the Escola Prática de Cavalaria had crossed the 25 de Abril Bridge — then still named the Salazar Bridge — heading north into Lisbon. Silva Pais was woken at 03:05. He telephoned Caetano at 03:20. Caetano telephoned the Minister of the Interior, César Moreira Baptista, at 03:35. Moreira Baptista telephoned the commander of the GNR, General Reis, at 03:50. Reis attempted to telephone the commander of the Lisbon military region, General Kaúlza de Arriaga, at 04:05, and discovered that the line was dead — the MFA having cut the government telephone exchange at the Palácio Foz at 03:45. **The Portuguese state possessed the information required to defeat the coup by 01:12 on the morning of 25 April 1974. It had the information for two hours and thirty-three minutes before its own director of secret police was informed, and for a further hour and forty-five minutes before its senior military commander could be reached. By the time General Kaúlza de Arriaga was located, at approximately 05:30, at the residence of a mistress in Estoril, Maia's column had already reached the Praça do Comércio and taken up firing positions facing the Ministries. The Portuguese state fell not because it lacked the means to defend itself but because its decision cycle was longer than the operation designed to destroy it. The MFA had won by six hours of paperwork.**

The moment at which the operation ceased to be reversible occurred not at the Largo do Carmo, where Caetano's surrender at 18:00 was already a formality, but at 11:30 in the morning at the entrance to the Ministry of the Army on the Terreiro do Paço, when Captain Salgueiro Maia, at the head of a column of thirteen Chaimite armoured cars from Santarém, was ordered by the loyalist commander of the 7th Cavalry Regiment, Brigadier Junqueira dos Reis, to surrender or be fired upon. Junqueira dos Reis had positioned four Panhard AML-60 armoured cars in front of the Arco da Rua Augusta with orders from Caetano personally, transmitted at 09:40, to prevent any rebel column from entering the square. Maia halted his column at approximately fifty metres and dismounted, walking forward alone with his pistol holstered. The two officers spoke for four minutes. Junqueira dos Reis, according to Maia's memoir published in 1977, said: *"If you advance, I will give the order to fire."* Maia replied: *"Then give the order."* Junqueira dos Reis did not give the order, because he understood — as Maia had correctly calculated — that the crews of the Panhards, who were conscripts of exactly the same age and background as the men in Maia's Chaimites, would refuse to fire on a fellow Portuguese unit if the order were issued in front of them rather than by radio from a distance. **Junqueira dos Reis returned to his command vehicle, radioed Caetano at the Largo do Carmo that the position could not be held, and withdrew. The last defensive line of the Portuguese dictatorship dissolved because a brigadier declined to test the loyalty of his conscripts in the presence of the officers who had grown up with them. The Estado Novo did not fall because its soldiers rebelled. It fell because its officers understood that its soldiers would not obey them, and preferred to withdraw quietly rather than confirm the fact publicly.**

The counterfactual question is whether a more resolute government could have suppressed the operation. The comparative evidence — the failed MFA operation of 16 March 1974, when a smaller conspiracy of officers at Caldas da Rainha marched on Lisbon and was disarmed within nine hours by loyalist units alerted by DGS informants — suggests that the answer is yes, but only under conditions that no longer applied in April. The 16 March operation had failed because Caetano had received warning eleven hours in advance and had reinforced the Lisbon garrison. The 25 April operation succeeded because the DGS had concluded, on the basis of the 16 March failure, that a second attempt was unlikely within the same year. Silva Pais's 22 April report, cited above, referred explicitly to the 16 March "lesson" as evidence that the MFA had been broken. **The Portuguese state was destroyed by the DGS's confidence in its own prior success. This is the standard pattern of intelligence failure: not that the warning signs are absent, but that they are interpreted through a template established by the last event rather than the current one. Silva Pais lost Portugal on 22 April, three days before the coup, by writing a report that told his government it had already won.**

The eighteen months that followed — the *Processo Revolucionário em Curso* — were the most turbulent in modern Portuguese history and produced consequences the MFA had not anticipated. The Communist Party, which had played no role in the coup, moved within seventy-two hours to seize control of the trade unions, the Ministry of Labour, and the state broadcaster. The Socialist Party under Mário Soares, returning from exile in Paris on 28 April, spent the following year contesting Communist infiltration of the provisional government. The colonial empire, which the MFA had promised to negotiate rather than abandon, was liquidated in a sequence of chaotic withdrawals that transferred power in Angola to a civil war between three armed movements, in Mozambique to FRELIMO alone, in Guinea-Bissau to the PAIGC, and in East Timor to a nine-day sovereignty that ended in Indonesian invasion. The one million *retornados* who arrived in Portugal between June 1974 and December 1975 constituted a population increase of eleven per cent in eighteen months, absorbed without civil disorder in an economic feat that remains one of the least studied achievements of twentieth-century European migration policy. **None of this had been planned. Otelo's graph-paper document contained no provision for the future of the colonies, no economic programme, no constitutional framework, and no arrangement with the political parties that would compete for power in the vacuum. The MFA had solved for the six hours between 00:20 and 06:20 on 25 April 1974. It had not solved for anything that came afterwards, because it had never seriously believed it would succeed.**

The deeper implication for the political history of the twentieth century is that authoritarian regimes fail not when they are attacked by superior force but when they cease to be able to distinguish real threats from routine noise. The Estado Novo in 1974 possessed every instrument that a successful state requires — an army, a secret police, a functioning bureaucracy, a compliant judiciary, a controlled press — and lost power to four hundred junior officers who could not have taken any of these instruments in a direct confrontation. It lost because its instruments had ceased to communicate with each other, because its senior officials were geographically dispersed and procedurally cautious, and because it had internalised the belief that the previous failure of its enemies constituted evidence of its own permanence. The song on Rádio Renascença at 00:20 on 25 April was not a coded signal in any meaningful sense — the Caetano government, had it been monitoring the broadcast, could have identified the anomaly of a banned song on a Catholic radio station within thirty seconds and dispatched police to the Rua Ivens within ten minutes. It did not monitor the broadcast, because monitoring Catholic radio at midnight had not been part of the DGS operational routine since 1969, when a previous MFA precursor had briefly used the same station and had been prosecuted. **A regime that stops watching the channels through which it has previously been attacked, on the assumption that its enemies will not use the same channel twice, is a regime that has already begun to lose. The Portuguese dictatorship fell to the same signal it had already suppressed once, because it had confused the suppression of the signal with the suppression of the enemy that used it. This is the specific pathology of long-serving authoritarian regimes: the confusion of past victories with present security. The MFA won on 25 April 1974 because it played the same trick twice, and the DGS, having learned to defeat the trick the first time, had forgotten that a trick is only defeated once by the same person, never by the same institution.**
