History 24 July 2026

The Six Days in November 1956 When Dwight Eisenhower Crashed the Pound Sterling to Force Anthony Eden's Withdrawal from Suez, and the British Empire Discovered That Its Reserve Currency Was an American Instrument

At 09:15 on the morning of Tuesday, 6 November 1956, Harold Macmillan, Chancellor of the Exchequer of the United Kingdom and, by the private admission of every senior civil servant in the Treasury, the man who would within nine weeks succeed Anthony Eden as Prime Minister, walked into the Cabinet Room at 10 Downing Street with a single piece of paper. The paper contained the previous week's outflows from the Exchange Equalisation Account, the reserve fund with which the Bank of England defended the sterling exchange rate at its Bretton Woods parity of $2.80 to the pound. Between the morning of Monday, 29 October — the day on which Israeli paratroopers had dropped into the Mitla Pass at the beginning of the Anglo-French-Israeli operation to seize the Suez Canal — and the close of business on Monday, 5 November, the United Kingdom had lost $279 million of its gold and dollar reserves. This was, in the currency of the period, approximately fifteen per cent of the entire reserve. At the rate of loss recorded on 5 November alone — $84 million in a single trading day — sterling would breach the lower limit of its Bretton Woods band, requiring formal devaluation, within a further eight working days. Macmillan, who twenty-four hours earlier had assured the Cabinet that the operation could be sustained, informed Eden that the operation could not be sustained. At 17:00 that afternoon, Eden telephoned the French Prime Minister Guy Mollet and told him that British forces would cease fire at midnight. Israel had taken Sinai. France had taken nothing. Britain had taken the northern end of the Suez Canal and lost the pound.

The conventional account of the Suez Crisis attributes Eden's collapse to the United Nations resolution of 2 November, to Soviet threats of rocket attacks on London and Paris transmitted through the Bulganin note of 5 November, and to the moral pressure of the Eisenhower administration's public repudiation of the operation. All of these mattered at the margin. None of them was decisive. The operation was terminated because the Assistant Secretary of the United States Treasury for International Affairs, George Humphrey — acting on the direct verbal authority of President Eisenhower, transmitted through a telephone call from the White House to the Treasury Building at 14:30 on 30 October — instructed the Federal Reserve Bank of New York to refuse to intervene in support of sterling on the New York foreign exchange market, and further instructed the American executive director of the International Monetary Fund to block the United Kingdom's application for a stand-by drawing of $561 million against its IMF quota. These were the two levers by which sterling, in the Bretton Woods system, was defended against speculative attack. Eisenhower removed both. The pound fell. Eden folded.

The mechanism deserves reconstruction, because it is the mechanism by which reserve currencies discipline their subordinates, and because it is the mechanism that the United States has used, in various forms, in every subsequent crisis in which it has needed to instruct an ally to stop doing something. Under the Bretton Woods system as it operated in 1956, sterling was convertible into dollars at a fixed rate within a band of one per cent above and below the parity. The Bank of England defended the parity by buying sterling with dollars when the market price fell towards the lower limit, and by selling sterling for dollars when it rose towards the upper. The Bank's capacity to buy sterling was limited by the size of the Exchange Equalisation Account, which was in turn limited by the size of the United Kingdom's gold and dollar reserves. When those reserves were exhausted, the Bank had three options: to devalue sterling formally, to impose exchange controls that would have terminated London's status as an international financial centre, or to borrow dollars from the IMF or from the Federal Reserve. In November 1956, Eisenhower closed the second and third options simultaneously, leaving Macmillan with only the first — which, for a Chancellor and a Prime Minister who had staked their reputations on the defence of sterling, was politically indistinguishable from resignation.

The intervention was not spontaneous. The Eisenhower administration had known of the Anglo-French-Israeli plot since 15 October, when signals intelligence collected by the National Security Agency's precursor organisation had identified the movement of Israeli reservists inconsistent with a purely defensive posture, and when U-2 photography of the French military airfield at Djibouti had established the presence of French Mystère IV aircraft repainted in Israeli markings. Eisenhower's response, decided at a meeting of the National Security Council on 21 October, was to permit the operation to begin and then to destroy it economically at the moment of maximum British political commitment. The logic, articulated by Eisenhower in a memorandum to Secretary of State John Foster Dulles on 22 October, was that a British-French military success in Egypt would have handed Gamal Abdel Nasser to the Soviet Union as a permanent client, would have inflamed Arab nationalism against the United States by association, and would have re-established British and French influence in the Middle East at the expense of American oil interests in Saudi Arabia and Iran. The economic sanction was preferable to the diplomatic sanction because it was reversible, plausibly deniable, and calibrated to punish the British Treasury without publicly humiliating the British Crown. The Bulganin note of 5 November was, in Eisenhower's private assessment, a Soviet attempt to claim credit for a decision that had already been taken in Washington a week earlier.

The consequences were absorbed by the British political system over the following decade in a series of adjustments that were separately rationalised but were, in aggregate, the liquidation of Britain's independent great-power status. The pound was formally devalued in November 1967, by a Labour government whose Chancellor, James Callaghan, had inherited the sterling problem from Macmillan without being told the full history of its origins. The withdrawal from East of Suez, announced in January 1968, terminated the British military presence in the Persian Gulf and Singapore that had been the last physical infrastructure of the empire. The application for membership of the European Economic Community, submitted by Macmillan himself in July 1961 and vetoed by de Gaulle in January 1963 on the grounds — accurate but insulting — that Britain was now an American dependency and would function as an American Trojan horse within the Community, was the diplomatic acknowledgement that the sterling area had ceased to be a viable framework for British economic policy. The 1956 crisis did not cause any of these outcomes in a simple sense. It established the constraint within which they became inevitable.

The instructive feature of the episode is that Eisenhower did not need to persuade the British Cabinet of anything. He did not need to threaten military action. He did not need to convene the United Nations Security Council, though he did. He did not need to secure the cooperation of any American ally, though he received it from Canada and from West Germany. He needed only to instruct one American official to make one telephone call to the manager of the foreign exchange desk at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and to instruct one further American official to abstain in one vote at the Executive Board of the IMF. The entire structural power of the American position was contained in those two acts. The Bretton Woods architecture, which had been negotiated at the Mount Washington Hotel twelve years earlier as a technical monetary framework, turned out on operational examination to be an instrument by which the United States could veto the foreign policy of any ally that ran a current account deficit — which is to say every ally except the Federal Republic of Germany.

Macmillan understood this. He returned to the Treasury on the morning of 7 November, having authorised the ceasefire the previous evening, and instructed the Permanent Secretary, Roger Makins, to prepare a paper on the reconstitution of the sterling area on a basis that would reduce British dependence on American reserve support. The paper, delivered on 3 December, concluded that no such reconstitution was possible: the sterling area's viability depended on the willingness of Commonwealth central banks to hold sterling reserves, and the Commonwealth central banks would hold sterling reserves only for as long as sterling was convertible into dollars, and sterling was convertible into dollars only for as long as the United States permitted it to be. The conclusion of the Makins paper was that the United Kingdom's monetary independence had been an illusion since 1949, that the illusion had been maintained by American forbearance rather than by British reserves, and that the withdrawal of forbearance in November 1956 had disclosed a structural condition rather than created one. Macmillan, who succeeded Eden on 10 January 1957, read the paper, filed it, and spent the six years of his premiership rebuilding the Anglo-American relationship on the basis that the United Kingdom would henceforth conduct no foreign policy of consequence without prior American consent.

The lesson is not that the United States is an unreliable ally, which is a trivial observation, nor that reserve currencies confer geopolitical power, which is a commonplace one. The lesson is that the geopolitical power of a reserve currency is exercised not through visible confrontation but through the withdrawal of the invisible support on which the subordinate power's daily operations depend, and that the moment of exercise is normally the moment at which the subordinate power discovers that the support was invisible because it was continuous, not because it was absent. Britain in November 1956 did not lose a war it was winning. It discovered that it had been fighting inside a framework whose terms of settlement were being written elsewhere, and that the elsewhere had decided the war would end. The Chinese authorities, watching the American dollar system in 2026 from the far side of the last decade of American monetary primacy, are understood in the Beijing archives that will be released in fifty years to have studied Suez as their principal reference case. They have concluded, correctly, that the alternative to owning the system is to be owned by it. Whether they have concluded, also correctly, that the transition from being owned to owning cannot be effected without a war, is the question on which the twenty-first century will turn.
