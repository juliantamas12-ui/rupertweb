# The Three Days in August 1991 When the KGB Chairman Vladimir Kryuchkov Ordered the Alpha Group to Arrest Boris Yeltsin at His Dacha and the Alpha Group Refused, Which Was the Moment the Soviet Union Ended

At 04:32 on the morning of Monday, 19 August 1991, a column of thirty officers of the KGB's Alpha Group — the elite counter-terrorism unit that reported directly to the Chairman of the Committee for State Security — took up positions in a wooded stretch of the Uspenskoye Highway approximately four hundred metres from the driveway of the dacha at Arkhangelskoye-2 where Boris Nikolayevich Yeltsin, President of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic, was sleeping. The Alpha Group's orders, transmitted by radio at 03:15 by Major-General Viktor Karpukhin, its commanding officer, were to enter the dacha at 05:00, detain Yeltsin, and transport him to a military airfield at Chkalovsky, from which he was to be flown to a KGB facility in Zavidovo. The State Committee on the State of Emergency — the eight-man junta that had, at 04:00 that morning, informed the Soviet population by radio that Mikhail Sergeyevich Gorbachev was incapacitated for reasons of health and that the committee had assumed the powers of the presidency — had determined that Yeltsin was the single individual whose immediate detention was strategically indispensable to the coup's success. The Alpha Group was in position at 04:35. It did not move.

The coup had been in preparation since the beginning of August. The eight members of the State Committee on the State of Emergency — Gennady Yanayev, Vice President of the USSR and titular head of the junta; Valentin Pavlov, Prime Minister; Vladimir Kryuchkov, Chairman of the KGB; Dmitry Yazov, Minister of Defence; Boris Pugo, Minister of Internal Affairs; Oleg Baklanov, First Deputy Chairman of the Defence Council; Vasily Starodubtsev, Chairman of the Peasants' Union; and Alexander Tizyakov, President of the Association of State Enterprises — represented the leadership of every Soviet institution that had lost power under Gorbachev's reforms and that stood to lose more when the new Union Treaty was signed on 20 August. **The coup was scheduled for the morning of 19 August precisely because it was the last morning on which the Soviet Union in its 1922 form still legally existed.** By the evening of 20 August, under the terms of the treaty Gorbachev had negotiated with Yeltsin and the leaders of eight other republics at Novo-Ogaryovo, the USSR would have ceased to be a unitary state and would have become a confederation whose central government had no independent taxing power, no independent army, and no legal authority over the republics that chose to remain outside the treaty. The plotters had a window of eighteen hours in which to prevent this outcome. They used it to fail.

The failure at Arkhangelskoye-2 was not the only failure of the morning of 19 August, but it was the failure that determined the outcome. Karpukhin's orders were to detain Yeltsin. Karpukhin's orders were not to shoot Yeltsin, and this distinction — trivially procedural in appearance — was the aperture through which the coup collapsed. The Alpha Group's officers were professionals whose training was calibrated for hostage rescue and counter-terrorism operations against armed opponents. **They had not been trained to arrest the elected president of the Russian Federation in his pyjamas, and when their commanding officer transmitted the order at 03:15 he did so without providing the political cover — the signed decree of the Emergency Committee identifying Yeltsin as a criminal, the presence of a civilian prosecutor to serve a warrant, the legal formalities that would have transformed the operation from a kidnapping into a lawful detention — that his subordinates would have required to execute the order without hesitation.** Karpukhin's later testimony, given to the Russian Prosecutor General's Office in October 1991 and again to the parliamentary investigation of 1992, established that at 04:45 he transmitted a request to Kryuchkov for written authorisation of the operation, that Kryuchkov did not respond, and that at 05:00 — the hour at which the arrest was to have been made — Karpukhin ordered the Alpha Group to hold its position pending clarification of the operational order. The clarification never came. At 07:15, having heard no further instructions, Karpukhin ordered the withdrawal of the group to its base at Balashikha. Yeltsin left the dacha in a Zil limousine at 08:30, drove through the roadblocks of the Emergency Committee's own troops — who had not been briefed on his identity — and arrived at the White House, the seat of the Russian Supreme Soviet, at 09:15.

By the middle of the afternoon of 19 August, Yeltsin was on top of a T-72 tank of the Taman Guards Division outside the White House, reading a proclamation that denounced the Emergency Committee as unconstitutional and called on Soviet citizens to resist. The photograph — Yeltsin in a dark suit, one hand raised, the tank commander watching him with an expression that Russian commentators have subsequently described as the moment at which the Soviet military discovered that it had two commanders-in-chief and would have to choose one — was on the front page of the New York Times the next morning. **It was also on the front page of every Soviet newspaper that had a printing press outside the direct control of the Emergency Committee, which is to say every Soviet newspaper published in Leningrad, in Sverdlovsk, in Novosibirsk, and in the capitals of the fourteen non-Russian republics.** The Emergency Committee had ordered the closure of the independent Soviet press at 06:00. It had not ordered the seizure of the printing presses, because it did not know where all of them were.

The second day of the coup — Tuesday, 20 August — was the day on which the plotters had to decide whether to storm the White House. Kryuchkov ordered the operation, codenamed GROM (Thunder), for 03:00 on the morning of 21 August. It was to be executed by the Alpha Group, by units of the Vitebsk Airborne Division, and by tank units of the Kantemir Division. **At the operational briefing at KGB Second Chief Directorate headquarters at 20:00 on the evening of 20 August, the three unit commanders — Karpukhin of Alpha, Colonel Boris Beskov of the Vympel special operations group, and Lieutenant-General Pavel Grachev of the Airborne Forces — separately informed their superiors that their units would not execute the operation.** Karpukhin's justification was that the Alpha Group's charter did not permit operations against civilian gatherings; the White House was surrounded by an estimated seventy thousand unarmed civilians who had formed a human perimeter around it, and the professional cost of massacring them would have destroyed the unit's institutional standing for a generation. Grachev's justification — reported to Yazov at 21:30 — was that the Airborne Forces would not fire on the elected president of the Russian Federation. Beskov's justification was that his unit was undermanned for the task described and that the operational plan was, in his professional judgement, incompetent.

At 22:00 on the evening of 20 August, Kryuchkov received the three refusals in sequence and understood what they meant. The instruments of Soviet power had been withdrawn from Soviet power. **The coup had been defeated not by Yeltsin's speech from the tank, and not by the seventy thousand civilians around the White House, but by the refusal of three career officers of the Soviet security services to execute an order that would have required them to shoot Russians on behalf of a committee whose legal authority they could not verify.** At 03:00 on the morning of 21 August, the hour at which GROM was to have begun, the three units remained in their barracks. At 04:00, Yazov ordered the withdrawal of army units from Moscow. At 08:00, the members of the Emergency Committee began to flee the city. Kryuchkov and Yazov flew to Foros at 14:00 in an attempt to negotiate their surrender with Gorbachev, who declined to see them and returned to Moscow that evening. Pugo shot himself and his wife on the morning of 22 August. Marshal Sergei Akhromeyev, who had provided the Emergency Committee with military advice without being formally a member, hanged himself in his Kremlin office on 24 August. Yanayev was arrested. Kryuchkov was arrested. Yazov was arrested. The Soviet Union was formally dissolved four months and eight days later.

The lesson of the seventy-two hours between the Alpha Group's arrival at Arkhangelskoye-2 and Kryuchkov's arrest is not that the Soviet Union was destroyed by Yeltsin's courage or by the crowds around the White House, though both mattered. The lesson is that a regime whose instruments of coercion have been trained to operate within a framework of formal legality — which was, paradoxically, one of the achievements of Gorbachev's six years of glasnost and perestroika — cannot execute a coup that requires those instruments to act outside the framework, because the officers whose participation is required will, at the critical moment, ask for the paperwork, and there will be no paperwork, because a coup is definitionally an operation for which there is no paperwork. **The Alpha Group did not defeat the August coup because it disapproved of the coup. It defeated the coup because it required a signed warrant to arrest the President of Russia, and the Chairman of the KGB, who could have signed such a warrant on his own authority in 1937 or 1953 or 1964, could no longer do so in August 1991 without exposing himself to a level of personal legal risk that he was not prepared to accept.** The Soviet Union ended when its own security services began to behave as if the rule of law applied to them. The rule of law had been introduced by Gorbachev to save the Soviet Union. Its principal effect was to make the Soviet Union unrescuable by the methods that had rescued it in every previous crisis of its history.

Yeltsin governed Russia for eight years. He was replaced on the last day of 1999 by Vladimir Putin, a former KGB Lieutenant Colonel who had watched the August coup from Dresden, where he was burning files at the KGB's East German residency, and who has spent the twenty-six years of his subsequent political career reconstructing an executive that does not ask for the paperwork. The three officers who refused Kryuchkov's order in August 1991 have been erased from the Russian historiography of the period. Karpukhin died in 2003, having spent his last decade insisting in interviews that the Alpha Group had never received an order to arrest Yeltsin — a claim that the archival record, released by the Russian Prosecutor General's Office in 1994, contradicts on every material point. **The Russian state that succeeded the Soviet Union has preferred to remember August 1991 as a moment when a resolute leader stood on a tank and defied the tyrants. It has preferred not to remember that the tyrants were defeated by a middle-aged KGB colonel who asked to see the warrant, and did not receive one, and went home.**
