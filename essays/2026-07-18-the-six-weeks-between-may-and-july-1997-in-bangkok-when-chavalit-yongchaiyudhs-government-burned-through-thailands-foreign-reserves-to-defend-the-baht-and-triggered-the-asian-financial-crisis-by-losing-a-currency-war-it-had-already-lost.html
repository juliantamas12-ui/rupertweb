The Six Weeks Between May and July 1997 in Bangkok When Chavalit Yongchaiyudh's Government Burned Through Thailand's Foreign Reserves to Defend the Baht and Triggered the Asian Financial Crisis by Losing a Currency War It Had Already Lost

On 2 July 1997, at 08:30 Bangkok time, the Bank of Thailand announced that it was abandoning the fixed exchange rate that had pegged the baht to a basket of currencies dominated by the US dollar since 1984, and would allow the currency to float. Within hours the baht had lost 15 per cent of its value. Within a fortnight it had lost 20 per cent. By the end of the year it had lost half. The announcement — read by finance minister Thanong Bidaya in a hurried press conference that had been scheduled for 06:00 that morning and delayed for two and a half hours while the cabinet argued about the exact language — was the trigger for the most severe regional financial crisis of the twentieth century, which within six months had spread to Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, South Korea and Hong Kong, cost approximately $850 billion in destroyed asset values, forced the resignations of Suharto in Indonesia and Kim Young-sam in South Korea, and reshaped the East Asian development model in ways that are still being worked out three decades later.

What is worth understanding about the Thai devaluation is not that it happened but what the Thai government did in the six weeks before it happened. Between mid-May and 2 July 1997, the Chavalit Yongchaiyudh government — a fragile six-party coalition led by a retired general who had come to power in November 1996 on a populist platform of expanding rural credit and defending Thai economic sovereignty against foreign speculators — spent an estimated $23 billion of Thailand's $38 billion in foreign exchange reserves defending a currency peg it knew, from at least the first week of May, that it could not permanently hold. The government also entered into approximately $28 billion in forward contracts to sell dollars for baht, which committed the Bank of Thailand to delivering dollars it did not possess at exchange rates it could no longer sustain, and which converted a currency crisis into a solvency crisis. When the peg was abandoned on 2 July, the Bank of Thailand had approximately $2.8 billion in usable reserves — enough to cover roughly two weeks of imports — and $28 billion in forward liabilities that it could not honour without borrowing from abroad. The devaluation, in other words, was not a policy choice. It was a bankruptcy declaration disguised as a policy choice, and the Thai government had spent six weeks and 60 per cent of the nation's foreign savings pretending otherwise.

The peg's structural problem had been visible for at least eighteen months. Thailand had liberalised its capital account between 1990 and 1994 without floating the baht — a combination that macroeconomic theory calls the "impossible trinity" and that the Thai finance ministry had convinced itself did not apply to the Thai case because of Thailand's export competitiveness and current account discipline. It applied. Thai banks, and in particular the fifty-eight finance companies that had proliferated in the early 1990s under the light regulatory hand of the Bank of Thailand, borrowed dollars from Japanese and European banks at low interest rates, converted them to baht at the fixed peg, and lent them to Thai property developers at rates that captured a spread of 8 to 12 percentage points. The property boom this financed produced a stock of unsold Bangkok office space that reached the equivalent of five years of demand by 1996. The finance companies were, by mid-1996, insolvent in every economically meaningful sense, and the Thai regulatory apparatus was aware of this and had chosen not to act. The Bank of Thailand's own internal estimate, prepared by governor Rerngchai Marakanond in December 1996 and shown to fewer than a dozen officials, was that between eighteen and twenty-two of the fifty-eight finance companies were unable to meet their obligations without central bank support. The estimate was accurate. The response was to extend liquidity assistance through the Financial Institutions Development Fund — approximately 430 billion baht by June 1997 — and to conceal the extent of the intervention from the market.

The currency attack began in earnest on 14 May 1997. Hedge funds — principally Julian Robertson's Tiger Management and George Soros's Quantum Fund, though the attack ultimately involved perhaps two dozen institutions — began building large short positions in the baht through the offshore forwards market, having concluded from the finance-company distress and from the deteriorating current account balance (which reached –8 per cent of GDP in 1996) that the peg could not hold. The Bank of Thailand's response was to intervene in the offshore market to drive short-term interbank interest rates to 1,000 per cent — literally 1,000 per cent, briefly — in order to force the hedge funds to close their positions at a loss. This defence succeeded, in the narrow sense that the immediate attack was repelled and Julian Robertson lost approximately $2 billion. It failed, in the larger sense that it committed the Bank of Thailand to a policy of forward-market intervention that concealed the erosion of its reserves and made the eventual devaluation a solvency event rather than a policy event.

The critical political fact is that Chavalit's government did not want to know what its own central bank was doing. Chavalit had been elected on a promise to defend the baht — his campaign had explicitly framed devaluation as a betrayal of national dignity — and his finance minister at the time, Amnuay Viravan, had resigned on 19 June 1997 in protest at the government's refusal to accept the inevitability of devaluation. Amnuay's replacement, Thanong Bidaya, took office on 21 June with three days of Treasury briefings that he later described as "the most frightening documents I have ever read." By 24 June, Thanong had concluded that devaluation was unavoidable. Between 24 June and 2 July, the Chavalit cabinet debated the timing of an announcement while the Bank of Thailand continued to burn through reserves at a rate of approximately $1.5 billion per week defending a peg that the finance minister had privately abandoned. The peg was defended for eight further days after its principal defender had concluded it should be abandoned, because Chavalit could not bring himself to announce the failure of the policy he had been elected to protect.

The eight-day delay was consequential. It exhausted the reserves that Thailand would have needed to negotiate a stabilisation package from a position of solvency. It committed the Bank of Thailand to forward contracts that increased the eventual scale of the devaluation. It denied the Bank of Thailand the interest-rate room it would have needed to defend the floated currency in the weeks immediately following the float. And it destroyed whatever credibility the Chavalit government had retained with the Thai financial community, which had by late June concluded that the government was concealing information about the state of the reserves. When Thanong announced the float on 2 July, the announcement was accompanied by the freezing of sixteen finance companies — a partial admission of the finance-company crisis that had been concealed for six months — and by the suspension of trading in the shares of a further forty-two. The Thai state, in a single morning, admitted three separate failures — the currency peg, the finance-company regulation and the government's honesty about both — that it had spent months insisting were not failures.

The regional contagion followed the pattern that the Thai devaluation had established. The Philippine peso was floated on 11 July. The Indonesian rupiah widened its trading band on 11 July and floated on 14 August. The Malaysian ringgit was subjected to a speculative attack that Mahathir Mohamad's government met with capital controls on 1 September 1998, breaking with the IMF orthodoxy that Thailand, Indonesia and South Korea were compelled to accept. The Hong Kong dollar was attacked in October 1997, defended successfully by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority at a cost that transformed the political economy of the special administrative region. The Korean won collapsed in November 1997, requiring an IMF programme of $58 billion — at the time the largest in history — that reshaped the chaebol-based Korean development model in ways that the Kim Dae-jung government would spend five years working through. Russia defaulted in August 1998 on debt that had been priced on the assumption that emerging-market default risk was rising in a predictable way, which it was not, and Long-Term Capital Management collapsed in September 1998 for reasons that traced ultimately back to the Bangkok forwards market. The crisis that Chavalit's government had insisted was a Thai problem was, within eighteen months, a global problem.

The intellectual consequences of the Asian crisis are still with us. The IMF programmes imposed on Thailand, Indonesia and South Korea in 1997-98 — with their conditions on capital account liberalisation, financial-sector restructuring and fiscal austerity — became the touchstone against which every subsequent developing-country encounter with the fund would be measured, and would be judged, on the whole, punitively. The East Asian development model, which had been credited with the "miracle" that the World Bank documented in its 1993 report, was reassessed by the same institutions that had promoted it. Capital-account liberalisation, which had been the operating principle of IMF policy since the 1980s, was quietly reconsidered — in 2012 the IMF formally endorsed the use of capital controls in specific circumstances, reversing three decades of doctrine. And the accumulation of foreign exchange reserves by East Asian central banks in the decade after the crisis — reserves that had reached approximately $6 trillion by 2010, with China alone holding more than $3 trillion — was the direct legacy of the humiliation of 1997, and became the mechanism by which East Asian savings financed the American current account deficit that produced the 2008 financial crisis.

Chavalit himself resigned on 3 November 1997, replaced by the Democrat Party leader Chuan Leekpai in a coalition reshuffling that avoided a general election. He remained active in Thai politics for a further decade, holding a series of ceremonial positions and briefly serving as deputy prime minister under Thaksin Shinawatra. He died in Bangkok in 2024. His obituaries recorded him primarily as the general who had been prime minister during the 1997 crisis, which is accurate but incomplete: he was the prime minister who had spent Thailand's foreign reserves defending a policy his own finance minister had told him to abandon, and who had converted a currency devaluation into a regional financial crisis by insisting for eight days that the devaluation was not necessary.

The lesson is a specific and uncomfortable one about the relationship between democratic politics and financial technocracy. A currency peg is a promise that a government makes to itself about the discipline it will accept in exchange for stability. The peg fails when the discipline fails. The failure of the peg, once it becomes visible to markets, is not something a government can talk its way out of by burning through its reserves. The reserves are not the peg's defence. They are the evidence of the peg's failure. Every dollar spent defending an unsustainable peg is a dollar transferred from the country's future to speculators betting on the government's inability to admit what it has already lost. Chavalit spent $23 billion transferring Thailand's savings to hedge funds that had correctly identified what his own central bank knew and refused to say. The transfer was not a bug. It was the mechanism by which markets punish governments for lying about their reserves. It is still the mechanism. The Chinese government, the Russian government and the Turkish government have each, in the two and a half decades since Bangkok, found themselves defending currency positions that they eventually had to abandon, and each has repeated some part of the Thai pattern — the intervention, the concealment, the forward contracts, the eventual admission — with variations that reflect their particular political economies. The Thai experience remains the reference case, because it is the case in which the entire mechanism was documented in real time and later reconstructed by three separate official inquiries, and because it is the case in which the political cost of admitting a policy failure was measured in the trillions of dollars of asset value that the failure to admit it ultimately destroyed.
