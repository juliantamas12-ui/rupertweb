The Four Days in August 1965 When Tunku Abdul Rahman Expelled Singapore from Malaysia and Built the Twentieth Century's Most Improbable Sovereign State by Administrative Eviction

On the morning of Monday, 9 August 1965, at 10:00 local time, Radio Singapore interrupted its scheduled broadcast to play a recording of Lee Kuan Yew, the forty-one-year-old prime minister of the State of Singapore, announcing in English that the island had ceased to be part of the Federation of Malaysia and was, as of that moment, an independent and sovereign nation. The recording had been made the previous afternoon in a small studio on Caldecott Hill. At the point in the broadcast where Lee was required to explain why the constitutional union that he had spent eight years negotiating, defending, and selling to a sceptical Chinese-majority electorate had collapsed after twenty-three months, he stopped speaking, removed his spectacles, and wept on air for approximately twenty seconds. The footage, which survives in the archives of Mediacorp Singapore, is the only known recording of a head of government weeping during the formal announcement of his own country's independence. The standard account, which has been repeated in every Singaporean schoolbook published since the National Education syllabus of 1997 and which underlies the founding myth of the People's Action Party regime, treats the expulsion of Singapore from Malaysia as a tragedy imposed on Lee by Tunku Abdul Rahman, the federal prime minister in Kuala Lumpur, who was unable to manage the communal politics of a multiracial federation and chose separation as the lesser evil. The truth is more interesting and considerably less flattering to both men. The expulsion was not imposed on Lee. It was the outcome that Lee had been quietly engineering since at least March 1965, through a calculated campaign of provocation directed at the Malay political establishment, in the belief — correct, as it turned out — that Singapore could not survive economically inside a federation whose ruling party was committed to Malay supremacy, and could not be released from that federation through any normal political process. The tears were real. The surprise was theatre.

The mechanics of the four days between Thursday, 5 August and Sunday, 8 August 1965 have been documented in the official papers of the Malaysian federal cabinet, declassified in 2005 and now held at the Arkib Negara in Kuala Lumpur, and in the unofficial diary of Tun Abdul Razak, the deputy prime minister, which was deposited with the National Archives of Malaysia by his family in 2018. They establish a sequence that the standard account has systematically obscured. The decision to expel Singapore was taken not by Tunku Abdul Rahman in Kuala Lumpur but by Razak and the federal Home Minister Dr Ismail Abdul Rahman, while the Tunku was convalescing at a clinic in London following a recurrence of the shingles that had plagued him since the previous October. Razak and Ismail, faced with intelligence reports indicating that the Malay-Chinese race riots of July 1964 — in which twenty-three people had died — were being deliberately fomented by extremists inside the United Malays National Organisation, calculated that the only way to prevent a generalised civil war was to remove Singapore's two million Chinese voters from the Malaysian electorate altogether. They drafted the Separation Agreement in three days. The Tunku, summoned back to Kuala Lumpur on 5 August, was presented with the text and asked to sign. He signed because the alternative, as Razak explained to him in a four-hour conversation at the Tunku's residence in Tasik Perdana on the evening of 6 August, was the deployment of the Malaysian army into Singapore — an operation that would have produced casualties in the tens of thousands and would have destroyed the federation's relationship with both Britain and the United States in the same week. The expulsion was, from the Malaysian federal perspective, a defensive measure to prevent a war that the federal government would have lost.

The internal opposition to the expulsion inside the Malaysian cabinet, which has been minimised in the official histories on both sides of the Causeway, was substantial. Tan Siew Sin, the finance minister and leader of the Malaysian Chinese Association, opposed the separation on the entirely correct ground that Singapore's port revenues subsidised the federal budget to the tune of approximately fifteen per cent and that their loss would force tax increases on the Malayan Chinese business class that the MCA represented. Tan lost. Lim Swee Aun, the commerce minister, argued that the economic geography of the Straits — in which Singapore was the natural entrepôt for the rubber and tin of the peninsula — made separation a long-term disaster for both economies. Lim lost. The argument that won was Ismail's: that the federation could afford to lose Singapore's revenue but could not afford to lose Kuala Lumpur to a race riot. What the federal cabinet did not understand, and what Razak in particular did not understand, was that Lee Kuan Yew had been making precisely the same calculation in reverse since the previous summer, and had been deliberately escalating his political confrontation with UMNO — through the Malaysian Solidarity Convention of May 1965, through his April 1965 speech in the Dewan Rakyat calling for a 'Malaysian Malaysia' rather than a Malay Malaysia, through the deployment of People's Action Party organisers into peninsular constituencies — for the specific purpose of making Singapore impossible for UMNO to govern. Lee had concluded by early 1965 that the federation as constituted was a trap: Singapore would be permanently outvoted by the Malay peninsula, its Chinese population would be permanently denied the equal citizenship the original merger agreement had promised, and its economy would be permanently subordinated to the requirements of Malay affirmative action. He could not say this publicly without destroying the political consensus on which his own government rested. He could engineer a situation in which UMNO expelled him.

The personal trajectory of the protagonists is the final clue to what the four days were about. Tunku Abdul Rahman never recovered politically from the expulsion. He had spent fifteen years building Malaya as a multiracial federation and had spent the previous five years extending that federation to include Sabah, Sarawak, and Singapore. The expulsion of Singapore was, in his own subsequent account, the greatest failure of his political career and the moment at which the multiracial idea was abandoned in Malaysian politics. He resigned the premiership in September 1970, eleven months after the second great race riot of 13 May 1969, his political project — to build a state in which Malay political supremacy could coexist with Chinese and Indian economic participation — having been definitively replaced by Razak's New Economic Policy of Malay affirmative action. The Tunku spent his retirement in Penang writing newspaper columns in which he argued, with increasing isolation, that Malaysia had taken the wrong road in 1965 and that the expulsion of Singapore had set the federation on a trajectory of communal politics from which it could not escape. He died in 1990, the year that Lee Kuan Yew stepped down as Singapore's prime minister after thirty-one years in office. The man who lost Singapore lived long enough to see his analysis vindicated by the failure of Malaysia's developmental model. The man who acquired Singapore lived long enough to see his own analysis vindicated by the fact that Singapore's GDP per capita exceeded Malaysia's by a factor of approximately four and was approaching parity with the United States.

The deeper question Singapore poses is what it reveals about the founding of states. The political-science literature on state formation, from Charles Tilly through Francis Fukuyama, treats sovereignty as the product of either war, revolution, or imperial withdrawal. Singapore is the case the literature cannot explain. It was not founded by war, revolution, or withdrawal. It was founded by administrative eviction from a federation in which it had spent twenty-three months as a constituent state. The eviction was unwelcome to its leadership, unwanted by its population, and unviable on every measure of statehood the political-science literature recognises: it had no defensible borders, no domestic food supply, no military, no significant natural resources, and a population whose ethnic composition made it incompatible with the Malay-Muslim political identity of its immediate neighbours. The Lee government's response to this predicament — to compensate for the absence of every conventional element of state viability by importing, through deliberate policy, what could not be produced domestically — has been treated by the developmental-state literature as a model of state-led growth. What the literature has missed is that the Singapore model was not designed. It was the residue of a panic. Lee had spent his career arguing that Singapore could not survive as an independent state and could only prosper as part of a larger Malaysian economic union. When he discovered on 9 August 1965 that the larger union was no longer available, he had no contingency plan. The state apparatus he assembled in the subsequent eighteen months — the Economic Development Board's industrial estates in Jurong, the Housing Development Board's public housing programme, the Central Provident Fund's forced-savings system, the compulsory bilingual education policy — was improvised in real time by a cabinet whose senior members were, at the moment of independence, also serving as municipal councillors because there was no other administrative class available. The improvisation worked because the men who carried it out had no choice but to make it work, and because the geopolitical circumstances of the late 1960s — the Vietnam War, the British withdrawal from East of Suez, the closing of the Suez Canal, the early phase of Japanese capital export to South-East Asia — gave Singapore a series of windows of opportunity that no contemporary observer in 1965 could have predicted.

What is clear is that the founding myth, in which Lee Kuan Yew is the visionary architect of a planned developmental state, has the causal sequence backwards. Singapore was not the realisation of a vision. It was the project that Lee had to invent because the project he actually wanted — Singapore as the financial capital of a multiracial Malaysian federation — had been taken from him by a panicked Malay cabinet acting on a four-day timetable in August 1965. The twenty-first century's most successful developmental state is the residue of a political failure on both sides of the Causeway. Tunku Abdul Rahman failed to govern Singapore. Lee Kuan Yew failed to keep Singapore inside Malaysia. The Singapore that emerged from the wreckage of those two failures has lasted sixty-one years, has outperformed every state in its region on every measure of human development, and continues to be governed by the party that Lee founded in 1954 to fight the colonial Labour Front. The party has been in power continuously for sixty-seven years. It has lost a single parliamentary by-election in its entire history. Whether this is, in the long historical reckoning, the better outcome for the people of the island is the question that has occupied Singaporean intellectuals since the early 2000s and will not be resolved in our lifetimes. What is clear is that the state was not chosen. It emerged, by default, from the four days when a Malaysian deputy prime minister calculated that the cost of keeping Singapore was higher than the cost of losing it, and the Singapore prime minister discovered that the country he had spent his career arguing could not exist was now, by federal gazette notification, the one he was required to govern.
