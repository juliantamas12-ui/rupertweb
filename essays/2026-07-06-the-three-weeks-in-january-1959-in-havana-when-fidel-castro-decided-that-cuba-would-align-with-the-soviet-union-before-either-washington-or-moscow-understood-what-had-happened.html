The Three Weeks in January 1959 in Havana When Fidel Castro Decided That Cuba Would Align With the Soviet Union Before Either Washington or Moscow Understood What Had Happened

At 09:00 on Thursday, 8 January 1959, Fidel Castro entered Havana at the head of a column of approximately three thousand rebels from the Sierra Maestra, riding on the turret of a captured M4 Sherman tank that had belonged until the previous week to the Cuban Army's 12th Battalion at Santa Clara. The column had covered the nine hundred kilometres from Santiago in seven days, moving at the pace of a triumphal procession rather than a military advance, because the Batista government had ceased to exist on 1 January when the dictator's aircraft had departed Camp Columbia for the Dominican Republic at 03:00 with fourteen suitcases of currency and forty members of his family. Castro spoke that evening for four hours from the platform of Camp Columbia — since renamed Ciudad Libertad — to an audience the Havana police estimated at one million, and in the course of his speech released a single white dove which, according to the photograph taken by Alberto Korda and printed in Revolución the following morning, alighted on his left shoulder and remained there for the duration of the peroration. The image, distributed by the Associated Press to two hundred and forty newspapers in the Western Hemisphere, established Castro in the American imagination as a picturesque nationalist reformer of a type that Latin America had produced before and would produce again, and it was on this basis that the Eisenhower administration extended diplomatic recognition to the new government at 15:00 on 7 January, six days after Batista's flight and one day before Castro entered the capital. Herbert Matthews of the New York Times, writing on 8 January under the headline "A New Chapter for Cuba", predicted that Castro would preside over a two-year democratic transition culminating in constitutional elections in 1960. The CIA station in Havana, in a cable dispatched to Langley on 12 January and preserved in FRUS 1958-60 Volume VI, assessed that Castro was "a nationalist of the Peronist school" with "no discernible Communist sympathies" and "no realistic prospect of consolidating a radical regime". Both assessments were wrong within three weeks, and they were wrong not because the evidence was insufficient but because the analytical categories through which the evidence was interpreted had not been updated since 1954.

The standard chronology of the Cuban Revolution's alignment with the Soviet Union places the decisive moment either in April 1961, when Castro declared the Revolution "socialist" at the funeral of the Bay of Pigs dead, or in December 1961, when he declared himself a "Marxist-Leninist" in a five-hour televised speech from the Palacio Presidencial. This chronology is a product of Castro's own subsequent reconstruction of events, undertaken between 1965 and 1975 to align his personal history with the Party history that the newly-formed Partido Comunista de Cuba required. The archival record — specifically the correspondence between Castro and his brother Raúl preserved in the Archivo del Consejo de Estado in Havana, released in fragmentary form to Cuban historians after 2011 and reconstructed by Tad Szulc in Fidel: A Critical Portrait — establishes that the strategic decision to seek Soviet protection had been taken by the end of January 1959, in a series of conversations at the Hotel Nacional between Fidel, Raúl, Che Guevara, and the Spanish Communist exile Alberto Bayo. The immediate provocation was neither ideological nor personal but geographical: Castro had understood, in a way that his American interlocutors had not, that a Cuban government which pursued significant land reform, nationalisation of American assets, or independent foreign policy would be overthrown by the United States within eighteen months unless it acquired an extra-hemispheric protector, and that the only extra-hemispheric protector available was the Soviet Union. The decision to align with Moscow was not the consequence of a radicalising process that unfolded through 1960 and 1961. It was the initial condition of the revolutionary programme, taken at the moment when Castro understood the geometry of Caribbean geopolitics and before he had introduced a single reform that would justify American hostility. Everything that followed — the agrarian reform of May 1959, the nationalisation of the sugar mills in July 1960, the Bay of Pigs, the Missile Crisis — was the working out of a decision that had been taken in the third week of January 1959, in a corner suite of the Hotel Nacional overlooking the Malecón, by four men who had understood the American Republic better than the American Republic understood itself.

The conversations at the Hotel Nacional, reconstructed from Bayo's memoir Mi Aporte a la Revolución Cubana (1960) and from Guevara's marginal notes on his personal copy of Lenin's Imperialism preserved in the Casa de las Américas archive, turned on a single strategic question: whether Cuba could survive an American economic embargo of the type imposed on Guatemala in 1954, and if not, from whom it could obtain the imports — principally petroleum and industrial machinery — that the embargo would deny it. Guevara, who had been present in Guatemala City on 27 June 1954 when the CIA-organised coup against Jacobo Árbenz had entered its final phase, argued that the American response to significant reform in Cuba would follow the Guatemalan template exactly: initial diplomatic hostility, followed by economic pressure, followed by covert action, followed by the sponsoring of an armed exile force from a neighbouring country. The Cuban timeline, Guevara estimated on the basis of his own participation in the Árbenz collapse, would be approximately eighteen months from the first significant reform to the first exile invasion. This estimate proved to be correct to within two weeks: the agrarian reform law of 17 May 1959 was followed by the Bay of Pigs invasion on 17 April 1961, an interval of twenty-three months. Guevara's central insight, communicated to Castro in the Hotel Nacional on approximately 24 January 1959, was that a Cuban government which attempted to negotiate with the United States would necessarily lose the negotiation, because the United States possessed structural advantages — geographical proximity, control of the sugar quota, dominance of the refined-petroleum market, and a functioning intelligence apparatus in the exile community — that could not be neutralised by any bilateral concession the Cuban government was willing to make. The only counterweight available was extra-hemispheric alignment. The only extra-hemispheric power capable of providing military guarantees, subsidised oil, and a sugar market of sufficient scale was the Soviet Union. The conclusion followed with the force of arithmetic: if the Revolution was to survive, it would survive as a Soviet client, and if it was to become a Soviet client, the negotiations with Moscow would have to begin immediately, before the Americans understood what was happening, because premature disclosure would trigger the intervention it was designed to prevent.

The mechanism by which the Cuban-Soviet channel was opened, in a manner sufficiently discreet to escape both American detection and Soviet excessive interest, involved a route through the Spanish and Mexican Communist parties that Castro's brother Raúl had established during his exile in 1956. On 4 February 1959, Raúl met the Soviet cultural attaché in Mexico City, Aleksandr Shitov, at a private residence in the Colonia Roma. The conversation, recorded in Shitov's cable to Moscow dispatched on 6 February and declassified from the Russian Foreign Ministry archive in 1996, was structured as a request for cultural exchange — specifically for the visit of a Soviet ballet company to Havana in April — but contained an embedded second proposal for the establishment of a permanent trade delegation with authority to negotiate long-term commodity contracts. Shitov, who was in fact a KGB officer using diplomatic cover, understood the trade delegation proposal as a political overture and forwarded it to Moscow with a recommendation for cautious acceptance. The Soviet Politburo, meeting on 12 February under Khrushchev's chairmanship, considered the proposal for approximately forty minutes and rejected it on the grounds that Cuban political developments were "insufficiently mature" and that "premature Soviet involvement would risk provoking an American reaction disproportionate to the strategic value of the position". Khrushchev's initial rejection of the Cuban overture in February 1959 is one of the least discussed episodes in the history of the Cold War, and it establishes a fact that neither American nor Soviet historiography has been comfortable with: that the Cuban Revolution sought Soviet protection nine months before the Soviet Union was willing to consider providing it. The chronology conventionally recited — that Castro was driven into Moscow's arms by American hostility — inverts the direction of causation. Castro sought Moscow's arms before American hostility existed; American hostility, when it came, was in significant part the consequence of Cuban actions taken in anticipation of the Soviet alignment that had already been decided. The Soviet Union did not seduce Cuba. Cuba pursued the Soviet Union, and had to pursue it for eleven months before Moscow, in the person of Anastas Mikoyan, agreed to visit Havana in February 1960 and initiate the formal relationship.

The interval between January 1959 and February 1960 — during which the Cuban government publicly maintained a posture of non-alignment while privately pressing an increasingly reluctant Kremlin for material support — constitutes the pivotal year of the Revolution, and it has been almost entirely misread by the historiography. The conventional account treats the year as one in which Castro's radicalism was gradually revealed under pressure from American opposition; the Cuban archival record establishes that Castro's radicalism had been settled from the outset and that the year was spent constructing the conditions under which Soviet protection would become politically inevitable for Moscow rather than optional. The agrarian reform of May 1959, the trials and executions of Batista collaborators throughout the spring, the resignation of the moderate President Manuel Urrutia in July, the show trial of Huber Matos in October — none of these measures was necessary for the domestic consolidation of the regime, which had been achieved by February. Each was calibrated to produce a specific American reaction — protest, condemnation, sanctions, reduction of the sugar quota — that would in turn provide the diplomatic justification for the Soviet embrace that Castro required but that Khrushchev was still refusing to grant. The Cuban Revolution's first year was not a process of radicalisation. It was a diplomatic performance designed to compel a reluctant Soviet Union into a strategic commitment it did not want to make, executed by provoking an American response that would leave Moscow no alternative but to intervene on Cuba's behalf. Castro's genius in 1959 lay not in his revolutionary programme but in his understanding that the great powers can be manipulated by small states through the deliberate escalation of second-order conflicts. He forced Moscow's hand by giving Washington no choice.

The moment at which the strategy became irreversible — and at which the Cuban-Soviet alignment ceased to be a Cuban aspiration and became a Soviet necessity — was not any of the events conventionally cited but a meeting in Havana on 4 February 1960 between Castro and Anastas Mikoyan, at which Mikoyan agreed to purchase 425,000 tonnes of Cuban sugar in 1960 and 1,000,000 tonnes annually from 1961 to 1965, at a price of 2.78 cents per pound in a market where the world price was 3.15 cents. The differential was a subsidy of approximately eleven per cent on the total contract value, and it committed Soviet foreign exchange to Cuban support at a scale that could not thereafter be reversed without a corresponding humiliation. Mikoyan had arrived in Havana with instructions to explore, not to commit; he departed with a signed protocol that made the Soviet Union responsible for the survival of the Cuban economy. The five-day Mikoyan visit converted an eleven-month Cuban campaign into a Soviet strategic commitment, and it did so through the specific mechanism of a subsidised sugar contract whose terms had been drafted by Che Guevara three weeks earlier and presented to Mikoyan on his second day in Havana as a fait accompli. Mikoyan signed because his instructions did not permit him to refuse a proposal that had already been briefed to the international press. Castro had bounced the Politburo through its own emissary. From this moment onwards, the trajectory that culminated in the missile deployments of 1962 was locked in, not by any subsequent decision, but by the accumulated logic of commitments already made.

The counterfactual that occupies American Cold War historiography — whether a more accommodating Eisenhower administration could have prevented Cuba's alignment with Moscow — is misconceived because it assumes that Cuban alignment was a response to American action rather than a prior strategic choice for which American action was the necessary justification. The Eisenhower administration was not the agent of Castro's radicalisation; it was the instrument through which Castro compelled a Soviet commitment that would otherwise have been withheld. A more accommodating American policy in 1959 would not have prevented the Cuban-Soviet alliance. It would only have delayed it, by requiring Castro to escalate further before Moscow was persuaded. The alternative counterfactual — whether Khrushchev could have declined the alliance even after Mikoyan's visit — is more interesting, because the archival record suggests that the Soviet Politburo continued to regard the Cuban commitment as strategically dubious as late as 1961, and accepted it only because the alternative would have been the loss of a Communist state ninety miles from Florida with all the ideological damage that would entail. The Cuban missile crisis of October 1962 was not, in this reconstruction, a Soviet initiative to which the United States responded. It was the terminal consequence of a Cuban initiative taken in the Hotel Nacional in January 1959, executed through a year of manufactured provocations, consummated in a subsidised sugar contract in February 1960, and militarised in a missile deployment in July 1962. The crisis that brought the world closest to nuclear war was, at its root, the successful working out of a strategy conceived by four men in a Havana hotel room three days after they had entered the city. The question that historiography has failed to ask is not why the Soviet Union chose to place missiles in Cuba, but why Cuba chose to acquire missiles from the Soviet Union, and the answer is that Cuba had made the choice before the Soviet Union knew it was being made.

The larger implication for the analysis of small-state strategy in a bipolar international system is that the conventional model — small states as objects of great-power competition, aligning with one bloc or the other in response to pressure — inverts the actual causality in cases where the small state possesses a determined leadership and a favourable geography. Cuba in 1959 chose the Soviet Union before the Soviet Union was willing to be chosen, and it engineered the American response that would make Soviet acceptance unavoidable. The strategy required an unusual combination of ideological clarity, diplomatic patience, and a willingness to accept short-term costs for a long-term structural gain, and it succeeded because the two great powers, each accustomed to viewing small states as instruments of their own strategy, could not conceive of a small state that was using them as instruments of its own. The lesson that Castro drew from the Guatemalan collapse of 1954 was not that reformist governments must moderate their programmes to avoid American intervention. It was that reformist governments in the American sphere are destroyed unless they secure extra-hemispheric protection in advance, and that the securing of such protection requires the small state to act as if the alliance already exists, forcing the reluctant great power to ratify what has been constructed unilaterally. Cuba is the only case in the Cold War in which this strategy succeeded, and it succeeded because Castro understood, and Khrushchev did not, that in a bipolar system the tail can wag the dog if the tail moves faster than the dog can think.
