# The Afternoon of 4 August 1922 Near the Village of Ab-i-Derya in the Baldzhuan Valley, When a Red Army Machine-Gun Section Killed Enver Pasha at the Head of a Cavalry Charge and Terminated the Pan-Turkist Project That Had Governed Ottoman Strategy for Fifteen Years

At approximately 15:40 on the afternoon of Friday, 4 August 1922, in a shallow depression in the Baldzhuan valley of eastern Bukhara — a landscape of dry hills and irrigated cotton fields lying between the Vakhsh and Kyzylsu rivers in what is today the Khatlon province of Tajikistan — a section of the 8th Cavalry Brigade of the Red Army of Workers and Peasants, commanded by a twenty-nine-year-old Georgian former Tsarist NCO named Yakov Melkumov, opened fire with two Maxim guns at a range of approximately three hundred metres on a mounted formation of roughly two hundred Basmachi horsemen advancing at the trot across open ground. The horsemen were led personally, at their head and slightly forward of the line, by a small, slender man of forty-one wearing a green tunic of pre-war Ottoman general officer's cut, a white astrakhan cap, and the insignia of the rank of Commander-in-Chief of the Armies of Islam and Son-in-Law of the Caliph. The man was Ismail Enver, formerly Minister of War of the Ottoman Empire, formerly Vice-Generalissimo, formerly the effective head of state of a polity of twenty-five million subjects, and formerly — in his own estimation, and it was not entirely delusional — the successor to Tamerlane, Genghis Khan, and Timur as the unifier of the Turkic peoples of Central Asia into a single Islamic state stretching from the Bosphorus to the Chinese frontier. The Maxim guns fired for perhaps forty seconds. Enver's horse went down first. Enver rose, drew his sabre, and ran forward on foot into the fire. He was struck five times in the chest and abdomen. The cavalry charge dissolved. The pan-Turkist project, which had been the animating idea of Ottoman grand strategy since 1908 and had killed perhaps three million people in the process of its pursuit, ended at 15:41.

The conventional account of Enver's death treats it as a footnote to the Russian Civil War — a colourful episode in the pacification of Soviet Central Asia, involving an exiled adventurer who had lost his empire and gone looking for another one in the mountains. This is not wrong, but it is a category error. Enver did not go to Bukhara in October 1921 because he was an adventurer. He went because the pan-Turkist strategy that he and Talaat Pasha and Cemal Pasha had pursued since the Young Turk coup of 24 July 1908 required, in its final iteration after the loss of Anatolia to Mustafa Kemal, a base of operations east of the Caspian from which the Turkic peoples of Russian Turkestan could be organised into a state capable of federating with, and eventually absorbing, the Republic of Turkey that Kemal was in the process of consolidating around Ankara. The Basmachi revolt against Bolshevik rule, which had begun in Ferghana in February 1918 as a peasant reaction to grain requisitioning and had by 1921 acquired a religious character under the leadership of local emirs and Sufi sheikhs, was to be the vehicle. Enver's ambition was to weld it into a regular army, seize Bukhara and Samarkand, declare an Islamic Emirate of Turkestan under his own leadership, negotiate a federation with Kabul under Amanullah Khan and with Ankara under whatever government would replace Kemal, and present the resulting bloc as the true successor to the Ottoman Empire. He had discussed the plan in Berlin in September 1920 with Karl Radek, who had encouraged him under the impression that he could be used to destabilise British India. He had discussed it in Moscow in October 1920 with Lenin, who had received him politely, given him a residence, and instructed Feliks Dzerzhinsky to monitor his movements. He had discussed it in Baku at the Congress of the Peoples of the East in September 1920, at which he had been denied the platform by Grigory Zinoviev on the grounds that his pan-Turkism was incompatible with proletarian internationalism, but had circulated a written statement in Turkish that had been enthusiastically received by the Central Asian delegates.

The Bolsheviks, in other words, had known what Enver was, had used him for eighteen months as a lever against Kemal — whose government they were simultaneously arming, on the calculation that a Kemalist Turkey aligned with Moscow was preferable to a Kemalist Turkey aligned with London — and had discarded him in October 1921 when he crossed from Batumi to Bukhara without their authorisation and immediately joined the Basmachi. The reversal was decided at a Politburo meeting on 3 November 1921. Lenin's memorandum of that meeting, preserved in the Presidential Archive and released in a Russian scholarly edition in 2001, describes Enver as "an oriental Napoleon of the second rank, useful abroad, intolerable at home." The operational instruction to the Turkestan Front, transmitted through Mikhail Frunze on 8 November, was that Enver was to be captured if possible and killed if not.

The mechanism by which the second option was executed is worth reconstructing, because it illustrates the technical asymmetry that would define every subsequent Soviet counter-insurgency operation from Basmachistan to Afghanistan to Chechnya. The Red Army in Turkestan in the summer of 1922 was outnumbered by the Basmachi by a factor of perhaps three to one in effectives and by perhaps ten to one in horses. It was outmanoeuvred by them in every engagement of squadron scale or below. It could not hold rural districts against them at night. But it possessed three assets that the Basmachi did not: it possessed radio communications between brigade and army headquarters, permitting the concentration of force against a moving target within forty-eight hours; it possessed aircraft of the Farman F.30 and DH.9 types, capable of tracking mounted formations across the treeless steppe and reporting their positions by radio to ground forces; and it possessed Maxim guns mounted on tachanka carts, permitting the delivery of sustained automatic fire at the point of contact by mobile units. The combination of these three assets — reconnaissance from the air, coordination by radio, and concentrated automatic fire at the moment of engagement — was the tactical formula by which pre-modern cavalry armies had been defeated on every steppe from Manchuria to the Sahara since 1898. Enver, who had commanded Ottoman armies against the Russians at Sarikamish in December 1914 and had lost seventy-eight thousand men in ten days to a combination of frostbite and Russian machine-guns, understood the formula intellectually. He believed, on the basis of a mystical conception of Turkic martial spirit that he had absorbed in Berlin from Friedrich von Bernhardi and refined in Damascus from the theology of the Naqshbandi order, that the formula could be defeated by sufficiently determined cavalry élan. He was wrong. The formula killed him at 15:41 on 4 August 1922, and it killed the twenty-eight men of his immediate suite in the same forty-second burst.

The consequences propagated through three theatres. In Turkey, Mustafa Kemal — who had received the news of Enver's death by telegraph from the Soviet ambassador in Ankara on 12 August, four days before he launched the great offensive against the Greek army in Anatolia — was liberated from the possibility of a pan-Turkist rival to his own project of consolidating a nation-state on Anatolian territory. The Republic that he proclaimed on 29 October 1923 was defined constitutionally against everything Enver had stood for: it was Anatolian rather than pan-Turkic, secular rather than Islamic, republican rather than caliphal, and territorial rather than expansionist. The abolition of the Caliphate on 3 March 1924, which shocked Sunni Muslims from Cairo to Delhi, was intelligible only as a repudiation of the specific claim to universal Muslim leadership that Enver had asserted as Son-in-Law of the Caliph. Kemal's Turkey, in short, was the negation of Enver's Turkey, and could not have been constructed while Enver was alive to embody the alternative. In Central Asia, the Basmachi revolt continued in diminishing intensity until the surrender of Ibrahim Bek in June 1931, but it never again possessed the ideological coherence or the strategic ambition that Enver had briefly given it. It became a peasant insurgency of local scope, which the Soviet state pacified by the standard combination of collectivisation, deportation, and the recruitment of Central Asian cadres into the Party. In Afghanistan, Amanullah Khan — who had been in correspondence with Enver through the summer of 1922 and had expected to receive him at Kabul in September — abandoned the plan for a Turco-Afghan federation and turned instead to the programme of internal modernisation that would culminate in his overthrow in 1929.

The instructive feature of Enver's death is that it did not merely eliminate a man. It eliminated a strategy that had governed Ottoman decision-making since the July revolution of 1908, that had determined the entry into the First World War on the German side in November 1914, that had directed the Sarikamish offensive in December 1914 and had thereby precipitated the Armenian deportations of 1915, and that had persisted through the collapse of 1918 and the exile of 1919 as the animating idea of a diaspora of Ottoman officers scattered from Berlin to Baku. The strategy did not survive the man because it had never had any institutional existence independent of him: it was a personal project of Enver's, sustained by his charisma and his marriage into the Ottoman dynasty and his ability to persuade successive audiences in Constantinople and Berlin and Moscow that the Turkic peoples were on the verge of unification if only they were provided with a leader. When the leader was shot in a cotton field in Tajikistan by a Georgian NCO named Melkumov, the project ceased to exist as a factor in international relations, and every subsequent Turkic nationalism — Kemalist, Azerbaijani, Uzbek, Kazakh, Turkmen, Uyghur — was constructed on national rather than pan-national foundations.

The general lesson is that projects which depend on a single carrier are terminated by the elimination of the carrier, even when they have been pursued for decades and have killed millions of people. This is unfashionable to say. The prevailing methodological orthodoxy in political history holds that structural forces determine outcomes and that individual leaders are epiphenomena. The orthodoxy is wrong in the specific sense that some projects, and pan-Turkism is one of them, are so dependent on the biographical accident of a particular carrier that they cannot be sustained after that carrier's death. Melkumov did not know, when he ordered his gunners to open fire at 15:40 on 4 August 1922, that he was closing an epoch. He knew that he was killing a bandit. He was doing both.
