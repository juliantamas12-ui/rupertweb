# The Hundred Days Between March and June 1953 When Lavrentiy Beria Attempted to End the Cold War, Dismantle the Gulag, and Reunify Germany — and Was Shot by His Terrified Colleagues for Being the Most Radical Reformer the Soviet Union Would Ever Produce

Lavrentiy Pavlovich Beria — Georgian by birth, chief of Soviet state security from 1938 to 1946, director of the atomic weapons project from 1945 to 1953, and the single most feared human being in the Soviet Union at the moment of Stalin's death — spent the last hundred days of his life attempting to liquidate the political system he had spent twenty-five years constructing. Between 5 March 1953, when Stalin died, and 26 June 1953, when Beria was arrested at a Presidium meeting in the Kremlin and shot in the basement of a military bunker six months later, he proposed and in most cases enacted a programme of reform so radical that its content did not become public knowledge for four decades and its coherence has still not been assimilated into the popular Western narrative of the Cold War. He amnestied 1.2 million Gulag prisoners. He shut down the Doctors' Plot investigation and exonerated the arrested physicians on live radio. He proposed to the Presidium the reunification of Germany as a neutral, non-socialist state. He suggested the dissolution of the collective farms in the Baltic republics and Western Ukraine. He recommended a rapprochement with Yugoslavia and the return of Tito to the socialist camp on terms Tito would accept. He initiated the release of political prisoners in Hungary and East Germany. He proposed that Russian be replaced by local languages in the administration of the non-Russian republics and that native cadres, not Russians, should hold the senior positions in those republics. He drafted, in early June, a memorandum arguing that the socialist bloc's East European satellites were an economic burden that the Soviet Union could not sustain and should be released. And then, on 26 June, the men he had served alongside for twenty years — Nikita Khrushchev, Georgy Malenkov, Vyacheslav Molotov, Nikolai Bulganin, Lazar Kaganovich, Anastas Mikoyan — arrested him at a Kremlin meeting they had called for that purpose, charged him with treason and espionage on behalf of British intelligence, tried him in secret, and executed him.

**The standard Western narrative treats Beria as a monster whose removal was a moral necessity, and treats the reforms he proposed as either insincere manoeuvres to consolidate personal power or as programmatic positions taken by a coalition of reformers of which he was merely a member. Both readings are wrong.** The archival record, which became accessible after 1991 through the Volkogonov collection and the Presidium protocols published by the Yakovlev Foundation, establishes that the reform programme of the spring of 1953 was driven by Beria personally, over the objections of his colleagues, and that he was removed not because his colleagues opposed his methods but because they were terrified by the direction of his conclusions. Beria was executed for proposing that the Soviet Union should stop being an empire.

To understand why this matters, one has to abandon a widespread misconception about the internal culture of the late Stalinist Politburo. The men around Stalin were not ideologues. They were operatives — administrators who had survived the purges by developing an acute sense of what the leader wanted and by delivering it without asking questions. Beria in particular was, by 1953, an operative of unusual analytical sophistication. He had run the atomic project, which required him to master industrial and scientific problems at a level his colleagues did not attempt. He had run the state security apparatus, which gave him information about the actual condition of Soviet society — the state of the labour camps, the resentments in the non-Russian republics, the collapse of agriculture, the disloyalty of the East European satellites — that none of his colleagues possessed at the same level of detail. And he had, more than any of them, an appreciation of what the atomic balance now permitted the Soviet Union to attempt. The successful test of the Soviet atomic device in August 1949 had, in Beria's analysis, removed the security rationale for the maintenance of an aggressive military posture in Eastern Europe. If Moscow could deter American nuclear attack, it did not need a forward defensive belt in Poland, Hungary, and East Germany. It could afford to release the satellites, reduce military expenditure, and reallocate the resources thus freed to the reconstruction of Soviet living standards.

Beria began proposing this analysis to his colleagues within seventy-two hours of Stalin's death. On 9 March 1953, at the funeral meeting of the reorganised Presidium, he demanded and received the ministry of internal affairs — a merger of the MGB and MVD that placed the entire security apparatus under his personal command — and immediately used this authority to release the Kremlin physicians arrested in the last months of Stalin's paranoia. The Doctors' Plot had been an antisemitic operation that Stalin had planned to escalate into a general purge of the Soviet Jewish population; Beria's dismantling of it, announced by Pravda on 4 April, was the first public signal that a change of direction was underway. He followed this with an amnesty decree, drafted personally, that released prisoners serving sentences of five years or less — approximately 1.2 million people, or roughly half the Gulag population — over the following ten weeks. The amnesty was contested within the Presidium. Molotov argued that the released prisoners would form a criminal underclass that would destabilise the cities; Beria's response, preserved in the stenographic record of the 4 April Presidium session, was that the labour camps were themselves a source of social pathology whose costs had come to exceed their benefits and that the Soviet economy would function better with the released labourers reintegrated into it. He was correct on the second point. He was also correct on the first: the released prisoners did produce a crime wave in the summer of 1953 that his colleagues would use, after his arrest, as retroactive justification for his removal.

**The German question was the operation that broke him.** In the spring of 1953, the German Democratic Republic was in the third year of a forced collectivisation and industrialisation programme that Walter Ulbricht had modelled on Stalinist practice and that had produced, by the winter of 1952-53, mass emigration to the West of approximately 500,000 East Germans, a collapse of food supply, and open unrest in the industrial centres. Beria's analysis of this situation, presented to the Presidium on 27 May 1953 in a joint memorandum with Malenkov, was that the East German experiment had failed, that the attempt to construct socialism in the eastern half of Germany was economically and politically unsustainable, and that the correct response was to abandon it. His concrete proposal was radical. The Soviet Union should propose to the Western powers the reunification of Germany as a neutral, unaligned state on the model of Austria — which was then in the final stages of the treaty negotiations that would produce, in May 1955, the Austrian State Treaty and Austrian neutrality. In exchange for the Western guarantees of German neutrality and the withdrawal of American forces, the Soviet Union would withdraw its own forces from East Germany and accept that the resulting German state would not be socialist. Beria's calculation was that a neutralised Germany at the centre of Europe would fatally undermine the American position on the continent, would eliminate the largest single item of Soviet military expenditure, and would return to Moscow the resources needed for domestic reconstruction. The socialist experiment in Germany was, in his analysis, a diplomatic asset to be traded, not a doctrinal commitment to be defended.

This proposal — that the Soviet Union voluntarily dissolve the German Democratic Republic — was the moment at which Beria lost his colleagues. The stenographic record of the 27 May meeting shows Molotov, who was foreign minister, arguing that the abandonment of East Germany would be understood in the Eastern bloc as a Soviet retreat and would trigger cascading demands for autonomy in Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and Romania. Khrushchev, who had not yet formally consolidated his position but was already the most politically ambitious member of the Presidium, argued that the German workers' state was a doctrinal commitment that could not be abandoned without abandoning Marxism-Leninism itself. **Beria's response, which is preserved in the notes taken by Khrushchev's aide Boris Ponomarev, was that Marxism-Leninism was a set of practical propositions about historical development and that if the historical development in question was not occurring — as it was manifestly not occurring in East Germany — then the doctrine required revision, not the reality.** This was the sentence at which, according to Ponomarev's later testimony, Khrushchev decided that Beria had to be removed. The doctrinal loyalty of the other Presidium members was not, in most cases, personally sincere; but their institutional survival depended on the maintenance of doctrinal orthodoxy, and Beria was proposing to dissolve the doctrine.

The June 17 uprising in East Berlin — which began four days after the Presidium had, under pressure from Beria and Malenkov, ordered Ulbricht to reverse the collectivisation programme — provided the accelerant. Approximately 400,000 East Germans took to the streets between 16 and 18 June demanding free elections, the resignation of Ulbricht, and reunification with the West. Soviet tanks suppressed the uprising, killing at least 55 protesters. Beria travelled personally to Berlin on 18 June to assess the situation; his colleagues in Moscow interpreted this as evidence that he was preparing to seize control of the East German operation and use it to force through his neutralisation proposal despite their opposition. Khrushchev used the week of Beria's absence — 18 to 25 June — to assemble a conspiracy against him. He secured the support of Marshal Georgy Zhukov, who commanded the ground forces of the Ministry of Defence and whose troops could be used to arrest Beria's own security personnel. He obtained the reluctant agreement of Malenkov, who had co-authored the German proposal but who now understood that his own survival required Beria's removal. He recruited Molotov, Bulganin, Kaganovich, Mikoyan, and Voroshilov. When Beria returned to Moscow on 25 June, he walked into a Presidium meeting scheduled for the following day at which every other member had already committed to his arrest.

The arrest itself, on 26 June 1953, was carried out by Marshal Zhukov and a squad of officers who entered the meeting room after Malenkov gave a prearranged signal. Beria was taken to a bunker at the headquarters of the Moscow Military District, held incommunicado, and interrogated for six months while the Presidium constructed a case against him. The eventual charges — that he had been a British agent since 1919, that he had conspired to restore capitalism, that he had planned to seize personal power through a military coup — were fabricated at a level of crudity that even the Soviet judicial system of 1953 found difficult to sustain. His trial, held in secret in December 1953 and presided over by Marshal Ivan Konev, was a formality. He was shot on 23 December 1953 in the basement of the bunker where he had been held, along with six of his closest subordinates. The physical execution was reportedly carried out by General Pavel Batitsky, who fired the fatal shot personally at Konev's order. Beria's body was cremated the same night and the ashes buried in an unmarked location. The archives of his ministry — approximately 300,000 files documenting his personal management of the atomic project, the intelligence networks, and the labour camps — were seized by Khrushchev's staff and either destroyed or sealed under the highest classification. Most remained sealed until 1991.

**The reforms Beria had proposed were not, however, reversed. They were re-enacted, over the following five years, by the same men who had executed him — but at a slower pace, in a more cautious form, and without acknowledging their author.** Khrushchev's Secret Speech of February 1956, denouncing Stalin's crimes, echoed Beria's memorandum on the Doctors' Plot from 4 April 1953. The rehabilitation of the Gulag prisoners, which Khrushchev accelerated after 1956, followed the pattern Beria had established with the amnesty of March 1953. The rapprochement with Yugoslavia, which Khrushchev completed with his trip to Belgrade in May 1955, was the policy Beria had recommended in June 1953. The Austrian State Treaty of May 1955, which produced the neutral unified Austria that Beria had proposed as a model for Germany, was signed by Molotov. Even the reform of the nationality policy in the non-Russian republics, which Khrushchev implemented in fragmentary form after 1956, was drawn from Beria's memoranda of the spring of 1953. The one Beria proposal that Khrushchev did not implement was the German one; and the failure to implement it — the maintenance of the East German experiment for another thirty-six years, at enormous economic cost to the Soviet Union — was, in the end, one of the several structural burdens that would bring the Soviet system down in 1991. **Beria had correctly identified, in May 1953, the specific commitment that would prove unsustainable. The Soviet Union spent the following thirty-eight years demonstrating that he had been right.**

The historiographical problem this presents is uncomfortable. Beria was a moral monster by any reasonable standard. He had personally overseen the executions of tens of thousands of people during the purges. He had run a system of sexual predation, using his ministerial position to abduct and rape women in central Moscow, that his colleagues knew about and tolerated. He was, in the ordinary sense of the term, a criminal. And he was also, at the moment of his death, the single most far-sighted analyst of the Soviet Union's strategic situation that the Politburo had ever contained. **The two facts are not in tension. They are causally connected: Beria's operational proximity to the actual mechanisms of Soviet power — the camps he had run, the intelligence networks he had directed, the industrial projects he had managed — had given him a picture of the system's real condition that his more doctrinally correct colleagues could not access precisely because their positions were built on the maintenance of doctrinal fictions.** He knew that the labour camps were economically counterproductive because he had seen the balance sheets. He knew that East Germany was a losing proposition because his agents had reported the emigration figures. He knew that the non-Russian republics were disaffected because his ministry had produced the disaffection reports. He proposed to act on this information. His colleagues did not want to know it. When he insisted on making them know it, they killed him.

The larger point is that the Soviet Union possessed, in the spring of 1953, a window of perhaps six months during which the Cold War might have ended by Soviet initiative on terms acceptable to the West. The window was closed by a small group of frightened men at a Kremlin meeting on 26 June. The consequences — the maintenance of East Germany, the arms race of the 1960s and 1970s, the Warsaw Pact interventions in Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968, the eventual collapse of 1991 — followed from that decision. **The counterfactual in which Beria lives, and the Soviet Union in 1953 releases its East European satellites, neutralises Germany, and negotiates a European settlement with the Eisenhower administration, is one of the most consequential unrealised possibilities in twentieth-century history. It failed to occur because the man who proposed it was a monster whom his colleagues could not tolerate — and because the proposal itself was too radical to be accepted from anyone.** The tragedy of Beria's hundred days is that the reform of the Soviet system required a reformer who was prepared to dismantle his own achievements, and the only man in the Presidium in 1953 who possessed both the analytical clarity and the personal ruthlessness to do so was also the man whose personal record made it impossible for his colleagues to trust him. He was executed for being simultaneously too clever and too dangerous — but the substantive content of what he was executed for was, in retrospect, correct in almost every particular.

Marshal Batitsky, who fired the shot, reportedly said as he pulled the trigger: "This is for the Party." The Party he was defending had, at that moment, seven months of its founding project remaining. It would spend the next thirty-eight years attempting to reproduce, in slower motion, the reforms it had killed Beria for proposing. **The bullet that ended Beria's life ended the possibility of a Soviet Union that reformed itself in 1953. Everything that followed — including the eventual collapse — was the working out of the alternative.**
