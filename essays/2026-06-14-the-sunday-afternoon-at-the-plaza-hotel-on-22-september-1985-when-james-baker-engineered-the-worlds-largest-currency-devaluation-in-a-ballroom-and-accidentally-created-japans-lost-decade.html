The Sunday Afternoon at the Plaza Hotel on 22 September 1985 When James Baker Engineered the World's Largest Currency Devaluation in a Ballroom and Accidentally Created Japan's Lost Decade

On the afternoon of Sunday, 22 September 1985, in the gold-leaf ballroom of the Plaza Hotel on Fifth Avenue in New York, the finance ministers and central bank governors of the United States, Japan, West Germany, France, and the United Kingdom signed a one-page communiqué declaring that "some further orderly appreciation of the main non-dollar currencies against the dollar is desirable." The phrasing was bureaucratic. The intent was a coordinated devaluation of the United States dollar against the Japanese yen and the West German Deutsche mark on a scale unprecedented in the history of floating exchange rates. The meeting had been convened over a single weekend by the new United States Treasury Secretary, James A. Baker III, who had taken office only seven months earlier in the second-term reshuffle of the Reagan administration. The official press release was issued at 18:00 New York time. By the Tokyo open the next morning the yen had moved from 240 to the dollar to roughly 230. Within twelve months it stood at 153. Within thirty months it touched 121. The dollar had lost half its value against the yen in less than three years.

The standard account treats the Plaza Accord as a triumph of international economic coordination — the moment at which the great powers proved they could manage the global monetary system jointly when bilateral pressures threatened it. There is some truth in this. The dollar had risen by sixty per cent on a trade-weighted basis between 1980 and early 1985, the United States current account deficit had blown out to three and a half per cent of GDP, and a Democratic House of Representatives under Dan Rostenkowski was preparing protectionist legislation — the Gephardt amendment, the Bentsen bill — that would have imposed import surcharges and broken the postwar trading order. Baker convened the Plaza meeting to head off Congress. He succeeded. The protectionist surge collapsed within six months. But the price of that success, paid not by the United States but by Japan, was the destruction of the Japanese economic model and the thirty-year deflationary depression that the Japanese now call ushinawareta sanjūnen — the lost three decades. The Plaza Accord did not cause this directly. What it caused was the policy response of the Bank of Japan to the yen's appreciation, and that response, taken in 1986 and 1987 to offset the deflationary shock of the strong yen, was the asset bubble whose collapse in 1990 ended Japanese growth as a structural fact.

To see this one has to understand what Japan in 1985 actually was. It was the second-largest economy in the world by nominal GDP. Its current account surplus had reached roughly four per cent of GDP, almost entirely against the United States. Its banks, freshly unshackled by the partial liberalisations of the early 1980s, were the largest in the world by total assets — by 1989, the top seven banks globally were all Japanese. Its industrial corporations — Toyota, Sony, Matsushita, Nippon Steel — were systematically outcompeting their American rivals in consumer electronics, automobiles, and steel. The Japanese model rested on three pillars: an undervalued exchange rate that subsidised exports, a financial system that channelled household savings into industrial investment at administered interest rates, and a corporate governance structure of cross-shareholdings and main-bank relationships that insulated managers from short-term capital markets. The first pillar was what Plaza was specifically designed to demolish.

Baker's negotiating position at Plaza was strong because the United States held two cards that the other four powers did not. The first was the threat of Congressional protectionism — Baker brought to the meeting copies of the Gephardt amendment and of the Bentsen trade bill and let his counterparts read them. The second was the willingness of the new Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker to allow the dollar to fall further than the other parties wanted, by holding United States interest rates below the level that would have defended the currency. Noboru Takeshita, the Japanese finance minister, agreed to the communiqué after a private conversation with Baker in which Baker reportedly told him that without Plaza there would be a thirty per cent across-the-board tariff on Japanese imports by spring 1986. Takeshita signed. He flew home expecting an appreciation of perhaps ten to fifteen per cent over a year. He got an appreciation of forty per cent in twelve months.

What happened next is the part of the story the Plaza commentariat has spent forty years not quite explaining. The Bank of Japan, faced with an exchange rate shock that threatened to collapse exports and plunge Japan into recession, did what its statutes obliged it to do: it eased monetary policy aggressively to offset the deflationary impact of the strong yen. The official discount rate was cut from five per cent in January 1986 to two and a half per cent by February 1987 — the lowest level in Japanese history. Bank lending grew by ten to twelve per cent per annum through 1987, 1988, and 1989. The Nikkei 225, which had stood at 13,000 on the day of Plaza, reached 38,915 on 29 December 1989. Tokyo land prices, on the official Ministry of Construction index, doubled in real terms between 1985 and 1990. At their peak, the grounds of the Imperial Palace in Tokyo were valued, by one widely circulated calculation, at more than the entire real estate of California. This was not the foreseen consequence of Plaza. It was the second-order consequence: the response by the Japanese authorities to a deflationary shock they had not signed up for, executed with the only instrument they possessed, and pursued past the point at which any reasonable central banker would have stopped.

The Bank of Japan governor through most of the bubble years was Satoshi Sumita, a former Ministry of Finance bureaucrat with no institutional independence and no political appetite for breaking the markets. His successor in December 1989, Yasushi Mieno, took office with the explicit mission of deflating the bubble. He raised the discount rate from three and a quarter per cent to six per cent in nine months, between May 1989 and August 1990. The Nikkei fell from 38,915 to 14,309 in twenty-one months. Tokyo land prices fell by approximately seventy per cent over the following decade. The Japanese banks, whose loan books had been collateralised against equity and real estate at peak valuations, became technically insolvent in 1992 and stayed insolvent — held up by regulatory forbearance and accounting fiction — until the Resona bailout of 2003. Through this entire period, Japanese GDP growth averaged below one per cent per annum, the population began its absolute decline in 2008, and the country that had been on track in 1985 to overtake the United States in nominal terms by 2010 became instead the world's first developed economy to experience structural deflation since the 1930s.

The counterfactual is worth pressing on. What if Plaza had never happened? The dollar would probably have begun to decline anyway by 1986 — the United States current account deficit was unsustainable and the markets were already turning — but the decline would have been slower, perhaps fifteen per cent against the yen over three years rather than fifty per cent. The Bank of Japan would not have needed to ease monetary policy to anything like the same degree. The Japanese asset bubble would have been smaller, perhaps half its actual size. The unwind of 1990 to 1993 would have been a normal cyclical downturn rather than a structural break. Japanese banks would not have been left with the cross-collateralised loan books that froze the financial system for a decade. The lost decade — the lost three decades — would not have happened in the form they took.

This is not a defence of the pre-Plaza dollar. The dollar's overvaluation in 1984 and early 1985 was a real problem and the United States current account deficit was a real constraint. But the specific instrument Baker chose — a coordinated G5 communiqué committing the central banks of the other major economies to engineer a dollar decline of unspecified magnitude — placed the entire burden of adjustment on the Japanese current account and forced the Bank of Japan into a monetary policy that no one in the room at the Plaza had explicitly endorsed and that the Bank of Japan itself had no institutional means to refuse. Baker got what he wanted. Congress dropped the protectionist legislation. The United States ran trade deficits with Japan for the next thirty-five years without ever again facing a serious tariff threat. The cost was paid by Japanese households whose savings were vaporised in the asset collapse, by Japanese workers whose wages stagnated for a generation, and by the Japanese state, which has run public debt above 200 per cent of GDP since 2010 without growth to inflate it away.

The deeper lesson of Plaza is one the international financial institutions have spent forty years declining to draw: the costs of currency adjustments under floating exchange rates fall asymmetrically on the country whose currency is appreciating, because that country must use monetary policy to offset the deflationary shock, and monetary policy at the scale required to offset a major currency move will, almost without exception, generate an asset bubble whose unwind is more damaging than the original adjustment would have been. This is not a Japanese pathology. The same dynamic destroyed the Nordic economies in 1992, the Asian tigers in 1997, and the European periphery in 2010. The Plaza Accord is the founding case of the modern theory of how coordinated currency interventions misallocate the costs of global imbalances onto the surplus countries' financial systems. No subsequent currency agreement — Louvre in 1987, the various G7 statements of the 1990s, the unspoken G20 understandings since 2008 — has solved this problem. The system continues to run on the assumption that surplus countries can absorb adjustment costs that deficit countries cannot, and to be repeatedly surprised when those costs detonate ten years later in a banking crisis nobody saw coming.

Baker's afternoon at the Plaza Hotel was a tactical success of the first order. He stopped a protectionist surge, he engineered the largest currency realignment in the history of floating exchange rates, and he did it in a single weekend with five signatures on a one-page communiqué. It was also one of the most consequential economic policy errors of the twentieth century, and the country that paid for it was not the one whose Treasury Secretary called the meeting.
