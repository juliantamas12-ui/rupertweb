History 18 June 2026

The Saturday Afternoon of 1 December 2001 in Buenos Aires When Domingo Cavallo Froze the Deposits and Killed the Convertibility Regime He Had Spent a Decade Defending

On the afternoon of Saturday, 1 December 2001, in a conference room on the fifth floor of the Ministry of Economy on Hipólito Yrigoyen in Buenos Aires, Domingo Cavallo signed Decree 1570/2001 limiting cash withdrawals from Argentine bank accounts to 250 pesos per week. The decree, which would be published in the Boletín Oficial the following Monday morning and which the Argentine public would within hours name el corralito — the little playpen — was presented to the press as an emergency measure to halt the bank run that had taken seven billion dollars out of the Argentine financial system in the preceding three weeks. It was the opposite. It was the moment at which Cavallo, the architect of the 1991 convertibility law that had pegged the peso one-to-one to the United States dollar and that he had spent the subsequent decade defending against every challenger from the left and the right, conceded that the regime could not be saved and chose the form of its destruction. The standard account treats the corralito as a defensive measure that failed and forced devaluation. The truth is that the corralito was the first act of devaluation. Cavallo signed it not to save convertibility but to control which Argentines would lose what, in the collapse he now knew was unavoidable.

The mechanics of the decision matter because they have been systematically misremembered. By the last week of November 2001, the Argentine banking system was hemorrhaging deposits at a rate of approximately seven hundred million dollars per day. The IMF had refused, on 22 November, to release the 1.26 billion dollar tranche that had been promised in September, citing non-compliance with the zero-deficit law. The Banco Central had lost reserves from 26 billion dollars in January to under 15 billion by the end of November, and the rate of loss was accelerating. The convertibility law required that every peso in circulation be backed by a dollar in the reserves. At the current rate of deposit flight, the reserves would be exhausted by the third week of December. Cavallo had three options on the table when he convened his economic team at the Ministry on the morning of 30 November. He could devalue the peso openly, breaking the convertibility law and ending the regime he had built. He could default on the external debt, preserving reserves but destroying access to international capital. Or he could freeze the deposits, ending the bank run by administrative fiat and buying himself two or three months of political space in which to negotiate. He chose the third option. He chose it because the first two would have ended his political career immediately, and the third allowed him to pretend, for another twenty-one days, that he was still defending the convertibility regime rather than dismantling it.

The cruelty of the corralito as designed has been described by its defenders as the unavoidable consequence of a banking crisis. The cruelty was, in fact, the point. Argentine middle-class households held the bulk of their savings in dollar-denominated accounts at the major private banks — Banco Galicia, Banco Río, BBVA Francés — under the protection of the 1995 deposit guarantee that had been extended after the tequila crisis. They had been told for a decade by every Argentine finance minister, by the IMF, by the Wall Street analysts who covered the country, and by Cavallo himself that the convertibility regime guaranteed the dollar value of their deposits. The corralito froze the accounts. The subsequent pesification — the forced conversion of dollar deposits into peso deposits at the official one-to-one rate, decreed by Eduardo Duhalde's government on 3 February 2002 after the peso had been allowed to float to 1.4 to the dollar — transferred wealth from those middle-class depositors to the indebted corporate sector by an amount the Argentine economist Mario Damill later estimated at 23 percent of GDP. The corralito did not save the savings. It froze them in place so that they could be confiscated more efficiently when the devaluation Cavallo knew was coming arrived three weeks later. The decree was a tourniquet applied to a limb that had already been condemned to amputation, by the surgeon who had decided which patients would survive the procedure and which would not.

The political collapse followed within nineteen days. The middle class which had supported Fernando de la Rúa's Alianza government in the October 1999 elections, which had tolerated the austerity measures of the Machinea and López Murphy ministries through 2000 and the first half of 2001, and which had accepted Cavallo's return to the Ministry of Economy in March 2001 as the last chance to preserve the savings denominated in dollars that the convertibility regime represented, discovered on the morning of Monday 3 December that those savings were no longer accessible. The first cacerolazos — saucepan protests — began that evening in the wealthier neighborhoods of Buenos Aires: Recoleta, Belgrano, Palermo. They were not, in their first iteration, organized by the Peronist opposition or by the piquetero movements of the impoverished provincial north. They were organized by the bank-deposit-holding bourgeoisie of the federal capital, the same demographic that had been the political base of the Alianza government, and they were directed at the government that demographic had elected. By 19 December the protests had spread to every major Argentine city. By 20 December, de la Rúa had resigned and was airlifted from the roof of the Casa Rosada by helicopter. By the end of the year, Argentina had had five presidents. The corralito did not cause the collapse of the de la Rúa government. The collapse was already inevitable by 30 November. The corralito determined the social composition of the protests that brought it down, and that composition — the middle class rather than the urban poor — determined the political character of the successor governments and, ultimately, the Kirchnerist coalition that would dominate Argentine politics for the following fifteen years.

The deeper question is what Cavallo could have done differently. The Argentine state in November 2001 was in a position that economists call a sudden stop: the foreign capital that had financed the current account deficit through the 1990s had ceased to flow, and the gap could not be closed without either fiscal contraction at a scale the political system would not bear or default on the external debt at a scale the financial system would not survive. The convertibility regime, which had stabilised the Argentine economy in 1991 by ending the hyperinflation of the Alfonsín years, had calcified by 2001 into a constraint that prevented the monetary and exchange-rate adjustments that the sudden stop required. Cavallo understood this. He had said so in private to his economic team since September. What he had not said in public, and what he could not say in public without precipitating the bank run he was trying to prevent, was that the convertibility regime had to end. The corralito was the bridge he constructed between the public position he was still defending and the private position he had already conceded. The bridge collapsed on 20 December because Cavallo had underestimated the speed at which the middle class which had benefitted from convertibility would turn against the government that had ended it. He had assumed the depositors would tolerate a freeze for the duration of the political negotiations. He had assumed wrong. The depositors had read the regime as a contract; the corralito was a breach of contract; the political consequence followed at the speed of an SMS message between Recoleta apartment blocks at 9 pm on a December evening.

There is a final dimension to the decision that has not been adequately incorporated into the historiography. Cavallo, in his memoirs and in the long oral history he gave to the Argentine journalist Eduardo van der Kooy in 2008, has consistently presented the corralito as the only available alternative to immediate banking collapse. This is true as a description of the binary he faced on 30 November. It is misleading as a description of the binary he had faced six months earlier. In May 2001, when Cavallo had returned to the Ministry, the reserves had stood at twenty-four billion dollars and the bank run had not yet begun. He had at that point a window of perhaps three months in which an orderly exit from convertibility, negotiated with the IMF and announced as a coordinated policy package rather than imposed as an emergency measure, would have been possible. He did not take the window because he believed, as he had believed since 1991, that the convertibility regime was savable. He was the last person in the Argentine economic establishment who believed it. The corralito was the price of his conviction. He paid the price with other people's savings, and the political class that had failed to overrule him in May paid the price with its credibility through December. The collapse of the Argentine political system in the last twenty days of 2001 was, at the level of policy, the consequence of one man's refusal to abandon a regime he had built, and at the level of mechanism, the consequence of one decree he signed on a Saturday afternoon to buy himself three more weeks.

The convertibility regime had lasted ten years and seven months. It had been built on the promise that the Argentine state would not, under any circumstances, debase the currency in which its citizens held their savings. The promise had been kept through three external shocks — the tequila crisis of 1995, the Asian crisis of 1997, the Russian and Brazilian crises of 1998–99 — and had become, by 2001, the central element of the contract between the Argentine middle class and the post-democratic political system. The corralito broke the contract. The political consequences of that breach have not, twenty-five years later, been fully absorbed by the Argentine system. The currency of trust that the convertibility regime had created was destroyed in the same decree that froze the deposits. Domingo Cavallo had spent a decade arguing that the Argentine state could be trusted with money. On the afternoon of 1 December 2001 he signed the decree that proved he was wrong. The proof was administered, deliberately, with his own signature, in the building from which he had administered the regime he was now ending. He resigned eighteen days later. He has never held public office since. The convertibility regime he built has not been re-established. The middle class he froze out of its savings has voted, in every election since, for the parties that promised to protect them from people like him.
