History 16 June 2026

The Night of 14–15 August 1947 at the Viceroy's House in Delhi When Mountbatten Brought Independence Forward by Ten Months and Made Partition Logistically Impossible to Carry Out

On the night of 14–15 August 1947, in the white sandstone palace that Edwin Lutyens had built on Raisina Hill to house the Viceroy of India, Lord Louis Mountbatten signed the instruments transferring sovereignty over the subcontinent to two new dominions whose borders nobody had yet been told. The line that would divide Punjab and Bengal between India and Pakistan had been drawn the previous week by Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a British barrister who had never set foot in India before his arrival on 8 July, working from outdated census maps in a bungalow on the Viceroy's estate. Mountbatten had received the completed Radcliffe Award on 13 August. He sealed it in an envelope and ordered that it not be published until 17 August — two days after independence. The two new states would come into existence not knowing where they ended. Within six weeks an estimated one to two million people would be dead and twelve to fifteen million displaced in the largest forced migration in human history. The standard account treats the violence of Partition as a tragedy of communal hatreds the British could not contain. The truth is that the violence was the predictable consequence of a single administrative decision Mountbatten took in his first month in office: to bring the date of British withdrawal forward from June 1948 to August 1947, and to do so without telling the Indian political class until the calendar was already set.

Mountbatten arrived in Delhi as the last Viceroy on 22 March 1947 with a mandate from the Attlee cabinet to transfer power by June 1948. He decided within six weeks that the date was wrong. The reasons he gave, both at the time and in his later self-justifications to Alan Campbell-Johnson and to the historian Philip Ziegler, were a mixture of the operational and the theatrical. The communal violence in Punjab and Bengal was worsening; the Muslim League and the Congress had reached deadlock in the interim government; the British administration was haemorrhaging officers as the Indian Civil Service collapsed. All of this was true. None of it was a reason to compress the timetable by ten months. The ten months were the operational margin built into the June 1948 date by the British military planners who had spent two years calculating what a partition would require. They had concluded — in papers Mountbatten read and dismissed — that a workable transfer needed boundary commissions to deliver their awards a minimum of three months before independence, that the boundary force required to police the line needed to be in position six weeks before, and that the population exchange, if it could not be prevented, had to be supervised by a still-intact British command structure with units deployed forward. Mountbatten's compression of the timetable rendered each of these conditions impossible to meet.

The decision was taken in a single meeting on 2 June 1947 at the Viceroy's House, when Mountbatten told Nehru, Jinnah, Patel, Baldev Singh, and Liaquat Ali Khan that he had chosen 15 August as the date of transfer. He had cleared the date with London the previous week without consulting any of the men in the room. The date itself was personal: 15 August was the second anniversary of the Japanese surrender at Tokyo Bay, where Mountbatten as Supreme Allied Commander South-East Asia Command had taken the Japanese capitulation in Singapore in September 1945. He chose the anniversary of his own war for the end of British India. Nehru accepted because the Congress had been waiting twenty-eight years for the transfer of power and was not going to refuse it over a calendar question. Jinnah accepted because the Muslim League had been waiting seven years for Pakistan and was not going to refuse it for the same reason. Neither man had the technical staff to evaluate what the compression would do to the boundary work. Mountbatten did. He had the Indian Army general staff and the Punjab Boundary Force planners in the next room. He overruled them.

The mechanics of what followed are usually treated as a separate story from the timetable decision. They were not. Radcliffe was appointed on 30 June. He arrived in India on 8 July. He was given five weeks to draw a line through two provinces containing eighty-eight million people, on the basis of district-level census data from 1941 that did not record the fine grain of religious distribution within tehsils and villages. He had no Indian assistant of his own choosing — both boundary commissions were composed of two Congress and two League nominees, who deadlocked on every contested district, leaving Radcliffe as the casting vote in every case. The maps he worked from were inaccurate on the location of canals, on the boundaries between Sikh and Muslim landholdings in central Punjab, and on the demographic composition of the Calcutta hinterland. He finished his awards on 12 August. Mountbatten then sat on them for five days.

The reason for the suppression has been debated ever since. Mountbatten's later account — that he wanted to avoid spoiling the independence celebrations — is the version his biographers have generally accepted. The contemporary documents tell a different story. V.P. Menon, the constitutional adviser to the Viceroy, wrote in his minutes of 13 August that Mountbatten was concerned that publication of the award before transfer would expose him personally to demands for revision from both Nehru and Jinnah, who would still be his political principals until midnight on 14 August. The award gave the Muslim-majority tehsils of Gurdaspur to India — providing the only land route from India to Kashmir, a fact whose strategic consequences are still playing out — and gave the Chittagong Hill Tracts, a 97 per cent Buddhist district, to Pakistan. Either decision could have been challenged by the affected party while the British were still in legal authority. By delaying publication until 17 August, Mountbatten ensured that the line would be announced to two sovereign states that no longer had any constitutional recourse against the British government that had drawn it. He protected his own position at the cost of the only mechanism by which the most contested boundary in twentieth-century history could have been adjusted before it was enforced on the ground.

The consequence was that on the morning of 15 August 1947, the inhabitants of Punjab did not know which country they were in. The Sikh population of Lahore, the Muslim population of Amritsar, the Hindu refugees already streaming east out of Rawalpindi — all of them were waiting for the announcement of a line that, when it came, would tell hundreds of thousands of them that they had two days to leave their homes. The Punjab Boundary Force, the joint Indian-Pakistani military unit Mountbatten had created to police the transition, had 23,000 troops to cover a population of twenty-eight million across an area the size of England. Its commander, Major-General Pete Rees, had told Auchinleck on 8 August that he needed three times that number to maintain order. He had been told that no more were available because the compressed timetable had not allowed for the redeployment. By 1 September 1947 the force had been disbanded as ineffective and the killing was being conducted, on both sides of the line, by the regular armies of India and Pakistan against each other's civilians.

The counterfactual is sharper than the standard literature allows. If Mountbatten had held to the June 1948 date, the boundary commissions would have completed their work in February or March 1948, the awards would have been published with three to four months of British constitutional authority remaining for the adjustment of the most egregious anomalies, the boundary force would have had time to deploy at full strength along the line, and the transfer of populations — which had become inevitable once the boundary was drawn — would have been supervised rather than improvised. The estimate by the historian Yasmin Khan, in The Great Partition (2007), is that a properly resourced and politically protected transition might have reduced the death toll by an order of magnitude. The figure of one to two million is itself contested — Christopher Bayly and Tim Harper have argued for a lower estimate of half a million — but the order of magnitude is not in serious dispute, and neither is the proposition that the compressed timetable was the single largest cause of the body count.

The deeper question is why Mountbatten did it. The answer the documents support is the one his admirers find hardest to accept: he wanted the credit for ending the British Empire in India and he wanted it before the Conservative Party returned to power in London. Attlee's Labour government was not, in the summer of 1947, in obvious electoral trouble — that would come with the convertibility crisis in August — but Mountbatten had grown up in a political family and understood that the consensus on Indian independence was Labour's project, that Churchill and the Conservative right were still hostile, and that a transfer of power completed before any change of government in London would be politically irreversible. By advancing the date by ten months he made the transfer a fait accompli before either British politics or Indian opposition could reopen the question. He achieved the result he wanted. The price was paid by the people of Punjab and Bengal, who did not know they were paying it.

The lesson the historians have generally drawn from Partition — that the British departure was too late, too rushed, too cynical, too imperial in its final administrative act — collapses on inspection the three propositions into one. Only the second is true. The departure was rushed because a single man decided in the first month of his Viceroyalty to compress a transition timetable that had been carefully calibrated by people who knew more than he did about what it would require, and he did so for reasons that were, at root, about his own political position rather than the welfare of the population he was governing. The British did not lose control of Partition. They handed it over to two new states without the operational margin required to manage it, in the full knowledge of what that margin had been calculated to provide. The million dead are not a footnote to the end of the Raj. They are the cost, paid in advance and in full, of Lord Louis Mountbatten's calendar.
