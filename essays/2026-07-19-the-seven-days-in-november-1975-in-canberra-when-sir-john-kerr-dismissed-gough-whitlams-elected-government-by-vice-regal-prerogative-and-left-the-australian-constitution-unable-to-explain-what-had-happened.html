History 19 July 2026

The Seven Days in November 1975 in Canberra When Sir John Kerr Dismissed Gough Whitlam's Elected Government by Vice-Regal Prerogative and Left the Australian Constitution Unable to Explain What Had Happened

At 13:15 on Tuesday, 11 November 1975, in the study of Government House on the north shore of Lake Burley Griffin, the Governor-General of Australia, Sir John Kerr, handed the Prime Minister of Australia, Edward Gough Whitlam, a two-page letter that dismissed him from office. Whitlam had walked into the room expecting to advise the Governor-General to call a half-Senate election as a way out of a budget deadlock. He walked out having ceased to be Prime Minister. Within ninety minutes, the leader of the Liberal opposition, Malcolm Fraser — who had been waiting in an anteroom of Government House since 13:10 without Whitlam's knowledge — had been commissioned as caretaker Prime Minister. Within three hours, Fraser had secured the passage of the appropriation bills that Whitlam had been unable to pass. Within four hours, Kerr had prorogued Parliament and issued writs for a double dissolution. The elected government of a Westminster democracy had been removed from office by a viceroy exercising a prerogative that most Australian constitutional lawyers had believed, until that afternoon, was theoretically available but practically unusable. Fifty-one years later, Australia has never satisfactorily explained what happened, and the constitution has never been amended to prevent it from happening again.

What is worth understanding about the Dismissal is not that it happened but that Kerr took seven days to decide to do it and told nobody who mattered during those seven days that he was thinking about it. Between 4 November and 11 November 1975, the Governor-General of Australia conducted a series of private consultations — with the Chief Justice of the High Court, Sir Garfield Barwick; with the former Chief Justice, Sir Owen Dixon's successor Sir Anthony Mason (who has confirmed his participation only in 2012); with retired judges; with the Palace, through the Queen's Private Secretary, Sir Martin Charteris — while continuing to accept Whitlam's advice as though nothing were being contemplated. He did not warn Whitlam that his tenure was at risk. He did not tell Whitlam that he considered the constitutional crisis to have reached the point at which the reserve powers might be used. He specifically declined to tell Whitlam because he had concluded — on advice from Barwick — that a warned Prime Minister would advise the Queen to dismiss the Governor-General first, and that the reserve powers could only be exercised by ambush. The Dismissal was, in other words, a coup by a viceroy against his own First Minister, executed by deliberate deception, and constitutionally defended on the ground that the deception was necessary to preserve the constitution.

The underlying crisis was real. Whitlam had been elected in December 1972 on the biggest reforming programme in Australian history — universal healthcare, university fees abolished, the vote extended to eighteen-year-olds, conscription ended, diplomatic recognition of the People's Republic of China — and had governed with the confidence of a leader convinced that his mandate was moral rather than merely numerical. The 1974 double dissolution had returned him with a reduced majority in the House of Representatives and continuing minority status in the Senate, where the coalition had, through a combination of state government appointments to casual vacancies that departed from convention, secured a majority it had not won at the polls. On 16 October 1975, Fraser announced that the coalition would use its Senate numbers to deny supply — that is, to block the appropriation bills funding the operations of government — until Whitlam called a general election. Supply-blocking of this kind had no precedent in Westminster practice. In every previous Westminster democracy the upper house had accepted, either by convention or by statute, that it could not deny the government the money to function. Fraser was asserting that the Senate could, and that the Governor-General should intervene if the Prime Minister refused to hold an election in the face of the blockade.

Whitlam's response was that supply would run out in late November, that he would find ways to pay the public service in the interim (through banking arrangements that involved private guarantees from the Commonwealth Bank), and that the constitutional crisis was Fraser's problem rather than his. His confidence was based on a simple political calculation: coalition senators from safe Liberal states would eventually break ranks under electoral pressure, supply would pass, and Fraser would be humiliated. The calculation was reasonable. In the final week before the Dismissal there was persuasive evidence that at least two coalition senators — Reg Withers is the most-cited example, though the evidence is contested — were preparing to cross the floor. Whitlam needed to hold his nerve for perhaps seventy-two hours. If he had, the Dismissal would never have happened, because the trigger for the reserve power — an inability of the government to obtain supply — would have disappeared.

Kerr's decision to strike on 11 November rather than wait was therefore the operative act, and the operative act was based on a legal opinion that had been solicited in secret from a Chief Justice who should not have given it. Sir Garfield Barwick, who had been a Liberal politician before his appointment to the bench, provided Kerr on 10 November with a written opinion that (a) the Governor-General possessed reserve powers to dismiss a Prime Minister who could not obtain supply, and (b) these powers could be exercised without warning. The opinion was solicited by Kerr through channels that bypassed the normal law-officer route — the Solicitor-General, Maurice Byers, would have advised against the Dismissal and had done so orally — and it was solicited from a Chief Justice sitting on a court that could be asked, potentially within days, to rule on the constitutional validity of the very action the opinion authorised. Barwick's willingness to provide the opinion, and Anthony Mason's separate advisory role (which Mason denied for thirty-seven years and confirmed only in 2012), converted the High Court from a possible arbiter of the crisis into a participant in it. The Dismissal was thus not merely a viceroy's action but a coordinated action by a viceroy and two future Chief Justices, executed against a sitting Prime Minister who was denied the opportunity to seek his own legal advice, and executed with a speed that made judicial review of the initial dismissal effectively impossible.

The Palace knew. This is the fact that Australian constitutional discourse spent forty-five years denying and that the release of the Palace Letters on 14 July 2020 established beyond dispute. The correspondence between Kerr and Sir Martin Charteris, released after a Federal Court decision that overturned National Archives claims of "personal" status, shows that Kerr had been discussing the possibility of dismissal with the Palace since at least September 1975, that Charteris had provided him with detailed advice on the reserve powers throughout October and early November, and that the Palace's position — carefully hedged, always deniable, formally neutral — was in substance that Kerr possessed the powers he ultimately exercised. Queen Elizabeth II did not authorise the Dismissal. She was not asked. But her Private Secretary provided the Governor-General with encouragement of the kind that a viceroy in a state of constitutional anxiety understands as permission. The convention that the sovereign is above politics — that the Palace does not participate in the internal constitutional crises of realms other than its own — was breached in 1975, and the breach was concealed for forty-five years by a National Archives classification that had no statutory basis.

The election that followed the Dismissal, on 13 December 1975, returned Fraser with the largest majority in Australian federal history. The electorate was asked to choose between a Prime Minister who had been dismissed for refusing to hold an election and an opposition leader who had produced the crisis by refusing to pass supply, and it chose the opposition leader by fifty-five seats to thirty-six. The verdict has been misread ever since as a ratification of the Dismissal. It was not. The Whitlam government had exhausted its political credit through three years of administrative chaos, ministerial scandals (the Loans Affair, the sacking of ministers, the Khemlani debacle), and an economic downturn that would have destroyed any incumbent government in 1975 or 1976. The Dismissal shortened the timeline. It did not create the outcome. If Whitlam had held on until March 1976 and gone to the country in normal course, he would have lost by a similar margin, and the constitutional crisis would have been resolved by the ordinary political mechanism of an election. Kerr's action removed the ordinary mechanism and substituted a viceregal one, and Australia has spent five decades treating the ratification of the substitution as evidence of its legitimacy.

The constitutional consequences are that the reserve powers of the Australian Crown remain, in 2026, exactly what they were on 10 November 1975 — undefined, uncodified, and available in circumstances that no document specifies. The 1999 republic referendum, which would have converted the Governor-General into an elected or parliamentary-appointed President, was defeated by a coalition of monarchists and republicans-who-wanted-a-different-model, in a plebiscite whose complexity Prime Minister John Howard had specifically designed to produce that outcome. The Australian constitution therefore continues to license, in law, an action that Australian political culture treats as illegitimate in fact. The gap between the two is filled by a convention — that reserve powers will not be used again — that is only as strong as the next Governor-General's willingness to observe it. Kerr's replacement, Sir Zelman Cowen, restored the office's credibility through the studious avoidance of anything resembling political initiative. His successors have all followed the same rule. But the rule is a personal one. It binds nothing. The Dismissal is not a historical event that Australia has resolved. It is a constitutional door that Australia has agreed not to open, and the agreement lasts only as long as the agreement.

Kerr himself resigned as Governor-General in December 1977, having become unable to appear in public without being booed. He accepted an appointment as Australian Ambassador to UNESCO that Fraser withdrew after public protest, retreated to a small flat in London, drank himself into disgrace at a Melbourne Cup ceremony in 1977 that produced the defining photograph of the Dismissal era (Kerr slurring on a podium, tie askew, the object of national contempt), and died in Sydney in 1991. Whitlam lived until 2014, was rehabilitated as the great reforming Prime Minister of Australian modernity, and gave his final political speech in 2010 at a Labor rally, at the age of ninety-four, urging the party to remember that "the government that has been elected must be allowed to govern." He never used Kerr's name. He never needed to. The audience knew.

The lesson of the Dismissal is that constitutional monarchies are held together by conventions that dissolve on contact with a viceroy willing to disregard them. The Australian Governor-General's reserve powers had been in continuous existence for seventy-four years before Kerr used them, without being used, because the office had been held by a series of men who had understood their role to be ornamental. Kerr understood his role to be constitutional, and the moment a viceroy understands his role to be constitutional, the constitution becomes whatever the viceroy says it is. The prevention of a repeat is not a matter of law. It is a matter of the character of the next Kerr, whom Australia has no institutional means of identifying in advance and no legal means of restraining once identified. The Dismissal did not resolve a constitutional crisis. It manufactured a constitutional silence, and the silence is Australia's answer, fifty-one years later, to the question of whether the reserve powers exist. They do. They have not been used again. Nobody knows why.
