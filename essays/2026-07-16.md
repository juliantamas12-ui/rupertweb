# The Three Weeks in April 1945 When Heinrich Himmler Attempted to Surrender Germany to the Western Allies Through a Swedish Count and Discovered That the War Had Already Passed Him By

Heinrich Himmler — Reichsführer-SS, chief of the German police, minister of the interior, commander of the Replacement Army, plenipotentiary for the strengthening of Germandom, and, from July 1944, the second most powerful human being in the Third Reich — spent the last three weeks of April 1945 attempting to negotiate the surrender of Germany to the Western Allies over the head of Adolf Hitler, and discovered, in the small hours of 28 April at a police station in Lübeck, that the war he thought he was ending had ended without him three days earlier in a Reims schoolroom, that the American president he thought he was negotiating with had been dead for two weeks, and that the peace he thought he was purchasing was worth exactly what a war criminal's signature is worth on a document nobody had authorised him to sign. He would be dead by his own hand three weeks after that, on 23 May, in a British interrogation cell in Lüneburg, after biting through a cyanide capsule that a sergeant-major had failed to find during a routine strip search. The Bernadotte affair — the negotiations between Himmler and the Swedish Count Folke Bernadotte, vice-president of the Swedish Red Cross, that took place at the Swedish consulate in Lübeck and at an SS field hospital at Hohenlychen between 19 February and 24 April 1945 — is one of the most instructive episodes of the collapse of the Third Reich, because it establishes with unusual precision what the leadership of the Nazi state actually believed about its own position at the moment of maximum crisis, and how comprehensively that belief diverged from the reality that the Western Allies had already imposed on it.

**The standard narrative treats the Himmler-Bernadotte negotiations as a footnote in the German collapse — a doomed and slightly comical attempt by a mass murderer to purchase respectability at the last moment. This is a misreading.** The negotiations were not comic and they were not doomed by any structural feature of the diplomatic situation. They were doomed by two specific decisions taken by the Western Allies: the Casablanca declaration of unconditional surrender in January 1943, and the ratification of that declaration by the incoming Truman administration in the days immediately after Franklin Roosevelt's death on 12 April 1945. Himmler had rational grounds to believe, in the winter of 1944-45, that the Western Allies would accept a separate peace that would allow the Wehrmacht to continue fighting the Red Army in the east. He was wrong about this — but he was not stupidly wrong. His error was a specific failure to understand a specific decision by a specific American administration, and the trajectory of the negotiations reveals, better than almost any other episode, the precise geometry of the Grand Alliance in the last weeks of the European war.

Himmler's calculation, which he had been developing privately since the failed assassination attempt of 20 July 1944, rested on three assumptions. The first was that the Western Allies feared Soviet domination of central Europe more than they feared a rump Nazi Germany, and that this fear would grow as the Red Army approached the Elbe. The second was that the German army in the east, if properly reinforced from a stabilised western front, could still hold the Vistula line long enough to permit a diplomatic settlement. The third was that Roosevelt, whom Himmler regarded as a sick man politically dependent on his advisers, could be persuaded to abandon Stalin under the right circumstances by intermediaries who possessed sufficient standing in neutral capitals. All three assumptions were wrong, and Himmler had access to information that would have disabused him of each of them, but the belief structure of the SS command in 1944-45 was hermetic and self-reinforcing in a way that made empirical correction almost impossible. He continued to believe, until the third week of April 1945, that a separate peace was purchasable.

The instrument he selected for this purchase was Count Folke Bernadotte af Wisborg, the nephew of King Gustav V of Sweden and the vice-president of the Swedish Red Cross. Bernadotte had entered the German picture through a humanitarian operation — the White Buses mission, launched in February 1945 to evacuate Scandinavian prisoners from German concentration camps to internment in Sweden. The mission required negotiation with Himmler, who alone had the authority to release the prisoners, and Bernadotte travelled to Berlin four times between 19 February and 21 April to conduct these negotiations personally. **The White Buses evacuated approximately 15,000 people — Norwegians, Danes, and a substantial number of Jewish prisoners in the final weeks — from Neuengamme, Ravensbrück, and Theresienstadt, and constitute one of the most successful humanitarian operations of the war.** But their broader diplomatic significance is that they placed Bernadotte in repeated personal contact with Himmler, who came to regard the Swedish count as a trustworthy intermediary through whom a larger negotiation could be pursued.

The larger negotiation crystallised at a meeting on 12 February 1945 at the SS field hospital at Hohenlychen, sixty miles north of Berlin, where Himmler was recovering from an intestinal complaint under the care of his personal physician Karl Gebhardt. Himmler, through his masseur and confidant Felix Kersten — a Finnish-born practitioner who had established himself as indispensable to Himmler's chronic stomach cramps and had used this position to extract concessions from him on Jewish matters throughout 1944 — proposed that Bernadotte carry a message to General Dwight Eisenhower proposing the surrender of German forces on the western front while the war continued in the east. Bernadotte, who had been briefed by the Swedish foreign minister Christian Günther and who understood perfectly that the proposal violated the Casablanca declaration, agreed to carry the message on the condition that Himmler continue to release Scandinavian prisoners in the interim. This was the deal: Himmler traded 15,000 concentration camp prisoners for a Swedish diplomatic channel to Eisenhower. The channel, when it was finally used in earnest on the night of 23-24 April 1945 at the Swedish consulate in Lübeck, would produce nothing. But between February and April, it kept Himmler engaged in an activity that saved lives — the last such activity in which he would ever engage.

**The critical meeting occurred on the night of 23-24 April 1945 in the cellar of the Swedish consulate in Lübeck.** Berlin was surrounded by the Red Army. Hitler was in the Führerbunker preparing his testament and his suicide. Himmler had left Berlin two days earlier and had established a temporary headquarters at Hohenlychen from which he could access Bernadotte. The two men met in Lübeck under Allied bombing — the Royal Air Force conducted a raid on the city that night that forced them into the consulate cellar — and Himmler, in the presence of Kersten and the SS-Brigadeführer Walter Schellenberg, made his final proposal. He would surrender all German forces on the western front to Eisenhower, unconditionally, immediately. He would continue to fight the Red Army in the east. He would replace Hitler as head of the German state on the assumption — which he communicated to Bernadotte in the same conversation — that Hitler would be dead within days. He asked Bernadotte to communicate this proposal to the Swedish government, which would transmit it to the Western Allies through its representatives in Washington and London.

Bernadotte transmitted the proposal. The response was crushing. Christian Günther conveyed the proposal to the American minister in Stockholm, Herschel Johnson, and the British minister, Victor Mallet, on the afternoon of 24 April. Johnson cabled Washington immediately. Mallet cabled London. The response from both capitals was identical and immediate: the offer was to be rejected, and the rejection was to be conveyed to Moscow first. **On 25 April 1945, President Harry Truman — who had been president for thirteen days — personally telephoned Winston Churchill to agree that Stalin should be informed of the Himmler approach before any formal reply was made. On 26 April, Churchill sent Stalin a personal telegram summarising Himmler's proposal and rejecting it. Stalin's response, sent within hours, thanked Churchill for the loyalty of the notification and confirmed that any German surrender would be to all three Allied powers simultaneously.** The Himmler proposal thus produced not a Western breach with the Soviet Union but a small reinforcement of Allied unity at a moment when the collapse of the alliance was already visible in other files. The chain of decisions from Casablanca in January 1943 through Yalta in February 1945 to the Truman-Churchill exchange of 25-26 April 1945 had produced a diplomatic instrument — unconditional surrender to all three powers — that no German intermediary, regardless of standing or credibility, could break.

The news reached Himmler on the morning of 28 April, at a temporary headquarters he had established in a schoolhouse near Plön in Schleswig-Holstein. He received simultaneously the Reuters cable summarising the Allied rejection and — through the news services that his subordinates were monitoring — the announcement that Hitler had learned of his negotiations from a BBC broadcast the previous evening. Hitler had responded, in the bunker, by ordering Himmler's arrest, stripping him of all offices, and dictating a testament in which Grand Admiral Karl Dönitz — not Himmler, not Göring — was named as his successor as head of state. **The two central assumptions of Himmler's diplomacy — that the Western Allies would break with the Soviet Union, and that he would succeed Hitler — collapsed on the same morning within four hours of each other.** He spent the next thirty-six hours in a state that his subordinates described as vacillating between rage and paralysis. On 30 April, Hitler shot himself. On 1 May, Dönitz — now head of state — refused Himmler's request to be named foreign minister of the new government and instructed him to leave the government's temporary seat at Flensburg. On 5 May, Dönitz formally dismissed Himmler from all remaining offices. On 6 May, Himmler shaved his moustache, put on a sergeant's uniform of the field gendarmerie, wrapped an eye patch over his left eye, obtained forged papers in the name of Heinrich Hitzinger, and began walking south with a small group of former SS officers.

The masquerade lasted seventeen days. On 21 May, at a British checkpoint at Bremervörde, "Hitzinger" was arrested for possessing suspicious papers — a British intelligence circular had specifically flagged discharge documents from the Secret Field Police as high-priority screens because it was known that senior SS personnel had been issued such papers. He was moved to a British interrogation centre at Barnstedt, where on the morning of 23 May he removed the eye patch, replaced his uniform with civilian clothes, and identified himself to the camp commandant Captain Thomas Selvester as Heinrich Himmler. Selvester, initially incredulous, verified the identification against Foreign Office photographs and immediately notified the Second Army headquarters at Lüneburg. Himmler was moved to Lüneburg the same afternoon for detailed interrogation. He was strip-searched by Sergeant-Major Edwin Austin — an experienced NCO who had conducted such searches on hundreds of prisoners — and Austin found and confiscated a small brass cylinder that he correctly identified as a cyanide capsule. Austin did not, however, examine Himmler's mouth. **At 22:45 on 23 May 1945, during a further examination by an army doctor, Himmler bit through a second cyanide capsule that he had been holding under his tongue and died in approximately fifteen minutes on the floor of the interrogation room.** His body was buried in an unmarked grave on Lüneburg Heath by three British sergeants — the exact location was recorded on a map that was subsequently lost — and his death mask was destroyed at the order of the Second Army's commanding general to prevent the emergence of a shrine.

**The historical interest of the Bernadotte affair is not that it failed — it was structurally certain to fail from the moment the Casablanca declaration was made — but that it reveals the precise operational content of the Grand Alliance in its last functioning weeks.** The Truman-Churchill-Stalin exchange of 25-26 April was one of the last acts of the alliance in which all three parties behaved with complete good faith toward one another. By June, Stalin was manufacturing pretexts to keep American observers out of the Soviet zone in Germany; by July, at Potsdam, the alliance was visibly disintegrating; by March 1946, Churchill would deliver the Iron Curtain speech at Fulton. The rejection of Himmler's proposal was the last moment at which the Casablanca doctrine functioned as an operating principle rather than a slogan. Twelve months later, and possibly less, an approach through a Swedish intermediary with a proposal to accept German surrender in the west while the Soviet Union was excluded would have found a hearing in Washington and London. Himmler was six months too late. His personal deficiencies — the vanity, the doctrinal narrowness, the belief that his own cadaverous piety would somehow purify his record — obscured a diplomatic proposal that was, in its structural logic, six months ahead of its acceptability. If the war had ended in October 1945 rather than May 1945, the Bernadotte channel might have produced something. It did not, because the war ended in May, and the Truman administration was two weeks old, and the last echo of Roosevelt's Grand Alliance had not yet been drowned out by the first sounds of the Cold War.

There is one further point worth registering. **Bernadotte himself was assassinated three years later — on 17 September 1948, in West Jerusalem, by a Lehi hit squad that included the future Israeli prime minister Yitzhak Shamir among its senior planners — while serving as the United Nations mediator for the first Arab-Israeli war.** The Lehi assassins objected to Bernadotte's proposed partition plan, which they regarded as insufficiently favourable to the emerging state of Israel. The historical trajectory that took Bernadotte from negotiating with Himmler in 1945 to being shot by Zionist irregulars in 1948 is one of the sharpest ironies produced by the century — the humanitarian who saved 15,000 concentration camp prisoners, including several thousand Jews, from the SS was killed three years later by Jewish nationalists who found his diplomacy insufficiently ambitious for their cause. His killers were never prosecuted. The Swedish government's protest note was ignored. Shamir served as prime minister of Israel from 1983 to 1984 and again from 1986 to 1992, and the file on the Bernadotte assassination remained sealed in Israel until 1988.

The Bernadotte affair thus produces two historical lessons that are unusually difficult to reconcile. **The first is that unconditional surrender was a diplomatic instrument of extraordinary power, and its application in April 1945 preserved the Grand Alliance long enough to complete the destruction of the Third Reich as a coherent political entity.** Without it, the Western Allies would have been tempted, and might have succumbed, to the strategic logic that Himmler was offering. The doctrine — enunciated by Roosevelt at Casablanca in January 1943 partly on the advice of his father's old friend Admiral William Leahy and partly under the pressure of a specific political calculation about Congressional attitudes — held together the alliance at the moment when the alliance's underlying interests had already diverged. **The second lesson is that the men who negotiate the surrender of empires are rarely the men who deserve the moral credit for the negotiation.** Bernadotte, the humanitarian, was killed by nationalists who thought him insufficiently partisan. Himmler, the mass murderer, spent the last weeks of his life doing more objective good than in any previous period of his career, by keeping the White Buses running through a country that was collapsing around them. History does not distribute its moral awards according to the intentions of the actors. It distributes them according to the outcomes their actions produce, and by that criterion the six weeks between February and April 1945 in which Himmler and Bernadotte kept talking to each other — while millions were being murdered in the camps that Himmler still commanded — were the six weeks in which more lives were saved than in any comparable period of the war.

The larger geometry is this. The Second World War in Europe did not end in Berlin. It ended in a schoolroom in Reims on the morning of 7 May 1945 and, more formally, in a hangar at Karlshorst in Berlin on the night of 8-9 May. But it could have ended differently — earlier, on partial terms, with the war continuing in the east — and did not, because in the last three weeks of April 1945 the Truman administration and the Churchill government made the specific decision not to permit it to end that way. **That decision was the last act of the alliance that won the war. Everything that followed — from the sealing of the German surrender to the beginning of the Cold War — was the working out of a peace that Roosevelt had designed and Truman had ratified in the two weeks after Roosevelt's death.** The Bernadotte affair is the point at which that design was tested, and held. Himmler died in a British interrogation room three weeks later. Bernadotte died in a Jerusalem street three years later. Their negotiation is one of the small doors in twentieth-century history that opened onto a world that did not come to pass, and closed before anyone had time to notice what had almost happened inside it.
