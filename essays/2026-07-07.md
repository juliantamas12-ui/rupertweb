# The Four Nights in October 1907 in J.P. Morgan's Library on Madison Avenue When One Banker Rescued the American Financial System and Made the Federal Reserve Inevitable by Doing So

At 21:00 on Saturday, 19 October 1907, John Pierpont Morgan boarded a private railway car at Richmond, Virginia, where he had spent the week attending the triennial convention of the Protestant Episcopal Church as a lay delegate, and directed the conductor to make no scheduled stops until the train reached Jersey City. He had been reading a telegram from George Perkins, his junior partner at 23 Wall Street, which reported that the Knickerbocker Trust Company — the third-largest trust in New York, with deposits of approximately sixty-two million dollars and forty branches — was expected to close its doors on Monday morning as a consequence of its president Charles Barney's speculative involvement in the copper corner of F. Augustus Heinze, which had collapsed the previous Wednesday. Morgan arrived at his brownstone at 219 Madison Avenue at 09:30 on Sunday morning, held a two-hour meeting with Perkins and Benjamin Strong of Bankers Trust, and instructed Strong to conduct an emergency audit of the Knickerbocker's books between 12:00 and 18:00 that Sunday. Strong's report, delivered at 20:00 in Morgan's study on the second floor of the library building next door at 33 East 36th Street, concluded that Knickerbocker was insolvent and could not be rescued without exposing the rescuers to unacceptable losses. Morgan accepted the report and authorised the Knickerbocker to fail on Monday morning. He then spent the next four nights in his library — Sunday 20 October through Thursday 24 October 1907 — running the largest financial rescue operation in American history from a single room, without government authority, without a central bank, and without any legal framework that permitted what he was doing. He succeeded. **The consequence of his success was the National Monetary Commission of 1908, the Aldrich Plan of 1910, the Jekyll Island conference of November 1910, and the Federal Reserve Act of 23 December 1913. The American central bank exists because J.P. Morgan proved, over ninety-six hours in October 1907, that a private banker acting alone could stabilise the currency of a continental economy — and because the American political class concluded, correctly, that this was intolerable.**

The standard historiography treats the Panic of 1907 as the emergency that revealed the need for a central bank. It is more accurate to say that Morgan's rescue was itself the emergency, and the Federal Reserve was the political system's response to the discovery that a single private citizen possessed powers that ought to belong to the state. The panic itself had killed the Knickerbocker on Tuesday 22 October, drained approximately seventy-five million dollars from the New York clearinghouse banks by Wednesday, and by Thursday morning had reached the Trust Company of America, which held deposits of sixty-four million dollars and was the second-largest trust in the city. Morgan received the audit of Trust Company of America at 03:00 on Thursday 24 October from Benjamin Strong, who had been examining the books through the night. The audit concluded that Trust Company of America was solvent but illiquid — that its assets exceeded its liabilities but that it could not meet a run without external assistance. Morgan read the report, said "This is the place to stop the trouble, then", and summoned the presidents of the fourteen largest trust companies in New York to his library at 12:00 that day. He locked them in the East Room and refused to release them until they had committed twenty-five million dollars in mutual support, which they did at approximately 04:45 on the morning of Friday 25 October, by which point Morgan had also secured a twenty-five-million-dollar deposit from Treasury Secretary George Cortelyou (delivered in person to New York on Wednesday), a ten-million-dollar contribution from John D. Rockefeller (offered on Thursday), and a fifteen-million-dollar underwriting commitment from a syndicate of the six largest national banks. **The total was seventy-five million dollars in emergency liquidity, assembled in ninety-six hours, negotiated by a single seventy-year-old banker with severe rhinophyma and a hacking cough, working from a private library with a Renaissance ceiling and a Verrocchio bust of Lorenzo il Magnifico watching from the alcove. There was no legal authority for any of it. There was no legal prohibition of it either. This was the problem.**

The specific mechanism by which Morgan's authority operated is worth reconstructing, because it explains why the rescue was politically insupportable even though it was financially successful. Morgan possessed no formal power to compel any bank or trust to participate in the rescue. He possessed, instead, an informal power that derived from four structural facts: first, that his firm was the clearing agent for the sterling settlements of every major American railroad, which meant that any bank he chose to exclude from Morgan-cleared transactions would lose access to foreign exchange within a week; second, that his personal control of the boards of directors of United States Steel, International Harvester, and thirty-six other major corporations gave him the ability to move corporate deposits between banks with a telephone call; third, that his relationship with the Bank of England through his London partner Edward Grenfell allowed him to secure gold shipments from London to New York on twenty-four hours' notice, a capacity that no other American possessed; and fourth, that his personal reputation on Wall Street was such that his public declaration of confidence in an institution was itself a source of liquidity, because it caused depositors to redirect their withdrawals elsewhere. These four powers, operating together, allowed Morgan to conduct what was in every functional respect the operation of a central bank without occupying any public office. He set the terms on which the rescue would proceed, selected the institutions to be saved and those to be allowed to fail, dictated the interest rates at which the rescue funds would be lent, and imposed personnel changes on the rescued institutions as conditions of the assistance. **The Trust Company of America, the Lincoln Trust, and the Westinghouse Electric Company were saved. The Knickerbocker, the Consolidated National Bank, and the Mercantile National Bank were allowed to fail. The decisions were taken in a library on Madison Avenue by one man who was accountable to no one, and the country accepted his decisions because there was no alternative decision-maker available.**

The moment at which the rescue crystallised into a permanent political problem was not the end of the panic in November but the specific transaction of Sunday 3 November 1907, when Morgan authorised United States Steel to acquire the Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company in a share swap that eliminated a major competitor and required the personal assent of Theodore Roosevelt to proceed without antitrust prosecution. Morgan sent his junior partner Elbert Gary and Henry Clay Frick to Washington on the overnight train on Sunday evening; they arrived at the White House at 09:00 on Monday morning, presented the case for the acquisition as necessary to save the brokerage house of Moore and Schley (which held Tennessee Coal shares as collateral and would fail without the acquisition), and obtained Roosevelt's agreement in twenty minutes over breakfast. Roosevelt announced at 12:30 that he had "no reason to disapprove" the transaction. The Standard Oil dissolution suit was then in its second year, and Roosevelt was the president who had built his reputation on antitrust prosecution; his acquiescence in a Morgan-directed monopolisation of the steel industry, granted under the pressure of financial emergency, established the principle that private financial power could suspend the operation of public antitrust law when the private financial power judged it necessary. **This was the moment at which the political class of the American Republic understood the actual meaning of what had happened in October 1907. It was not that Morgan had saved the country. It was that the country had been saved by a man who then extracted, as the price of the rescue, a monopoly acquisition that the President was politically unable to refuse. The Federal Reserve was not designed to prevent bank panics. It was designed to prevent J.P. Morgan.**

The National Monetary Commission that Nelson Aldrich chaired between 1908 and 1912, and the Jekyll Island conference of November 1910 at which the operational plan for a central bank was drafted by Aldrich, Paul Warburg, Henry Davison, Frank Vanderlip, Piatt Andrew, and Benjamin Strong, are conventionally described as responses to the panic of 1907. They were not. They were responses to the rescue of 1907. The panic itself had been contained without government intervention; there was no functional problem that required a solution. What required a solution was the political fact that the containment had been achieved by a private mechanism whose continued availability could not be relied upon (Morgan was seventy in 1907 and died in 1913) and whose political costs — the effective transfer of antitrust discretion to a private banker, the concentration of decision-making authority in a single library — could not be repeated. The Aldrich Plan of January 1911, which became with modifications the Federal Reserve Act of December 1913, was a mechanism for reproducing Morgan's rescue capacity in a public institution that would be accountable, at least nominally, to political authority. **Its structure — twelve regional Reserve Banks, a Federal Reserve Board in Washington, a discount rate mechanism, an emergency lending facility, and a formal relationship with foreign central banks — was in every essential respect a public reconstruction of the private apparatus that Morgan had operated from his library and his firm's Wall Street offices. Benjamin Strong, who had audited the Knickerbocker for Morgan on 20 October 1907, became the first Governor of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York in 1914 and ran it until his death in 1928, applying, in the public form, the techniques he had learned in the private form seven years earlier. The continuity of personnel between the private rescue of 1907 and the public system of 1914 was not accidental. The Federal Reserve was, in significant measure, the Morgan rescue operation converted into a permanent government institution.**

The counterfactual that occupies American financial historiography — whether a central bank established earlier would have prevented the Panic of 1907 — is misconceived because it treats the panic as the problem the Federal Reserve was designed to solve. The panic was not the problem. Every industrial economy in the nineteenth century experienced periodic banking panics, and the American panics of 1873, 1884, 1893, and 1907 were not qualitatively different from the British panics of 1866 (which the Bank of England contained under Bagehot's principles) or the French panics of 1882 and 1889 (contained by the Banque de France). The problem specific to the United States in 1907 was not the panic itself but the identity of the rescuer, and the political intolerability of the fact that the rescuer had extracted a monopoly acquisition as his fee. **The Federal Reserve exists because in one specific week in October 1907, an American president was compelled by financial necessity to authorise a monopoly acquisition that violated his own antitrust programme, and the American political system concluded that no president should ever again be placed in that position. The public central bank was created not because the private mechanism had failed, but because it had succeeded on terms that could not be politically repeated. This is the paradox of the 1907 rescue: it demonstrated the necessity of the institution that was then created to make its own methods unnecessary.**

The larger implication for the analysis of financial regulation is that regulatory institutions are frequently created not to solve technical problems but to displace private actors whose successful solutions have generated intolerable political dependencies. The Securities and Exchange Commission was created in 1934 not because the 1929 crash had been unusually severe by nineteenth-century standards, but because the informal apparatus of pool operations, syndicate manipulations, and private disclosure norms that had governed American securities markets since the 1870s had produced a distribution of losses in 1929-1932 that concentrated wealth destruction on retail investors in a manner that the political system could no longer tolerate. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation was created in 1933 not because bank runs were a new phenomenon, but because the pattern of correspondent-bank rescues that had contained earlier runs had broken down in 1930-1932, requiring the substitution of a public guarantee for a private network. In each case, the technical function of the institution was subordinate to its political function, which was the extraction of a critical stabilising role from private hands and its lodgement in a public framework accountable, however imperfectly, to democratic authority. **The Federal Reserve is the archetype of this pattern, and J.P. Morgan is the essential figure in its creation, not because he opposed a central bank (he did not) but because his personal success in October 1907 was so complete, and so publicly visible, that it made the private alternative to a central bank politically untenable. He rescued the American financial system, and in rescuing it, made himself its last private rescuer. The library on Madison Avenue, which now operates as the Morgan Library and Museum, is the physical location at which the American state acquired, in a mode that historians have consistently misdescribed, one of its most consequential twentieth-century institutions. The Federal Reserve was built there, on four consecutive nights in October 1907, by the man whom the institution was subsequently designed to render obsolete.**
