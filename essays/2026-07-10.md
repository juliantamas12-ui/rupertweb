# The Nine Days in October and November 1962 When Mao Zedong Fought the Sino-Indian War to Discipline Nikita Khrushchev and Used Jawaharlal Nehru's Army as the Instrument of Instruction

At 07:00 on Saturday, 20 October 1962, the People's Liberation Army launched simultaneous offensives across a two-thousand-mile front stretching from Ladakh in the west to the North-East Frontier Agency in the east, advancing into positions held by the Indian Army with a speed and coordination that startled every military attaché in Delhi. In the Tawang sector, Chinese forces under General Zhang Guohua broke the Indian 7th Brigade in under seventy-two hours and drove it back forty kilometres to the Se La ridge; in the Chip Chap Valley in Aksai Chin, Chinese columns overran the Indian forward posts in a single morning. On 24 October, Beijing offered a ceasefire that Nehru rejected. On 14 November, the PLA resumed offensive operations. Between 18 and 20 November, Chinese forces broke the Se La position, killed or captured most of the Indian 4th Division, and advanced sixty kilometres south to the edge of the Assam plain, placing forward units within sight of the tea plantations near Tezpur and provoking a partial evacuation of the town by the Indian civil administration. Then, on 21 November, without warning, without negotiation, and without any Indian military recovery having occurred, the People's Republic of China unilaterally declared a ceasefire, ordered its troops to withdraw twenty kilometres behind the pre-war line of actual control, and repatriated its prisoners of war with their equipment. In every military metric that ordinarily determines whether a war has been won or lost — territory held, forces destroyed, strategic objectives achieved — the 1962 Sino-Indian War was a Chinese victory of a completeness that had no precedent in modern Asian warfare. And in every metric that ordinarily determines whether a state exploits a victory, China did the opposite of what a victor does. It surrendered its territorial gains. It returned its prisoners. It withdrew behind a line it had already crossed. **The standard interpretation, which treats the war as an Indian defeat over the border question and the ceasefire as a Chinese gesture of restraint or logistical necessity, mistakes both the object and the audience of the operation. Mao Zedong did not fight the 1962 war against India. He fought it against the Soviet Union, using the Indian Army as the instrument by which a specific ideological argument would be made to Nikita Khrushchev, and he terminated it at the precise moment when the argument had been made and further military operations would have blurred its clarity. The war was a piece of applied Marxist-Leninist theatre, staged for one man in one Kremlin office, and it succeeded so completely that it broke the Sino-Soviet alliance six months later at the Moscow talks of July 1963 and set the terms of the Sino-Soviet split that would define world communism for the next twenty-seven years.**

To see the war this way, one has to understand what Mao believed the Soviet Union had become by 1962, and why the belief was, from his perspective, intolerable. The Cuban Missile Crisis had concluded on 28 October 1962 — eight days into the Sino-Indian war — with Khrushchev's withdrawal of Soviet missiles from Cuba in exchange for an American pledge not to invade the island and a secret undertaking to remove Jupiter missiles from Turkey. To Kennedy and to Western observers this looked like a Soviet capitulation salvaged by clever diplomacy; to Mao, whose reading of the crisis was contained in a series of Politburo memoranda that were partially reproduced in the 1965 polemical exchange *A Comment on the Statement of the Communist Party of the U.S.A.*, it was proof of a systemic ideological corruption. A genuinely revolutionary power, in Mao's framework, would have escalated rather than retreated: the correlation of forces favoured the socialist camp in the long run, the risk of nuclear war was overstated by bourgeois analysts because imperialism was a paper tiger, and Khrushchev's decision to trade Cuban missiles for American assurances constituted "capitulationism" — the specific ideological error by which a socialist state abandoned its international revolutionary duty for the comfort of great-power accommodation. Mao had been warning about Khrushchev's capitulationism since 1956, when the secret speech had denounced Stalin and initiated the doctrine of "peaceful coexistence." By 1959, when Khrushchev refused to share nuclear weapons technology with China and withdrew Soviet advisors from Chinese nuclear facilities, Mao had concluded that the Soviet Union was no longer a revolutionary state at all. And by 1960, when the split became public at the Bucharest conference of communist parties, Mao had begun searching for a mechanism by which the ideological argument could be made in a form that neither Khrushchev nor the assembled parties of the international communist movement could evade. The mechanism could not be a speech. It could not be a document. It had to be an event — a demonstration that a revolutionary state acted in the world in a way that a capitulationist state did not, and that the difference between the two was visible on the ground. **India was chosen as the vehicle for the demonstration not because the border dispute was serious but because India was the country that Khrushchev had spent the previous six years cultivating as the Soviet Union's principal non-aligned partner in Asia, and because attacking India would force Khrushchev into a choice that had no acceptable answer.** If Khrushchev backed India against China, he would confirm that the Soviet Union prioritised its bourgeois-nationalist client over its socialist ally, thereby proving Mao's thesis about ideological corruption. If Khrushchev backed China against India, he would destroy his own Asian diplomacy and admit that peaceful coexistence with the non-communist world was subordinate to socialist solidarity, thereby retreating from the position he had staked out since 1956. Either answer served Mao. The war existed to force the question.

The evidence that this was Mao's strategic framework, rather than a border dispute that spiralled out of control, is dense and, in the post-2000 declassifications, essentially conclusive. Chinese military planning for the war began in earnest in June 1962, six weeks *before* the Indian "Forward Policy" of establishing new border posts had reached its most provocative stage. The Central Military Commission meeting of 6 October 1962, whose minutes were partially released in the 2000s as part of the compilation *Zhou Enlai junshi wenxuan*, records Mao stating that "we must fight a battle that will bring thirty years of peace" — a formulation that already assumed the strategic outcome would be a border stabilisation rather than a territorial gain, and that identified the temporal horizon of the operation as the international-political rather than the military. On 8 October, in a meeting with Marshal Liu Bocheng, Mao specified that the operation should be "sharp, brief, and unmistakable" — three adjectives that describe a signal rather than a war of conquest. And on 18 October, two days before the offensive began, the Chinese ambassador in Moscow, Liu Xiao, was instructed to inform the Soviet Foreign Ministry of the impending operation with a formulation that has been reconstructed from Soviet archival sources and Liu's later memoir: China intended to conduct a limited operation against India to defend its territorial integrity, and it expected the Soviet Union, "as a socialist ally," to support the Chinese position or at minimum to observe neutrality. This was not information-sharing. It was a demand for a commitment, delivered at a moment when Khrushchev was fully absorbed by the developing Cuban crisis and could not afford a simultaneous confrontation with Beijing. The timing was not coincidence. Mao had chosen the week in which Khrushchev was least able to think clearly about Chinese motives, and he had chosen it precisely because Khrushchev's incapacity in that week would be visible to every subsequent observer as the moment at which the Soviet Union had failed to perform the duties of socialist alliance.

Khrushchev's response constituted the successful execution of Mao's trap in real time. On 25 October — with Soviet ships turning back from the American quarantine line in the Caribbean and the Soviet leadership meeting more or less continuously in emergency session — *Pravda* published an editorial that praised the Chinese ceasefire offer and criticised Indian obduracy, effectively siding with Beijing on the border question. Twelve days later, after the Cuban crisis had ended, *Pravda* published a second editorial that reversed the first: the Soviet Union now regretted the fighting on both sides, called for a return to negotiations on the basis of the McMahon Line (which China rejected as a colonial imposition), and reaffirmed Soviet commitment to Indian economic development. Within three weeks, the Soviet Union had resumed the delivery of MiG-21 fighter aircraft to India that had been suspended during the October crisis, and Khrushchev had personally reassured Nehru through diplomatic channels that Soviet support for the Indian position on the border would continue notwithstanding the ideological complications of the Chinese case. **From Mao's perspective, this sequence was the demonstration. The Soviet Union had, when forced to choose, chosen its bourgeois-nationalist client over its socialist ally. The choice had been made under conditions of extreme visibility — the Cuban crisis had focused the attention of every communist party in the world on Moscow's behaviour, and every communist party had watched Moscow support socialist China in one editorial and non-aligned India in the next.** The rhetorical position of "peaceful coexistence with all countries regardless of social system," which Khrushchev had defended since 1956, had produced in practice the specific outcome of Soviet arms transfers to a country then at war with a socialist state. This was, in the Chinese formulation that would be codified in the 1963 polemics, "modern revisionism" — the transformation of proletarian internationalism into ordinary great-power diplomacy — and the 1962 war had exposed it in a form that no communist party could ignore.

The ceasefire of 21 November is the operational move that clinches the interpretation. If the war had been about the border, no rational commander would have withdrawn from positions already taken and from an Indian army that had ceased to exist as a coherent fighting force. If it had been about defeating India, the correct move on 20 November was to continue the advance to the Brahmaputra and dictate terms from Tezpur. If it had been about logistical exhaustion, the Chinese would have consolidated on the Se La-Bomdila line and negotiated from strength. The unilateral withdrawal to positions behind the pre-war line, executed within seven days of the ceasefire and involving the physical repatriation of captured Indian equipment, was operationally unnecessary and militarily counterproductive. It served exactly one purpose: it demonstrated that China had not fought the war for territory. If the war was not about territory, the question of what it was about became unavoidable, and the answer that the Chinese propaganda apparatus supplied — in Chinese, in Russian, in English, and in every language of the international communist movement between December 1962 and July 1963 — was that the war had been about proletarian internationalism, that China had defended the socialist camp against imperialist provocation while the Soviet Union had betrayed it, and that the difference between a revolutionary and a revisionist party was now visible to any communist willing to look. **The Chinese "victory" in the war was, in this sense, less important than the Chinese "restraint" after it. The restraint was the evidence that the war had never been for the object that a normal war would be for, and the anomaly of the restraint was the mechanism by which the ideological interpretation was forced onto the international communist movement as the only intelligible explanation of what had happened.** The 3,000-odd Chinese soldiers who died in the war were, like the 26,000 who would die on the Vietnamese border seventeen years later under Deng Xiaoping's very different strategic calculation, expenditure in the technical sense — a cost paid to produce a signal that could not be produced in any other currency.

The consequences unfolded on schedule. In February and March 1963, the Chinese Communist Party published *A Proposal Concerning the General Line of the International Communist Movement* — the twenty-five point document that constituted the first formal Chinese ideological indictment of the Soviet Union and that named "capitulationism" and "modern revisionism" as the two specific errors that the Cuban and Indian episodes had exposed. At the Moscow bilateral talks of July 1963 between Deng Xiaoping and Mikhail Suslov, the Chinese delegation refused every proposed formula for reconciliation and insisted that the ideological differences were "questions of principle" rather than "questions of detail" — a formulation that in Leninist rhetorical grammar signifies that no compromise is possible. On 20 July 1963, the talks collapsed. The Sino-Soviet alliance, formally in effect since 1950 and progressively hollowed since 1958, ceased to exist as an operative political relationship. In September 1963, *People's Daily* began publishing what would become the "Nine Comments on the Open Letter of the CPSU Central Committee" — a systematic Chinese ideological demolition of Soviet foreign and domestic policy that was distributed in over a hundred countries and that became the founding text of the Maoist tendency in the international communist movement, splitting communist parties from Albania to Indonesia to Peru along the fault line that the 1962 war had exposed. Within seven years, the Sino-Soviet border itself was in armed conflict at Zhenbao Island. Within ten years, the People's Republic of China had established diplomatic relations with the United States, largely on the basis that Chinese hostility to the Soviet Union was ideological and structural rather than tactical — a claim whose credibility rested on precisely the pattern of behaviour that the 1962 war had inaugurated.

The interpretation matters not because the war has been misunderstood in Western scholarship — Neville Maxwell's *India's China War* (1970) gestured toward it, and the post-2000 work of Chen Jian, Sergey Radchenko, and John Garver has established most of the archival basis — but because it changes what one thinks the Sino-Soviet split was. In the Western literature, the split is usually described as a consequence of accumulated ideological disagreements that eventually became too great for the alliance to bear: a bottom-up process in which doctrinal differences gradually corroded a political relationship. This description is not wrong but it is passive; it makes the split into something that happened to Mao and Khrushchev rather than something that either of them chose. **What the 1962 war shows is that the split was not corrosion but engineered fracture — that Mao Zedong deliberately constructed the situation in which the Soviet Union was forced to choose between its Asian diplomatic architecture and its socialist alliance, and that he constructed it because he understood that Khrushchev's choice, whichever way it went, would rupture the alliance in a form that could then be exploited to reorganise the international communist movement around Chinese leadership.** The 1962 war was not the cause of the Sino-Soviet split. It was the instrument by which the split was made irreversible, and it was designed for that purpose from the beginning. Nehru's army, the disputed McMahon Line, the border posts of the Forward Policy — these were the stage furniture. The play was about Khrushchev, and the audience was every communist party in the world.

There is a certain kind of political intelligence that treats the world as a set of interlocking games and understands that the outcome of any given game is less important than what the game reveals to observers about the players. Mao Zedong possessed this intelligence to a degree that has rarely been matched in the modern history of statecraft, and his application of it in October and November 1962 belongs on any short list of the most successful strategic operations of the twentieth century — successful precisely because the operation's real object was invisible to the participants who could have prevented it and remained invisible for decades afterwards to the historians who tried to explain it. Nehru died in 1964 believing he had been the victim of Chinese aggression, which he had been but not primarily. Khrushchev fell from power in October 1964 partly as a consequence of the ideological damage that the war had inflicted on his international standing, damage he had never fully understood because he had never understood that the war had been aimed at him. And Mao spent the next fourteen years, until his death in September 1976, in a position of ideological primacy within the international communist movement that no Soviet leader would ever recover — a primacy that had been purchased in nine days of fighting in the Himalayas with the Indian Army as the unwitting instrument of an argument that had never really involved India at all.
