The Weekend at Camp David from 13 to 15 August 1971 When Nixon Closed the Gold Window to Win a Domestic Political Argument and Ended Bretton Woods as a Side Effect

On the evening of Friday, 13 August 1971, fifteen men flew by helicopter from the South Lawn of the White House to Camp David in the Catoctin Mountains of Maryland, where they would spend the next forty-eight hours drafting the announcement that Richard Nixon would deliver from the Oval Office at 9:00 p.m. on Sunday, 15 August, pre-empting the network broadcast of Bonanza on NBC. The announcement, which Nixon would name the New Economic Policy in deliberate echo of Lenin, comprised three elements: a ninety-day freeze on wages and prices, a ten per cent surcharge on imports, and the suspension — described as temporary, in fact permanent — of the convertibility of the United States dollar into gold at the official rate of thirty-five dollars an ounce that had governed the international monetary system since the Bretton Woods conference of July 1944. The standard account, which has been repeated in every American economic-history textbook since Allen Matusow's 1998 study and which underlies the conventional narrative of the end of the postwar monetary order, treats the closing of the gold window as the technically unavoidable response to a balance-of-payments crisis that had been gathering since 1965 and had become acute in the first week of August 1971. The truth is that the gold window was closed because Nixon's Treasury Secretary, John Connally — a Texas Democrat who had been Lyndon Johnson's protégé, had survived the bullet that killed Kennedy in Dallas in November 1963, and had been recruited by Nixon in December 1970 to manage the domestic politics of the 1972 re-election campaign — needed a dramatic break with the existing system in order to seize control of American economic policy from the Federal Reserve, the Council of Economic Advisers, and the State Department, and to establish himself as the indispensable man of the second Nixon term. The end of Bretton Woods was not the goal of the Camp David weekend. It was the instrument by which Connally won an internal Republican argument about who would run American economic policy in 1972. The international monetary system was the collateral damage.

The mechanics of the weekend have been documented in three contemporaneous diaries — those of H. R. Haldeman, the White House chief of staff; Arthur Burns, the chairman of the Federal Reserve; and Paul Volcker, the Under-Secretary of the Treasury for Monetary Affairs — and in the official memoranda declassified between 1996 and 2010 and now held at the Nixon Presidential Library in Yorba Linda. They establish a sequence that the standard account has systematically obscured. The balance-of-payments crisis of early August 1971 was real but it was not the crisis that produced the announcement. The triggering event was a memorandum delivered to the Treasury on Monday, 9 August by the British Embassy in Washington, requesting the conversion of three billion dollars of Bank of England reserves into gold at the official rate. The memorandum, which was treated by the State Department as a routine administrative matter and by Connally's staff as a provocation, would in fact have reduced the United States gold stock from ten billion dollars to seven billion, a level that the Treasury had identified in March 1971 as the threshold below which formal suspension would become necessary within months. The British request, in other words, accelerated by perhaps six months a decision that was already inevitable on the existing trajectory. What Connally did between Monday, 9 August and Friday, 13 August was not to decide that the gold window should be closed. He decided that the gold window should be closed by him, at Camp David, in a weekend retreat from which the State Department and the Council of Economic Advisers would be excluded, and that the announcement would be packaged with the wage-price freeze and the import surcharge in a manner designed to make the closing of the window appear as the third element of an integrated economic strategy rather than as the technical adjustment it actually was. The packaging was political. The substance was reactive.

The internal opposition to the package, which has been minimised in the official history, was substantial and was concentrated in the two institutions Connally had decided to exclude. Arthur Burns, the Federal Reserve chairman, opposed the closing of the gold window for the conventional central banker's reason that the suspension of convertibility would, by removing the external discipline on American monetary policy, accelerate the domestic inflation that Burns had been attempting to contain since taking office in February 1970. Burns argued through the weekend, in three separate confrontations with Connally and one with Nixon directly on Saturday afternoon, that the British request could be met by a one-time gold sale, that the longer-term adjustment should be negotiated multilaterally through the International Monetary Fund, and that the unilateral suspension would destroy the system of international cooperation that the United States had built and led since 1944. Burns lost every argument. The reason he lost was not that his analysis was wrong — it was, in fact, vindicated by the inflation of 1973 to 1981 that ended only with Volcker's chairmanship and the recession of 1981–82 — but that Connally had persuaded Nixon, in a series of private conversations during July 1971, that the international economic system was a constraint imposed on American sovereignty by a class of European and Japanese bureaucrats who had grown comfortable with American subsidy and were no longer willing to bear their share of the costs of the Cold War. The argument that won at Camp David was not an economic argument. It was a sovereigntist political argument framed as economic necessity, and it was made by a Texas Democrat to a California Republican about a system built by New England internationalists. The closing of the gold window was, in domestic political terms, an act of revenge by the Sun Belt against the Eastern Establishment, carried out in the name of the working man whose wages the same announcement froze for ninety days.

The international consequences unfolded on a timetable that nobody at Camp David had anticipated. The European central banks, which had been informed of the announcement at 6:00 p.m. on Sunday, 15 August by emergency cable through their Washington embassies, suspended trading in their own currencies on Monday morning. The London gold market remained closed for a week. The Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971, which Connally negotiated personally and which devalued the dollar by 7.89 per cent against gold and revalued the European currencies and the yen by varying margins, was treated by the United States Treasury as a settlement of the post-Bretton Woods question and by every other party as a temporary expedient pending the next crisis. The next crisis arrived in February 1973, when speculative pressure forced a second devaluation, and the float that had been described in August 1971 as a temporary technical measure became the permanent regime of the international monetary system. The float was not designed at Camp David. The float was the system that emerged when no replacement for Bretton Woods could be negotiated, because the United States — having discovered in August 1971 that it could close the gold window without immediate catastrophic consequences — had lost the incentive to accept the constraints that any successor system would have required.

The personal trajectory of the Camp David protagonists is the final clue to what the weekend was about. Connally resigned from the Treasury in June 1972, ten months after the announcement, his political project — to position himself as Nixon's running mate or successor — having been frustrated by the Watergate break-in of that month and by Nixon's calculation that Connally's Democratic background made him unacceptable to the Republican base. Connally would switch parties in 1973, run for the Republican nomination in 1980, finish last in every primary he entered, and declare personal bankruptcy in 1987 after a failed Texas real-estate venture. Burns would serve out his term at the Federal Reserve until 1978 and would be replaced by William Miller, whose seventeen-month tenure produced the worst inflation in American postwar history and forced the appointment of Volcker in August 1979. Volcker, who had been the technical architect of the Camp David package and who had drafted the operational details of the gold-window closing while privately opposing the policy, would spend the subsequent forty years arguing in lectures and memoirs that the suspension of convertibility had been a mistake — not in its inevitability, which he conceded, but in the manner of its execution. The man who had drafted the announcement spent the rest of his career explaining what should have been done differently. The man who had imposed the policy died in obscurity in 1993, his political career having ended in the year of the policy's triumph. The man who had opposed the policy lived long enough to see his analysis vindicated by the inflation of the 1970s and ignored by the political class of the 1980s.

The deeper lesson of Camp David is what it reveals about the durability of postwar institutions. Bretton Woods had been the most carefully designed and the most successful international economic regime in history. It had survived the Korean War, the Suez crisis, the Berlin Wall, the Cuban Missile Crisis, the Vietnam escalation, and the French withdrawal from NATO's military command. It had survived twenty-seven years of accumulating American balance-of-payments deficits without a formal devaluation. It did not survive a single weekend at a Maryland mountain retreat at which a Texas politician decided to win a domestic political argument by breaking the international system that constrained him. The institutions that had been built between 1944 and 1971, with the contributions of Keynes and White and Harry Dexter and a generation of New Deal economists, were dismantled in forty-eight hours by men who did not, at the moment of dismantling, understand what they were destroying. The float that replaced them has now lasted fifty-five years — longer than Bretton Woods itself — and has survived crises that the gold-standard system could not have survived. Whether the float is, in the long historical reckoning, the better system is the question that has occupied international economists since 1973 and will not be resolved in our lifetimes. What is clear is that the system was not chosen. It emerged, by default, from the wreckage of a weekend that had been organised to settle a question of personal ambition within the Nixon White House. The international monetary order of the twenty-first century is the residue of John Connally's bid for the 1976 Republican presidential nomination.
