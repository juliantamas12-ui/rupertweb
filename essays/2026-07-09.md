# The Twenty-Nine Days in February and March 1979 When Deng Xiaoping Lost a War Against Vietnam on Purpose and Bought the Sino-American Alliance With the Corpses of His Own Soldiers

At 05:00 on Saturday, 17 February 1979, approximately 200,000 soldiers of the People's Liberation Army crossed the Sino-Vietnamese border along a 480-mile front stretching from the Gulf of Tonkin to the Laotian frontier, in what the Chinese state media described as a "self-defensive counterattack" against Vietnamese provocations and what every foreign observer immediately recognised as the largest Chinese military operation since the Korean War. The invading force, drawn primarily from the Guangzhou and Kunming military regions, was armed with equipment that was in most cases twenty years old, commanded by officers who in most cases had not seen combat since 1953, and instructed to advance approximately thirty kilometres into Vietnamese territory, seize the provincial capitals of Cao Bang, Lang Son, and Lao Cai, and withdraw. By 16 March, twenty-nine days after the invasion began, the entire Chinese force had returned to Chinese territory, having sustained approximately 26,000 killed and 37,000 wounded — casualties that in absolute terms exceeded the American losses in the whole of the Vietnam War compressed into four weeks — in exchange for the destruction of approximately three Vietnamese border towns and the killing of somewhere between 30,000 and 50,000 Vietnamese soldiers and civilians. The Vietnamese army, which had defeated the French in 1954 and the Americans in 1975, was not defeated in 1979. It was not even seriously engaged: the main Vietnamese formations, six of the country's best divisions, remained in Cambodia throughout the fighting, and the border was defended primarily by militia units and the Second Regional Army. In every military metric that ordinarily determines whether a war has been won or lost — territory held, forces destroyed, strategic objectives achieved — the 1979 Sino-Vietnamese War was a Chinese defeat, and it has been treated as such by virtually every Western military historian who has examined it. **This assessment is factually accurate and strategically illiterate. Deng Xiaoping did not fight the 1979 war to win it. He fought it to lose it in a specific way, in a specific location, at a specific moment, in front of a specific audience. The audience was the American national security establishment. The purpose was to demonstrate — through the willing sacrifice of tens of thousands of Chinese soldiers whom Deng could have kept alive by not invading — that the People's Republic of China was prepared to act as a strategic partner of the United States against the Soviet Union, and that Chinese resolve on this question was not rhetorical but expressible in blood. The war worked. The corpses bought Deng exactly what he wanted: the Sino-American strategic alignment that structured the last decade of the Cold War, the Chinese economic opening that the alignment made politically possible in Washington, and the four decades of American technology transfer, market access, and diplomatic patience that transformed the Chinese economy between 1979 and 2018. Twenty-six thousand dead Chinese soldiers is the price Deng Xiaoping paid for Reform and Opening. The war against Vietnam was the down payment on the Chinese miracle.**

The immediate context is often described as Vietnamese provocation, and there was Vietnamese provocation, but the causal sequence runs in the opposite direction from the one that Chinese state media presented and that most subsequent Western historiography has accepted. Vietnam had invaded Cambodia on 25 December 1978, overthrowing the Khmer Rouge regime of Pol Pot — a Chinese client — in a two-week campaign that ended with the fall of Phnom Penh on 7 January 1979. This was a real strategic setback for Beijing, and Deng did react to it. But the Chinese decision to invade Vietnam had been taken *before* the Vietnamese invasion of Cambodia, in a series of Politburo meetings between September and November 1978 whose minutes were partially declassified in the 2000s and which make the sequencing unambiguous. The Chinese invasion was planned in response to the Vietnamese signing of the Treaty of Friendship and Cooperation with the Soviet Union on 3 November 1978, which committed Moscow to defend Vietnam against Chinese attack and which Deng correctly interpreted as the completion of a Soviet strategic encirclement of China stretching from Mongolia through the Russian Far East to Vietnam and, if it succeeded, into Afghanistan (where the pro-Soviet PDPA had seized power in April 1978 and where Soviet troops would arrive in December 1979). The Cambodian question was the pretext. The Soviet encirclement was the problem. And the Chinese response to the problem was constrained by a specific asymmetry that Deng understood with unusual clarity: China could not fight the Soviet Union directly, because it would lose; China could not tolerate Soviet encirclement, because encirclement would over time convert every regional Chinese strategic problem into a Sino-Soviet strategic problem in which China would be at permanent disadvantage; and China could not break Soviet encirclement diplomatically without an external ally, because it had no leverage of its own. **The external ally could only be the United States. But the United States, in 1978, did not yet regard China as a serious strategic partner. It regarded China as a nuclear-armed developing country with a bizarre domestic politics whose alignment against Moscow was verbally useful but practically unproven. Deng needed to prove it. The proof had to be military, because military demonstrations are the only demonstrations that great powers take seriously, and it had to be against a Soviet ally, because a demonstration against anyone else would not signal what Deng needed to signal. Vietnam was the only Soviet ally against which China could plausibly conduct a limited war. So Deng invaded Vietnam.**

The evidence that Deng was using the war as a strategic signal to Washington rather than as an actual military operation against Hanoi is dense and, once one looks for it, almost embarrassingly explicit. Deng travelled to Washington on 29 January 1979 — nineteen days before the invasion — for the first state visit of a Chinese leader to the United States since the establishment of the People's Republic in 1949. In private meetings with President Jimmy Carter and National Security Advisor Zbigniew Brzezinski on 30 January and 31 January, Deng explicitly warned that China intended to "teach Vietnam a lesson" and requested American diplomatic support in the event that the Soviet Union responded. Brzezinski's memorandum of the conversation, declassified in 1999, records Deng as stating that the operation would be "limited in scope and time" and that its purpose was to demonstrate that "the Soviet Union cannot protect its allies." Carter privately warned Deng against the operation and publicly, on the record after Deng's departure, offered no support for it. But the operational significance of the visit was that Deng had told the Americans in advance what he intended to do, had described its strategic purpose accurately, and had returned to Beijing on 5 February with the knowledge that the American response would consist of formal disapproval and substantive tolerance. The Americans, in turn, had absorbed the fact that a Chinese leader was prepared to fly to Washington, tell them face to face that he was going to start a war against a Soviet ally, and then do it. This was not the behaviour of a country that regarded itself as a peripheral actor in the Cold War. It was the behaviour of a country that was applying for admission to the small club of powers whose military decisions the United States had to take seriously, and it worked immediately.

The military operation itself was designed with a level of self-imposed restraint that makes sense only under the strategic-signalling interpretation. The PLA did not use its air force, despite possessing roughly 400 combat aircraft based within range of Vietnamese targets. It did not attack Vietnamese ports or destroy the naval infrastructure at Haiphong that would have made a serious Vietnamese response to future Chinese pressure impossible. It did not target the bulk of Vietnamese forces in Cambodia, which could have been reached by amphibious operations that the PLA Navy was capable of conducting at reduced scale. It did not use its nuclear weapons even implicitly, in the sense that no Chinese nuclear force was placed on alert during the war. The invasion was, in the language of the military planners around Xu Shiyou (the Guangzhou region commander) and Yang Dezhi (the Kunming region commander), a "punitive expedition" — a technical term that specifies a limited operation whose purpose is to inflict damage sufficient to make a political point rather than to change the underlying balance of forces. The self-limitation was calibrated to Soviet capabilities: Deng understood that if he escalated the war seriously, the Soviet Union would be obliged by the November 1978 treaty to intervene in some form, and the intervention would trigger a Sino-Soviet crisis in which the American commitment to China would be tested before it existed. The war had to be small enough that Moscow could plausibly decline to respond and Washington could plausibly extend implicit support without either commitment becoming irrevocable. **The 26,000 Chinese dead were the consequence of this calibration. A larger operation would have produced more Chinese casualties in absolute terms but a lower casualty ratio, because the PLA would have used its air force, its heavy artillery, and its armoured divisions rather than sending unsupported infantry against prepared Vietnamese defences in mountainous terrain. Deng chose the operational profile that maximised Chinese casualties for a given territorial objective because the operational profile that maximised Chinese casualties also minimised the risk of Soviet intervention. The dead soldiers were, in the most precise sense of the term, expenditure — the resource that was being spent to purchase a strategic outcome that could not be purchased in any other currency. Chinese diplomatic historians in the 1980s, writing under Deng's direct patronage, described the war as "a great victory at some cost." The description reverses the actual relationship. It was a great cost at some victory. The victory was in Washington, not in Vietnam.**

The subsequent decade demonstrates the return on the investment with a clarity that borders on the vulgar. Within twelve months of the war's end, the United States and China signed the January 1980 memorandum of understanding on technology transfer that gave China access to American dual-use technologies previously restricted under COCOM. Within eighteen months, the CIA had established the joint SIGINT stations at Qitai and Korla in Xinjiang, which monitored Soviet missile tests from the Central Asian ranges and which represented the first significant intelligence-sharing arrangement between the two countries. Within two years, the Reagan administration — which had campaigned in 1980 on a platform of restoring diplomatic relations with Taiwan — had reversed course entirely, signed the August 1982 communiqué committing the United States to reducing arms sales to Taiwan, and initiated the arms sales to the PRC that included Sikorsky Black Hawk helicopters, avionics upgrades for the J-8 fighter, and the Peace Pearl artillery-locating radar programme. Within five years, the Chinese economy had received approximately $12 billion in American, Japanese, and European technology transfers, and the special economic zones at Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, and Xiamen — established in 1979 and 1980 — had begun the export-oriented industrialisation that would define the next thirty years. **None of this would have been possible without the strategic alignment that the 1979 war made credible, and the strategic alignment would not have been credible without the demonstration that the war provided. The Chinese economic miracle rests on a specific bargain that Deng struck in early 1979: that China would act as a counterweight to Soviet power in Asia, and that the United States would in return provide the technology, the market access, and the diplomatic tolerance that Chinese domestic development required. The war against Vietnam was not tangential to this bargain. It was the ratification of the bargain in the currency that Cold War statecraft understood, and the ratification was performed on the bodies of the 26,000 Chinese soldiers who did not need to die and whom Deng ordered into a war he had decided to lose.**

The moral question is worth stating precisely. Deng Xiaoping in early 1979 was in a position that no Chinese leader had occupied since the Qing dynasty and that few leaders of any country have ever occupied: he could see, with unusual clarity, the shape of the strategic decision he was making, and he could measure the price he was paying for it. He did not stumble into the Vietnam war. He planned it, executed it, and terminated it on schedule. The soldiers who died were not casualties of a miscalculation or of an operational failure. They were, in the technical vocabulary of grand strategy, a signal — a costly signal in the game-theoretic sense that its value to the receiver depended precisely on the fact that the sender could not have sent it cheaply. Deng understood the game theory intuitively, in the way that certain kinds of political intelligence understand the mathematics of decision without requiring the notation, and he applied it to human lives with an equanimity that ought to be shocking and that has been essentially unexamined in the Chinese historiographical tradition. The People's Republic has never conducted a full public accounting of the 1979 casualties. The war is commemorated in China, when it is commemorated at all, as a defensive counterattack against Vietnamese aggression; the strategic-signalling interpretation has never been officially endorsed and rarely appears in Chinese-language scholarship. **This is not because the interpretation is wrong. It is because the interpretation is correct, and its correctness is incompatible with the moral vocabulary that any modern state, including one governed by a Leninist party, is capable of applying to the deliberate expenditure of its own soldiers as a diplomatic instrument. Deng bought the Chinese economic miracle with 26,000 lives, and he judged the price to be reasonable. The historical record suggests that on this question, at least, his judgement was correct — that the alternative to the Vietnam war was not peace but a longer, deeper Sino-Soviet confrontation that would have consumed the reformist opening before it began, and that in the ledger of great power decision-making the twenty-nine days in February and March 1979 belong on the same page as the Louisiana Purchase, the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902, and the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact: a transaction in which a state acquired at moderate cost an asset of extraordinary and enduring strategic value. The moderate cost, in this case, was measured in Chinese soldiers. The extraordinary asset was the four decades of American acquiescence in the rise of China. It is not obvious that any Chinese leader could have obtained the second without paying the first. It is, however, obvious that Deng knew what he was doing, that he did it on purpose, and that the graves in the border cemeteries at Malipo and Pingxiang are, whatever else they may be, the concrete foundation on which the skyline of Shenzhen was subsequently built.**

The war matters now, in 2026, for reasons that were not obvious in 1979 and that only became visible as the Sino-American alignment that the war purchased began to dissolve after 2015. If the alignment was the product of a specific strategic transaction rather than an organic partnership, then its dissolution requires a different explanation than the one that has been fashionable in Washington: the alignment did not fail because China cheated, or because China changed, or because American policymakers were naive. It ended because the underlying strategic problem that had produced it — the Soviet threat to China — ceased to exist in December 1991, and the alignment was maintained for the subsequent twenty-four years by inertia, commercial interest, and the intellectual momentum of an entire policy generation that had been trained to think of China as the essential counterweight to a Soviet Union that no longer existed. **The 1979 war is the correct historical starting point for understanding the current Sino-American relationship because it establishes the terms of the original bargain in a form that both sides understood at the time and that both sides have since found it convenient to forget. The bargain was: Chinese strategic behaviour against the Soviet Union in exchange for American strategic patience with Chinese development. The Soviet Union is gone. The Chinese strategic behaviour that the bargain required is no longer being performed, because there is nothing left for it to be performed against. The American strategic patience is therefore being withdrawn. This is not a betrayal on either side. It is the natural termination of a contract that was fully performed by both parties and whose consideration has now been fully exhausted. The 26,000 Chinese soldiers who died in February and March 1979 delivered exactly what Deng promised the Americans they would deliver, and the Americans delivered exactly what Deng understood them to promise in return. Both sides got what they paid for. Neither side has anything left to owe the other. And the current confrontation, which is often described as a new Cold War, is more accurately described as what happens when the previous Cold War's transactions finally clear.**
