# The Afternoon of 13 March 1848 in Vienna When Klemens von Metternich Discovered That the Habsburg Police State He Had Built to Contain the French Revolution Had Been Ruling on a Bluff for Thirty-Three Years

On the morning of 13 March 1848, Klemens Wenzel Nepomuk Lothar, Prince von Metternich-Winneburg zu Beilstein, Chancellor of the Austrian Empire, Knight of the Order of the Golden Fleece, architect of the Congress of Vienna, and the principal engineer of the European counter-revolutionary order that had held since 1815, was seventy-four years old and confident. He had received, at breakfast, the intelligence reports from his Polizeihofstelle — the political police organisation he had built after 1815 and which had come to be regarded, correctly, as the most sophisticated instrument of state surveillance in continental Europe — and the reports indicated that the crowds gathering outside the Landhaus in the Herrengasse, where the Estates of Lower Austria were to meet that morning to petition the Emperor for constitutional reform, were composed largely of students and would disperse when the weather turned. The weather did not turn. The crowds did not disperse. By four o'clock in the afternoon, Metternich had resigned. By eleven o'clock that night, he was in a laundry cart, in the clothing of a female servant, being smuggled out of the Hofburg by a route that his own security architecture had made him the only person alive to know intimately, and he was on the road to Rothschild's house in Frankfurt and thence to exile in England. In eight hours the Habsburg counter-revolution collapsed. **What is remarkable is not that it collapsed. What is remarkable is that no one — Metternich least of all — had understood that it had been a bluff since 1815.**

The Metternich system, as it was called by its admirers and its enemies alike, had been constructed after Napoleon's defeat on the theory that the French Revolution had been produced by the collapse of legitimate authority under the pressure of ideas, and that the way to prevent a recurrence was to construct an authority whose legitimacy was self-evident and whose surveillance was total. The Congress of Vienna, over which Metternich presided between September 1814 and June 1815, restored the pre-revolutionary dynasties, redrew the map of Europe to eliminate the strategic vulnerabilities Napoleon had exploited, and produced the Concert of Europe — the mechanism by which the five great powers, Britain, Austria, Prussia, Russia, and (from 1818) France, would coordinate to suppress revolutionary movements wherever they arose. The Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 extended the system into the German Confederation. The Troppau Protocol of 1820 committed the eastern powers to armed intervention against revolutions in any member state. The system suppressed the Neapolitan revolution in 1821, the Piedmontese revolution in the same year, the Spanish liberals in 1823, and the German nationalist movements throughout the 1820s and 1830s. It endured, in modified form, the July Revolution in France in 1830 and the Belgian revolt of the same year, both of which Metternich failed to reverse but succeeded in containing. **By 1848 the Metternich system had been the operating principle of European politics for thirty-three years, and it had never been seriously challenged in the territory of the Austrian Empire itself.**

The reason it had never been challenged is that Metternich had constructed, in the Habsburg lands, an apparatus of preventive suppression whose effectiveness was measured not in the number of revolutionaries it arrested but in the number of revolutions that never occurred. The Polizeihofstelle read, by an internal estimate submitted to Metternich in 1846, approximately 40,000 items of private correspondence per year. Its network of informers reached into the universities, the coffee houses, the officer corps, the civil service, and the great houses of Vienna, Prague, and Budapest. Its censorship apparatus banned, between 1815 and 1848, an average of 900 books and periodicals per year and reviewed every printed word published in the Empire before publication. Its passport system prevented internal migration without official authorisation and made travel abroad — for anyone below the rank of the higher nobility — a matter of individual imperial permission. The system was expensive. It consumed, at its peak, approximately 6 per cent of the Empire's ordinary revenue. It was also, on any reasonable measure, effective: there had been, in thirty-three years, no serious revolutionary conspiracy within the Empire that had reached the operational stage, and the intellectual and political classes had accommodated themselves to a regime whose principal virtue, from their perspective, was that it was predictable.

**What no one had understood — and what the events of 13 March 1848 revealed — was that the effectiveness of the system was a function of the belief that it was effective, and that the belief was maintained not by the apparatus itself but by the memory of what the apparatus had done in the years immediately after Waterloo. By 1848 the memory had faded, and the apparatus, which had been built to prevent conspiracies, was structurally incapable of responding to a spontaneous mass movement that had no organisational structure to penetrate.** The Polizeihofstelle had approximately 800 salaried informers in Vienna in 1848. The crowd outside the Landhaus on the morning of 13 March numbered, at ten o'clock, approximately 4,000 people, and by two o'clock it numbered, by the estimate of the imperial engineer Franz von Hauslab, approximately 20,000. The informers could not identify the leaders because there were no leaders. The censorship apparatus could not suppress the demands because the demands were being shouted in the street. The passport system could not prevent internal migration because the migrants were the citizens of Vienna themselves, walking three hundred yards from their apartments to the Herrengasse. The instruments of the Metternich system were built to detect a conspiratorial minority. They were mute in the face of a mobilised majority, and the mobilised majority discovered, at approximately three o'clock in the afternoon, that it was in fact a majority.

The trigger was Kossuth's speech. Lajos Kossuth, the Hungarian liberal leader, had delivered on 3 March in the Hungarian Diet in Pressburg — the modern Bratislava — an address demanding a Hungarian constitution, ministerial responsibility, and the emancipation of the serfs. The speech had been printed, in translation, in the Viennese liberal press despite the censorship, and copies had been circulating in the coffee houses of the Inner City for a week. On the morning of 13 March, a group of students from the University of Vienna, led by a medical student named Adolf Fischhof whose name would not have appeared in any Polizeihofstelle file three days earlier, gathered in the courtyard of the Alte Universität, walked in procession to the Landhaus, and began to read the Kossuth speech aloud to the crowd that had assembled to observe the Estates of Lower Austria. The Estates, meeting inside, had prepared a modest petition for administrative reform. The crowd outside, listening to the Kossuth speech, produced, over the course of two hours, a set of demands that went far beyond the Estates' petition: freedom of the press, abolition of censorship, a constitution, ministerial responsibility, and the resignation of Metternich. **The demands were not organised. They emerged from the crowd. By one o'clock they had been reduced to writing by a committee of students that had not existed at ten o'clock, and by two o'clock the committee had presented them to the Estates and demanded that they be transmitted to the Emperor.**

Metternich, in the Hofburg, received news of the demands at approximately one thirty. He responded by ordering the deployment of Field Marshal Alfred zu Windisch-Grätz's garrison — the Vienna military establishment of approximately 14,000 troops — to disperse the crowd. Windisch-Grätz was in Prague. The acting commander, Archduke Albrecht, ordered the deployment of two battalions of the 25th Infantry Regiment to the Herrengasse and the Ballhausplatz. The troops fired on the crowd. The crowd did not disperse. Between two and four o'clock, at least forty-five civilians were killed by the Habsburg army in the streets of the city that had been the seat of the counter-revolution for thirty-three years, and the Habsburg army discovered that the killing of forty-five civilians had produced not the dispersal of the crowd but its expansion, as the workers of the Vorstädte — the working-class suburbs outside the Ringstrasse, where the Polizeihofstelle had no informer network at all — began to march on the Inner City in numbers that the garrison could not contain. **At approximately four o'clock, the imperial family met in the Hofburg. At approximately five o'clock, they decided that Metternich would have to go. At approximately six o'clock, he was informed. At approximately eight o'clock, he handed his resignation to the Emperor Ferdinand, a man who was, by any reasonable clinical criterion, mentally incapable of receiving it. At approximately eleven o'clock, in the laundry cart, in the clothing of a female servant, he left the Hofburg by a service passage that had been designed for the discreet exit of imperial mistresses in the reign of Joseph II.**

The system had lasted eight hours from the first speech in the Landhaus courtyard to the flight of its architect.

**What is worth understanding about this collapse is that it was not caused by the strength of the revolutionaries.** The revolutionaries of 13 March 1848 were students, artisans, and journalists. They had no weapons, no organisation, no funding, no international support, and no plan. They constructed, in the eight hours between ten in the morning and six in the evening, a movement whose demands were sufficiently coherent to be transmitted to the Emperor as a political programme. The programme was implemented, in modified form, over the following four months: censorship was abolished on 15 March; a constitution was promulgated on 25 April; the serfs were emancipated on 7 September; the Emperor Ferdinand abdicated on 2 December in favour of his eighteen-year-old nephew Franz Joseph, who would reign for sixty-eight years and would spend the entirety of that reign engaged in the reconstruction of the counter-revolutionary system that Metternich had built and that had collapsed in an afternoon. The revolutionaries did not, in the end, win. The counter-revolution of 1849, prosecuted by Windisch-Grätz in Prague and Vienna and by the Russian army in Hungary under the terms of the Holy Alliance that Metternich had negotiated in 1815, restored the Habsburg order in modified form. The 1848 revolution was, in the standard textbook formulation, a defeated revolution.

**But it defeated Metternich in an afternoon, and the reason it defeated him in an afternoon is that his system had been, for at least the previous decade, an elaborate stage set. The reality behind the stage set was a Habsburg administration that had, by 1848, exhausted the fiscal and human capacity to sustain the level of surveillance and preventive suppression that the system was theoretically providing. The 800 Vienna informers of 1848 had been 1,400 in 1830. The censorship apparatus, which had reviewed 900 items per year in the 1820s, was reviewing 400 in 1847 because the imperial treasury could no longer afford to pay the censors. The passport system was being evaded on a scale that the Polizeihofstelle's internal reports acknowledged and that the administration lacked the means to remedy. The apparatus of the Metternich system was still nominally in place, but it had been hollowed out by three decades of fiscal pressure, and its effectiveness by 1848 was maintained almost entirely by the assumption — held by the population that was theoretically being surveilled — that the apparatus was still what it had been in 1820.**

The events of 13 March 1848 destroyed the assumption, and the destruction of the assumption destroyed the apparatus. Once the crowd in the Herrengasse discovered, at approximately three o'clock in the afternoon, that the troops firing on it were not enough to disperse it, the entire architecture of preventive suppression that Metternich had built over thirty-three years lost its operational meaning. The informers went home. The censors stopped writing reports. The passport officials abandoned their posts. The apparatus did not resist. It evaporated, because it had been, for at least a decade, a memory of an apparatus rather than an apparatus in fact, and once the memory was contradicted by direct observation the memory ceased to control behaviour.

**The lesson of the Metternich system is that police states rule by consent — the consent of the ruled to be afraid of the state's capacity to observe them — and that when the consent is withdrawn the state discovers that its capacity is a fraction of what its subjects had believed.** Metternich understood this at some level. In his memoirs, written in exile in Brighton and completed in 1852, he described the events of 13 March 1848 as an accident produced by weather, by the incompetence of the acting commander, and by the misjudgement of the imperial family. He did not describe them as the discovery of a truth about his own system that had been true for at least a decade before it was discovered. He returned to Vienna in 1851, at the invitation of Franz Joseph, and lived quietly in the Rennweg until his death in 1859 at the age of eighty-six. He never held office again. He was consulted, in the last years of his life, by the young Emperor and by the counter-revolutionary ministers Bach and Schwarzenberg who were engaged in the reconstruction of the Habsburg state on a foundation of neo-absolutism that resembled the Metternich system in form but had been rebuilt, at enormous expense, on the understanding that the original had been an illusion.

**The neo-absolutist state that Franz Joseph constructed after 1849 lasted, in its Habsburg form, until 1918.** It was destroyed not by revolution but by a world war that its own diplomacy had helped to produce, and its collapse in November 1918 was, like the collapse of the Metternich system in March 1848, sudden and unresisted: the apparatus that had appeared, on 27 October 1918, to be capable of holding a multi-national empire together collapsed, by 12 November 1918, into a set of successor republics that had reconstituted themselves faster than the Habsburg administration could respond. The pattern was the same. The apparatus was hollow. The consent had been withdrawn. The state discovered, over the course of sixteen days, that it had been ruling on a bluff since — at the latest — the death of Franz Joseph in November 1916.

Metternich's system, in this sense, established the pattern for every subsequent European authoritarian state, and the collapse of Metternich's system on 13 March 1848 established the pattern for every subsequent European authoritarian collapse. The Habsburg empire collapsed in 1918 in the same way. The Wilhelmine empire collapsed in November 1918 in the same way. The Russian empire collapsed in February 1917 in the same way. The Communist regimes of Eastern Europe collapsed in November and December 1989 in the same way. In each case, an apparatus of surveillance and preventive suppression that had been credited with a capacity for repression that vastly exceeded its actual capacity was tested by a mass movement that its designers had not anticipated, and in each case the apparatus dissolved within days once the test revealed the discrepancy.

**The system had been a bluff. The subjects had been calling their own bluff, on themselves, for thirty-three years. Metternich, in the laundry cart at eleven o'clock at night on 13 March 1848, was the last person in Europe to understand this, and he understood it, one may reasonably suppose, on the road to Frankfurt.**
