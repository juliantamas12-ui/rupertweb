# The Six Months in 1979 When Zbigniew Brzezinski Persuaded Jimmy Carter to Bait the Soviet Union Into Invading Afghanistan and Built the Global Jihadist Infrastructure That Would Kill Three Thousand Americans Twenty-Two Years Later

**Date:** 20 July 2026
**Category:** Geopolitics

---

On 3 July 1979, in the Oval Office of the West Wing of the White House, President Jimmy Carter signed a Presidential Finding — a classified authorisation of covert action under Section 662 of the Foreign Assistance Act — that directed the Central Intelligence Agency to provide non-lethal aid to the Islamist insurgents fighting the Marxist government of Nur Muhammad Taraki in Afghanistan. The insurgents, at that point, were a scattered coalition of tribal fundamentalists, disaffected army officers, and mullahs radicalised by the People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan's ham-fisted attempt to impose land reform and female literacy on a rural society that considered both to be blasphemies. The Soviet Union had not invaded Afghanistan. There was, at the moment Carter signed the Finding, no Soviet-Afghan war and no evident intention on the Kremlin's part to fight one. The Finding was not a response to Soviet aggression. It was, according to the man who wrote it, an instrument designed to produce Soviet aggression, on the theory that a Soviet invasion of Afghanistan would deliver to the United States a strategic asset of incalculable value: **the Soviet Union's own Vietnam.**

The man who wrote it was Zbigniew Kazimierz Brzeziński, Carter's National Security Advisor, Polish by birth and Russophobic by inheritance, and the interview in which he confirmed the sequence — given to Vincent Jauvert of the Nouvel Observateur, published on 15 January 1998, and denied in bad faith by the American foreign policy establishment ever since — remains the single most disturbing document of late Cold War diplomacy. Asked whether he regretted having supported the mujahideen given that the network had produced the Taliban, al-Qaeda, and the international jihadist movement that had by 1998 already bombed the World Trade Center once, Brzeziński gave an answer that his defenders have spent a quarter of a century attempting to contextualise into meaninglessness. **"Regret what?"** he said. **"That secret operation was an excellent idea. It had the effect of drawing the Russians into the Afghan trap and you want me to regret it? The day that the Soviets officially crossed the border, I wrote to President Carter, in substance: 'We now have the opportunity of giving to the USSR its Vietnam war.'"**

What is worth understanding about this sequence is not that Brzeziński was mistaken about the consequences — everyone, at every point in the Cold War, was mistaken about the consequences — but that he was not mistaken. His theory of the case was correct at every stage. The Soviet Union did invade Afghanistan, on 24 December 1979, six months after Carter's Finding, in a manner that Brzeziński had predicted with such specificity that his 26 December memorandum to Carter reads more like a project retrospective than a briefing. The war did become the Soviet Vietnam. It consumed 15,000 Soviet dead, an estimated one million Afghan dead, and roughly $50 billion in Soviet military expenditure across ten years. It destroyed the Red Army's confidence in its own doctrine, produced the veterans' movement that Gorbachev would use as a lever against the party old guard, and provided the specific proximate cause of the Soviet Union's collapse in 1991. Every strategic goal that Brzeziński set for the operation in 1979 was achieved by 1989. The operation was, in the terms in which it was designed, one of the most successful covert actions in the history of American intelligence.

**And it produced 9/11.**

The connection is not a coincidence, and it is not a case of blowback in the general sense in which the term is deployed by opponents of American foreign policy. It is a case of blowback in the strict operational sense: the specific infrastructure that the CIA built to fight the Soviets in Afghanistan between 1979 and 1989 — the training camps in Pakistan's Federally Administered Tribal Areas, the recruitment networks in the Gulf States, the arms pipeline through the Inter-Services Intelligence agency, the ideological framework that presented the Afghan war as a global jihad rather than a national resistance — was, in 1989 when the Soviets withdrew, the largest, best-funded, and most operationally capable armed Islamist network that had ever existed. That network did not dissolve when the CIA lost interest in it in 1990. It reorganised. Its Pakistani, Saudi, Egyptian, and Yemeni graduates returned home, or moved on to Bosnia, Chechnya, Kashmir, and eventually New York and Washington, carrying with them the tactical and organisational skills that the CIA had spent ten years teaching them. Osama bin Laden was not a creature of the Afghan operation in the sense of having been personally recruited or paid by the CIA — the direct funding relationships ran through the ISI to Afghan commanders, not to Arab volunteers — but he was a creature of it in the sense that everything he needed to build al-Qaeda between 1988 and 2001 had been assembled, and paid for, by the American taxpayer between 1979 and 1989. The 9/11 hijackers trained in camps that had been built with CIA money. They travelled on networks that had been established by the ISI with CIA coordination. They were funded by mechanisms that had been developed to move Saudi money to the mujahideen. **The instruments of the attack were the accumulated capital of the operation that had produced the collapse of the Soviet Union.**

The Brzeziński argument — advanced in the 1998 interview and defended by American strategic thinkers ever since — is that the trade-off was worth it. The Soviet Union, he told Jauvert, was **"an evil empire responsible for the deaths of tens of millions and the enslavement of hundreds of millions."** The Afghan operation contributed to its collapse and to the liberation of Eastern Europe. Al-Qaeda, by contrast, is **"some agitated Muslims"** — his phrase, in the 1998 interview, three years before al-Qaeda would kill 2,977 Americans in a single morning — whose historical significance cannot be compared to that of the Soviet Union. The trade was a good one. The trade was, in fact, the trade that any serious grand strategist would have made in Brzeziński's position, and the fact that opponents of the war on terror refuse to see it as such is, in his view, evidence of their strategic infantilism rather than of his moral obtuseness.

The argument has three defects, and none of them has ever been adequately addressed by Brzeziński's defenders.

**The first is that the Soviet Union was already losing.** By 1979 the Soviet economic model had stagnated to the point that even the Politburo's internal projections could not construct a scenario in which per-capita output would catch up to Western European levels within a century. The war in Afghanistan was a symptom of Soviet decline — a client-state stabilisation operation of the kind that declining empires undertake — rather than a cause of it, and the Soviet collapse of 1991 would have occurred within a decade whether or not the Afghan war had been fought. The Afghan operation may have accelerated the timeline by three or four years. It did not change the outcome. The costs of the operation — the specific costs, the ones that produced the 9/11 attacks — were therefore paid in exchange for a marginal acceleration of a collapse that would have occurred anyway.

**The second is that Brzeziński's characterisation of the trade as one between the Soviet Union and "agitated Muslims" is a false equivalence in the opposite direction from the one his critics accuse him of making.** The Soviet Union was a state, and states can be deterred, negotiated with, and bounded by treaty. The global jihadist network is a non-state actor that cannot. The strategic problem that al-Qaeda posed to the United States in 2001 was of a categorically different kind than the strategic problem that the Soviet Union posed in 1979, and the disproportion between them cannot be measured in body counts or in the number of countries oppressed. What the Soviet Union possessed was a set of interests that could be understood and, in principle, accommodated. What al-Qaeda possessed was a millenarian theology that could be understood but not accommodated, and that produced a class of political violence — indiscriminate, mass-casualty, aimed at civilians rather than at the state — that the United States has been unable to defeat by any of the instruments it developed during the Cold War. The trade Brzeziński made was not a good one because it exchanged a comprehensible adversary for an incomprehensible one, and the American strategic apparatus has never recovered from the exchange.

**The third is that Brzeziński knew.** The internal documentation of the Carter administration establishes that Brzeziński was warned, repeatedly, between 1979 and 1980, that the Islamist character of the Afghan insurgency posed a long-term risk that would outlast the Soviet war. Cyrus Vance, Secretary of State until his resignation in April 1980, warned that arming the mujahideen would radicalise the region's Islamism in ways that would eventually threaten American interests. Stansfield Turner, Director of Central Intelligence until January 1981, cautioned that the network being constructed to run the operation would be difficult to control once the operation ended. Vance was dismissed by Brzeziński as a moralising incompetent whose objections could be safely ignored. Turner's warnings were noted and set aside. The Reagan administration, which inherited the operation in January 1981 and expanded it to a scale that Carter had not contemplated — Stinger missiles from 1986, direct CIA involvement in tactical planning from 1985, a funding pipeline that reached $700 million per year at its peak — did so on the explicit understanding, communicated by Brzeziński to William Casey in the 1980–81 transition, that the ideological character of the insurgency was a feature rather than a bug: the more radical the mujahideen, the more effective they would be against the Soviets, and the more likely the Soviets would be to overextend themselves in trying to contain them. The radicalism was designed in. The Islamism was designed in. The infrastructure that would produce al-Qaeda was designed in.

The Afghan operation is, in this specific sense, not a case of American incompetence or American short-sightedness. It is a case of American strategic virtuosity applied to a Cold War problem, without any regard for what would happen to the instruments of that virtuosity when the Cold War ended. Brzeziński's mistake — if it can be called a mistake, given that he never regarded it as one — was not that he failed to anticipate the consequences. It is that he anticipated them, weighed them against the collapse of the Soviet Union, and decided that the collapse was worth them. On 15 January 1998, when he told the Nouvel Observateur that the trade had been a good one, he was expressing a view that he had held consistently since 3 July 1979, and that no subsequent event had persuaded him to abandon.

**Brzeziński died on 26 May 2017**, at the age of 89, having lived long enough to see the towers fall, the two American wars that followed them, the collapse of the Iraqi state, the rise of the Islamic State, and the American strategic exhaustion of the 2010s. He did not, in any of the interviews he gave in the intervening years, revise the 1998 assessment. He was asked about it repeatedly. He answered, each time, that the collapse of the Soviet Union had been a strategic gain of such magnitude that no subsequent cost could outweigh it, and that the American foreign policy establishment's inability to accept this fact was a symptom of that establishment's incapacity for strategic seriousness. He was buried at Arlington National Cemetery, with military honours, on the recommendation of a Republican Secretary of Defense and a Democratic former President who spoke at his funeral. The obituaries described him as a great American strategist. The characterisation is correct. The greatness was of a kind that the country he served has spent the twenty-first century learning to survive.

The lesson of the Afghan operation is that the intelligence services of great powers are capable of building instruments whose strategic value in the short term is measured in the collapse of rival empires and whose strategic cost in the long term is measured in the collapse of the world order that the empire's collapse produces. **The mujahideen were the finest strategic instrument the CIA ever built.** They were also the mechanism by which the Cold War's peace dividend was cancelled, twenty-two years after it was declared, on a bright morning in September 2001 when the interest on Zbigniew Brzeziński's 1979 investment came due.
