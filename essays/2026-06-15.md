# The Afternoon of 22 November 1975 in the Cortes When Juan Carlos Swore Loyalty to Franco's Movimiento and Began Dismantling It That Same Week

On the afternoon of Saturday, 22 November 1975, two days after the death of Francisco Franco, the thirty-seven-year-old Juan Carlos de Borbón stood before the Cortes Españolas in the Palacio de las Cortes on the Carrera de San Jerónimo and swore, on the four Gospels held by the cardinal-primate Vicente Enrique y Tarancón, to uphold the Fundamental Laws of the Kingdom and the Principles of the National Movement. He spoke the formula in a flat, careful voice. He gave nothing away. The Francoist generals, the procuradores of the rump Cortes, the surviving veterans of the Civil War in their blue Falange shirts — all of them watched him take the oath that bound him, in law, to the regime that had spent twenty years preparing him for this throne. Within ten weeks Juan Carlos would dismiss the Francoist prime minister Carlos Arias Navarro. Within twelve months he would have legalised the Communist Party. Within fourteen months he would have called the first free elections in Spain since February 1936. The man who had sworn loyalty to the Movimiento Nacional on 22 November 1975 had, by the time he stood for re-election as constitutional monarch in December 1978, abolished every institution to which he had pledged himself.

The standard account treats the Spanish transition as a kind of national miracle — a collective decision by the political class to convert dictatorship into democracy through pact, compromise, and amnesia. There is some truth in this. The Moncloa Pacts of October 1977, the constitution of December 1978, the negotiated amnesty of the same year — these were genuine acts of political construction by men on both sides of the old divide who had decided that a second civil war was the worst available outcome. **But the transition did not begin with these pacts. It began with a single decision taken by the king during the first nine weeks of his reign: to use the legal machinery of the Franco regime to dismantle the Franco regime, from within, with the consent and signatures of the men who had built it. That decision was made by Juan Carlos personally, against the advice of almost every senior figure he had inherited, and it required a calculated and sustained act of deception against the political class that had placed him on the throne.**

To see this one has to understand what Juan Carlos was, in the constitutional architecture of the late Franco regime. He was not the legitimate heir to the Spanish throne. That title belonged to his father, Don Juan de Borbón y Battenberg, Count of Barcelona, who had been the recognised pretender since the death of Alfonso XIII in 1941 and who had spent thirty years in exile in Estoril, Portugal, condemning the Franco dictatorship and presenting himself as the constitutional alternative. Franco had no intention of restoring the legitimist Bourbon line. In 1948 he reached an arrangement with Don Juan to take the ten-year-old Juan Carlos to Spain for his education, to be raised under the personal supervision of the Caudillo, and in 1969, by the Law of Succession, Franco passed over Don Juan and designated Juan Carlos as his successor with the title Prince of Spain. The prince swore an oath to the Movimiento and the Fundamental Laws at the moment of his designation. Don Juan, in Estoril, denounced the act as a usurpation. Franco's calculation was clear: he had trained the boy for twenty years, he had picked him over his father precisely because he believed him pliable, and he was confident that the institutions he was leaving behind — the Movimiento, the syndicalist trade unions, the Consejo del Reino, the Cortes packed with appointed procuradores — would constrain whatever sentimentalism the young king might be tempted to indulge after his death.

This was the most catastrophic personnel decision of Franco's career. The man Franco had spent twenty years preparing was not, as the Caudillo believed, a politically empty vessel waiting to be filled with the doctrines of the National Movement. He was a Bourbon prince with a Bourbon's instinct for legitimacy and a Bourbon's contempt for the men who had usurped his family's throne. Juan Carlos had spent his twenties watching the Francoist political class up close — Carrero Blanco assassinated in December 1973, Arias Navarro shuffling between reformist gestures and reactionary panic, the generals of the Búnker plotting against any opening, the regime's economic technocrats privately admitting that the model was exhausted. **He had concluded, by the time of Franco's final illness in October 1975, that the regime was a corpse propped upright by the personal authority of a single dying man, and that the only question left was who would carry out the executor's office and on whose behalf.**

The oath on 22 November was the first move in that executor's office. The king took it because he had to: the legal title to the throne ran through the 1969 succession law, the succession law required the oath, and any refusal would have been a coup against an apparatus that still controlled the army, the police, and the bureaucracy. He took it knowing that he intended to break it. The historian Paul Preston has documented, from the diaries and later interviews of those present, that the king told his private secretary on the evening of the swearing-in: *Esto es para hoy. Mañana es otro asunto.* That is for today. Tomorrow is another matter.

The mechanism the king chose for the dismantling is the second piece of the story that the standard account underplays. He did not abrogate the Fundamental Laws by decree. He did not call a constituent assembly. He did not appeal to the army or the street. He used the regime's own legal machinery — the Cortes packed with Franco's procuradores, the Consejo del Reino, the Council of Ministers — to vote itself out of existence, in a sequence of acts whose constitutional propriety was unimpeachable and whose political effect was the total destruction of the system that authorised them. The instrument was the Law for Political Reform, drafted in the summer of 1976 by Adolfo Suárez — the king's astonishingly cynical choice for prime minister after he dismissed Arias Navarro in July 1976 — and presented to the Francoist Cortes in November 1976 with the simple promise that it would create a new bicameral parliament elected by universal suffrage. The Cortes voted for it 425 to 59, with 13 abstentions. The procuradores of the Movimiento, the procuradores appointed by Franco himself, the procuradores of the vertical syndicates and the Falange and the family branch — they voted to abolish their own offices and dissolve the chambers in which they sat. **It was the most successful act of constitutional suicide in the history of European politics, and it was achieved by lying to every one of the men whose votes were required to pass it.**

The lie had two layers. The first was the king's private assurance to the senior Francoist barons — Fernández Miranda, Areilza, Fraga, even the head of the army Manuel Gutiérrez Mellado — that the reform would preserve the essential continuities of the regime: the unity of Spain, the monarchy, the suppression of communism, the constitutional position of the army. The second was Suárez's specific promise to the Cortes that the Communist Party of Spain would not be legalised before the elections. Both promises were broken within months. The Communist Party was legalised on 9 April 1977 — Holy Saturday, chosen with calculated impudence to bury the announcement in the Easter weekend press cycle — by direct royal-prime ministerial decision against the unanimous opposition of the Consejo Superior del Ejército. The elections of June 1977 returned a parliament dominated by parties that did not recognise the Fundamental Laws, did not believe in the Movimiento, and immediately drafted a constitution that abolished both. By the time the army digested what had happened, the constitutional change was a fait accompli, the king was the symbolic guarantor of the new order, and the only available counter-move was the one Antonio Tejero attempted on 23 February 1981 — and which the king, by his television address that night, defeated.

The deeper question is whether the deception was justified. The men on whom Juan Carlos and Suárez practised it had spent forty years denying political freedom to half the country. The argument that the Francoist establishment had a moral claim to truthful treatment by the institutions to which they had appointed themselves is not, on examination, a strong one. **But the constitutional fact remains that the Spanish transition was achieved by a king who swore an oath he intended to break and a prime minister who made specific promises to the legislature he intended to abolish, and the long-term legitimacy of Spanish democracy rests on a foundation of executive bad faith that the country has spent fifty years declining to examine.** The political amnesia of the transition — the *pacto del olvido*, the unspoken agreement not to prosecute the crimes of the dictatorship — was the price the new order paid for the fact that its founding documents were signed by men who were being systematically deceived about what they were signing.

The counterfactual is the standard one for European constitutional change. If Juan Carlos had been the pliant Bourbon Franco believed he was preparing, the regime would have continued under one or other of the Francoist barons — Fraga, Suárez at the head of a continuity faction, or in the harder scenarios a Búnker general — and Spain would have followed Portugal into the kind of low-grade post-authoritarian instability that consumed the late 1970s in Lisbon. If Juan Carlos had been honest with the Francoist establishment about what he intended, the army would have moved against him by mid-1976 and the transition would have begun in a coup attempt rather than ended in one. The narrow corridor between these two outcomes — the one in which a king deceives the regime that placed him on his throne, uses its own institutions to vote themselves out of existence, and then defeats the inevitable military reaction four years later by speaking thirteen minutes on television at one in the morning — was navigated by a single man whose principal political asset was that nobody, including Franco, had ever taken him seriously.

The lesson of the Spanish transition is not the one the political scientists draw. It is not that democracy can be built by pact and compromise. It is that a hereditary system that is genuinely indifferent to its supposed political content — that is loyal only to the family that wears the crown — can be a more effective solvent of dictatorship than any of the revolutionary alternatives, precisely because the dictator's machinery will continue to take its orders from the king it has installed long after the king has decided to use those orders to destroy it. **Juan Carlos did not save Spanish democracy. He created it, by an act of dynastic self-interest the political class of the regime entirely failed to anticipate, and the Spanish state has spent the half-century since trying to forget that its constitutional order was founded on a Bourbon's lie.**
