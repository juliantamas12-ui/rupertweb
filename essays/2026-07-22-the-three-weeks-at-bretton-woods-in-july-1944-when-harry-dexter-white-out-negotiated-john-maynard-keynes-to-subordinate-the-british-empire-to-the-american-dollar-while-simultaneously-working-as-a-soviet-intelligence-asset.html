The Three Weeks at Bretton Woods in July 1944 When Harry Dexter White Out-Negotiated John Maynard Keynes to Subordinate the British Empire to the American Dollar While Simultaneously Working as a Soviet Intelligence Asset

On 1 July 1944, seven hundred and thirty delegates from forty-four Allied and associated nations arrived at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, to negotiate the post-war international monetary order. The war in Europe had eleven months to run. The war in the Pacific had fourteen. The German army was in France; the Red Army had entered Poland the previous week; the Manhattan Project had not yet tested its device. The two men who would decide the shape of the twentieth-century economy were John Maynard Keynes, sixty-one years old, mortally ill with the heart disease that would kill him twenty-one months later, and leading the British Treasury delegation as the most famous economist alive; and Harry Dexter White, fifty-one years old, Assistant Secretary of the United States Treasury, chief of the Division of Monetary Research, principal author of the American negotiating position, and — as the National Security Agency's Venona decrypts would establish in classified form by 1950 and in public form by 1995 — a paid asset of Soviet military intelligence operating under the codename JURIST. The Bretton Woods conference was, at one level, a technical negotiation about exchange rates and clearing mechanisms. At another level, it was the moment at which the British Empire ceased to be a first-rank power and the American dollar became the global reserve currency. At a third level, which nobody in the room understood, it was a Soviet intelligence operation whose principal agent was drafting the American position.

The negotiation that mattered had been settled before the delegates arrived. Between 1941 and 1944, White and Keynes had exchanged plans — the White Plan, first drafted in April 1942, and the Keynes Plan, first published as a British Treasury White Paper in April 1943 — that represented two fundamentally incompatible visions of the post-war order. Keynes proposed an International Clearing Union that would issue a synthetic reserve unit called the bancor, against which national currencies would be pegged, and which would impose symmetrical adjustment obligations on creditor and debtor countries alike. The Keynes Plan was designed, transparently, to prevent the American surplus of the 1940s from becoming the mechanism by which the American economy dictated the terms of trade to the rest of the world in the 1950s. It would have required the United States, as the war's sole surplus economy, to expand its imports or lose reserves to the Clearing Union. It was the plan of a country that expected to be a permanent debtor. The White Plan proposed the opposite. It centred on a Stabilization Fund — the entity that would become the International Monetary Fund — capitalised by national contributions in gold and national currencies, from which countries in balance-of-payments deficit could draw at increasing conditionality, and it imposed adjustment obligations exclusively on debtor countries. It was the plan of a country that expected to be a permanent creditor. And it made the American dollar, alone among national currencies, convertible into gold at $35 per ounce and thereby the anchor of the entire system.

Between April 1943 and July 1944, White and Keynes negotiated a Joint Statement of Principles that adopted the White Plan's architecture in every respect that mattered. The bancor was abandoned. The Clearing Union was abandoned. Symmetrical adjustment was abandoned. The dollar's gold-convertibility privilege was written into the founding document. By the time the delegates arrived at Bretton Woods on 1 July 1944, the substantive negotiation was over, and the three weeks in New Hampshire were, in effect, a ratification ceremony for terms that the American Treasury had already imposed on a British government that was, by the summer of 1944, financially incapable of resisting them. Britain owed the United States $21 billion under Lend-Lease. Britain's gold and dollar reserves in July 1944 stood at £453 million against liabilities to the sterling area of £3.4 billion. Britain was, in any meaningful accounting sense, insolvent, and White knew it, and Keynes knew that White knew it, and the negotiation at Bretton Woods was conducted in the shadow of that knowledge.

The mechanism by which White achieved this was quota politics. The IMF's governance would be weighted by member contributions — quotas — and the American quota, at $2.75 billion, would give the United States 27 per cent of the voting power and a permanent veto over the Fund's principal decisions, which required 80 per cent supermajorities. The British quota, at $1.3 billion, would give Britain 13 per cent. But the negotiation over the quotas of the smaller powers is where White operated with the most cunning, and where his simultaneous work for Soviet intelligence emerges as the least examined feature of the historiography. The Soviet quota, at $1.2 billion, was the third-largest at Bretton Woods, and it had been calculated on assumptions about Soviet gold reserves and Soviet foreign trade that were, by any independent estimate, considerable overstatements. White had personally negotiated the Soviet quota with Mikhail Stepanov of the Soviet delegation, and White had personally overruled the American technical staff who recommended a substantially lower figure. The Venona traffic, decrypted between 1946 and 1980 and released to the public in 1995, contains twelve identified references to JURIST providing information to Soviet handlers between 1943 and 1945, and includes at least one reference — cable 1119-1121 from New York to Moscow, 4-5 August 1944 — to the substance of the American negotiating position at Bretton Woods being communicated to Soviet intelligence in real time during the conference.

Consider what this means. The two men in the room who understood the technical structure of the emerging system were Keynes, who represented a British government that was being financially strangled by its ally, and White, who represented an American Treasury whose principal official on the file was reporting to a foreign intelligence service. The system that emerged from Bretton Woods was, from the American perspective, a stunning strategic achievement — the dollar became the world's reserve currency; American gold reserves, which had risen to 20,205 tons by 1949, backed the entire system; American exports enjoyed the seigniorage advantages of a reserve-currency issuer for the next quarter-century — and it was designed, in its technical particulars, by a Soviet asset. The two facts are not contradictory. The Soviet Union, in 1944, did not oppose the emergence of an American-dominated capitalist monetary order in Western Europe; it opposed the extension of that order into Eastern Europe. The Bretton Woods agreements, by fixing the American sphere at the boundaries of the sterling area and the American occupation zones, effectively conceded Eastern Europe to Soviet economic reconstruction on Soviet terms, and the Soviet Union declined to ratify the Bretton Woods agreements in December 1945 for reasons that were consistent with this reading. White's Soviet handlers did not need to sabotage Bretton Woods. They needed to ensure that the American position at Bretton Woods did not include Eastern European convertibility, and this is what White delivered.

Keynes understood, at some level, that he had been out-negotiated. His memorandum to the British Treasury of 25 July 1944, written on the last day of the conference, described the outcome as "the best we could get" and recorded that "the Americans have got what they wanted at every point that mattered." His draft speech to the House of Lords of 23 May 1944, presenting the interim agreement, contains the passage — subsequently deleted from the delivered version at Treasury insistence — in which he observed that "the essence of the plan is that it commits the sterling area to a policy of convertibility on terms that we shall not be able to sustain, and it will require a further American loan of a magnitude that we have not yet contemplated to make the transition feasible." The Anglo-American Loan Agreement of December 1945 — $3.75 billion at 2 per cent interest over fifty years, with a convertibility requirement at eighteen months — is the sequel that Keynes anticipated. The sterling convertibility crisis of August 1947, which forced Britain to suspend convertibility six weeks after it began and destroyed the fiction of Britain's reserve-currency status in a single month, is the crisis he anticipated. Keynes had died by then, on 21 April 1946, of a heart attack in his home in Sussex. He had been dead for sixteen months when the system he had ratified at Bretton Woods produced its first predictable failure, and the failure vindicated every criticism he had privately made of it and had been institutionally unable to voice.

White, meanwhile, had been promoted to Executive Director of the IMF in February 1946. He held the position for fourteen months. In March 1947, the FBI provided the White House with the first summaries of the Venona decrypts that identified him as JURIST. He was allowed to resign quietly. On 13 August 1948, he testified before the House Un-American Activities Committee, denied under oath that he had ever assisted Soviet intelligence, delivered a peroration on the American constitutional tradition that Whittaker Chambers, watching from the gallery, described as "the finest performance I ever saw on the Hill", and travelled to his farm in Fitzwilliam, New Hampshire, where he suffered a heart attack on 15 August and died on 16 August at the age of fifty-five. The IMF he had built lasted until August 1971. The dollar-gold parity he had negotiated lasted until August 1971. The system of American reserve-currency privilege he had constructed lasted until August 1971, and it was ended not by a Soviet intervention but by a Republican president closing the gold window to win a domestic political argument. The Bretton Woods system, in its intended form, was in operation for twenty-seven years. It has been the object of nostalgia for the fifty-five years since it collapsed, and the nostalgia has consistently underestimated the extent to which its architecture was designed by a man who had loyalties that the architects of the nostalgia have preferred not to examine.

The lesson of the three weeks at Bretton Woods is that the great institutional settlements of the twentieth century were not the products of the technical consensus that their subsequent reputations imply. They were the products of specific negotiations conducted by specific people whose motives were legible only to themselves, and the settlements were shaped decisively by whoever was in the room when the drafting was done. Keynes was in the room and lost. White was in the room and won, and won on behalf of at least two principals — the United States Treasury and, on the evidence of the Venona decrypts, the Soviet GRU — whose interests he had reconciled in a document that both sides mistook for a victory. The British Empire, which had entered the room as a great power, left as a client. It has been a client ever since.
