The Four Hours on 11 March 1985 in the Kremlin When Andrei Gromyko Nominated Gorbachev Before the Old Guard Could Caucus — and Decided the End of the Soviet Union by Procedural Ambush

On the morning of Monday, 11 March 1985, at 10:00 Moscow time, the Politburo of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union convened in the second-floor conference room of the Kremlin Senate building to choose a successor to Konstantin Chernenko, who had died at 19:20 the previous evening of pulmonary emphysema and chronic heart failure. The meeting had been scheduled for 14:00. Andrei Gromyko, the seventy-five-year-old foreign minister who had served every Soviet leader since Stalin, telephoned the duty secretary at 07:30 and asked that the time be brought forward by four hours. The reason he gave was that a foreign delegation was waiting in Vnukovo and required his attendance by evening. The reason he meant was that Viktor Grishin, the Moscow party boss, and Grigory Romanov, the Leningrad first secretary, were on aircraft returning from regional inspections and could not reach the Kremlin before noon. By the time the two men landed, Mikhail Gorbachev had been nominated, seconded, voted on, and confirmed as General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union. The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 is usually dated to the failed coup of August that year, or to the election of Yeltsin to the Russian presidency two months earlier, or to the Polish elections of June 1989. The procedural date is 11 March 1985, at approximately 11:15 Moscow time, when Gromyko named Gorbachev in a room that had been deliberately convened without the men who would have named anyone else.

The standard account of Gorbachev's elevation treats it as the orderly succession of a generational candidate within an established Politburo consensus. Anatoly Chernyaev, in his diary entry for that day, records the vote as unanimous. Gorbachev himself, in his 1995 memoirs, presents the meeting as the natural outcome of months of quiet positioning during Chernenko's illness. The Soviet historians of the late perestroika period — Roy Medvedev, Dmitri Volkogonov — accepted the unanimity at face value because the documentary record they had access to recorded no dissent. Neither did the documentary record. The dissent was preempted, by approximately three hours, through a logistical decision that no minute-taker recorded because no decision was formally taken. What happened in the Politburo room on the morning of 11 March was not a consensus. It was a procedural ambush, and the men who were ambushed did not realise until the afternoon that the succession they had spent four years preparing to contest had already been decided without them.

The mechanics matter because the Politburo of March 1985 was not, in any meaningful sense, in agreement about who should succeed Chernenko. The body had eleven full members. Four of them — Gorbachev, Gromyko, Nikolai Ryzhkov, and Eduard Shevardnadze (who became a full member that same week) — formed what the Western Kremlinologists later identified as the reform faction, though the term overstates the coherence of their views in March 1985. Three — Grishin, Romanov, and Nikolai Tikhonov, the eighty-year-old prime minister — were the surviving custodians of the Brezhnev order. Three more — Vladimir Shcherbitsky, Dinmukhamed Kunayev, and Vitaly Vorotnikov — were regional barons whose votes were available to whichever bloc could organise the meeting. Mikhail Solomentsev was a swing vote of his own. On a straight count, with all eleven men present and a contested nomination, Gorbachev's election was probable but not certain. Grishin had been Chernenko's preferred successor and had spent the final weeks of the dying General Secretary's tenure consolidating support among the regional first secretaries. The Politburo of 11 March 1985, fully assembled, would have elected Gorbachev by perhaps seven votes to four. The Politburo of 11 March 1985 as it actually convened at 10:00 elected him by nine to zero, with Grishin and Romanov absent.

The four-hour shift was Gromyko's. He had decided, by his own later admission in conversations with his son Anatoly recorded in the latter's 1997 memoir Andrei Gromyko: In the Labyrinths of the Kremlin, that Gorbachev was the only candidate capable of arresting the institutional decay of the Soviet state, and that the institutional decay had reached a point where a contested succession would be catastrophic regardless of the winner. His calculation was that a vote held under the procedural pressure of speed — convened before the absent members could return, held under the still-fresh shock of Chernenko's death, framed as a unity gesture to the foreign powers watching the transition — would produce a result that an unambushed Politburo could not produce. He was correct. The seconding speech he delivered, which Yegor Ligachev (who chaired the meeting in his capacity as Central Committee secretariat coordinator) preserved in his own memoir, ran for eleven minutes. It is the most consequential eleven minutes in Soviet political history after Stalin's death. Gromyko did not praise Gorbachev's policy positions, of which he knew little. He praised Gorbachev's "smile of iron" — zheleznaya ulybka — and the capacity, he said, to "speak with foreign statesmen as an equal." He was selling, to a room full of men who had spent two decades watching the Soviet diplomatic position erode, the prospect of a General Secretary capable of negotiating with Reagan. The selling worked because the men in the room were terrified of the alternative.

The deeper question is what would have happened if Grishin or Romanov had reached the Kremlin in time. Grishin had the votes of Tikhonov, of Shcherbitsky (whose Ukrainian patronage network was tied to the Brezhnev settlement), and probably of Kunayev. If the meeting had been held at 14:00 as originally scheduled, the bloc would have been four against four with Solomentsev as the casting vote, and Solomentsev had been Grishin's man since 1983. The probable outcome was Grishin as General Secretary by a margin of one. The historiography of what a Grishin general secretaryship would have meant is necessarily speculative, but the documentary evidence on Grishin's policy preferences is consistent enough to permit a sketch: continuation of the Brezhnev economic model with cosmetic adjustment; continued military commitment in Afghanistan; no negotiation with NATO beyond the existing INF framework; suppression of Solidarity in Poland through direct intervention if the Jaruzelski regime collapsed; and, critically, no glasnost. The Soviet Union under Grishin would not have reformed. It would have continued to decay along the trajectory it had followed since 1979 — declining oil revenues, declining grain yields, increasing technological backwardness, increasing military burden — and would have arrived, perhaps in 1995 rather than 1991, at a collapse that was uncontrolled rather than negotiated. The argument that the Soviet Union was doomed regardless of who succeeded Chernenko is correct in the long run and wrong in the medium. Doom is not a policy. The form of the collapse — peaceful or violent, negotiated or chaotic, with the nuclear arsenal secured or scattered — depended on the identity of the man who presided over it. That identity was decided by a phone call at 07:30 on a Monday morning.

The procedural ambush has a second dimension that is rarely commented on. Gromyko's seconding speech was delivered in the Politburo room, but the formal election of the General Secretary required ratification by the full Central Committee plenum, which met later that afternoon. The plenum had 319 members. Many of them had been Brezhnev appointees who owed their positions to the patronage network Grishin represented. A contested Politburo recommendation arriving at the plenum — with Grishin and Romanov publicly dissenting — would have been challenged by perhaps eighty members and the recommendation might have been referred back for reconsideration. A unanimous Politburo recommendation, presented by Gromyko himself with his unique authority as the senior surviving link to the Stalin generation, arrived at the plenum as a fait accompli. The plenum confirmed Gorbachev in approximately twenty minutes. Yegor Ligachev, in his closing remarks, used the word "unanimity" five times. The men in the hall who would have voted against Gorbachev in a contested ballot did not have the procedural opening to do so. The opening had been closed in the morning, by a phone call about a foreign delegation that did not, in fact, require Gromyko's attendance until the following Wednesday.

The lesson is one the Soviet system itself was structurally incapable of learning, though it had taught the lesson to others repeatedly. Procedural control of the meeting is more important than the substance of the question being decided. The Bolsheviks had won the October Revolution by controlling the agenda of the Second Congress of Soviets. Stalin had defeated Trotsky by controlling the calendar of the party congresses through the 1920s. Khrushchev had been removed in 1964 by a Politburo meeting convened, with similar speed, in his absence. The Soviet political class understood this principle better than any other in the twentieth century, and yet on the morning of 11 March 1985 the men who would have lost the succession failed to insist on the meeting being held at the scheduled hour. They failed because Gromyko's seniority, his reputation for caution, and his sixty-year service to the regime made his procedural request unchallengeable in real time. By the time it was challengeable, in the afternoon, the vote had been taken. Andrei Gromyko did not reform the Soviet Union. He did not believe in reforming the Soviet Union. He chose the man who would, and he chose him by a method that the Soviet system had taught him and that the Soviet system, in its terminal phase, could not defend against. The empire was ended, in the end, by the procedural reflexes of its most loyal servant.

Six years and three months later, on the night of 25 December 1991, Gorbachev signed the decree dissolving the office to which Gromyko had elevated him. The red flag came down over the Kremlin Senate building, on a roof visible from the window of the conference room in which the Politburo had met on the morning of 11 March 1985. Gromyko had died two years earlier, on 2 July 1989, at the age of seventy-nine. He had lived long enough to see the Berlin Wall come down but not long enough to see the Soviet Union itself dissolve. Whether he understood, in those last months, what his phone call on 11 March 1985 had set in motion — whether he understood that the institutional reflexes he had used against the old guard had handed power to a man who would dismantle the institution itself — is not recorded. The men who would have known are dead. The procedural minute of the morning meeting survives in the Russian state archives. It records the time of the convening, the names of the men present, the seconding speech, and the unanimous vote. It does not record the phone call at 07:30. There is no document for that. There never was.
