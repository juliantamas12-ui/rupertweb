# The Seventy-Two Hours in Late October 1962 When Fidel Castro Sent Nikita Khrushchev a Cable from the Havana Embassy Urging a Pre-Emptive Nuclear Strike on the United States, and Khrushchev Discovered That His Cuban Ally Had Passed Beyond His Operational Control

**Date:** 26 July 2026
**Category:** History

At 02:00 on the morning of Saturday, 27 October 1962, in the basement of the Soviet Embassy at 5th Avenue and 66th Street in the Miramar district of Havana, a small, bearded man of thirty-six in olive fatigues dictated a cable in Spanish to a KGB stenographer, who translated it into Russian in real time and passed each page as it was completed to the ambassador, Aleksandr Alekseev, for cypher transmission to Moscow. The cable was addressed to Nikita Sergeyevich Khrushchev, First Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union and Chairman of the Council of Ministers, and was sent under the personal signature of Fidel Alejandro Castro Ruz, Prime Minister of the Republic of Cuba. It stated, in the operative paragraph, that in the event of an American invasion of Cuba — which Castro assessed as certain within seventy-two hours — the Soviet Union should not merely respond with tactical nuclear weapons against the invading force, but should pre-emptively strike the continental United States with strategic weapons, accepting the consequent destruction of Cuba and the loss of the Cuban people as the unavoidable price of a general victory over imperialism.

The cable was received in Moscow at 08:30 Moscow time on 27 October, decrypted by the 8th Directorate of the KGB by 09:15, and placed before Khrushchev at the Presidium's morning session at 10:00. Khrushchev read it twice, put it down, and — according to the memoir of Fyodor Burlatsky, who was present as a Presidium speechwriter — said aloud: **"This is madness. He wants to drag us into the grave with him."** By 13:00 the same afternoon, Khrushchev had drafted the second of the two cables that would end the Cuban Missile Crisis: the cable to John Fitzgerald Kennedy conveying acceptance of the withdrawal of Soviet missiles from Cuba in exchange for a public American pledge not to invade the island. The cable was broadcast on Radio Moscow at 09:00 Washington time on Sunday, 28 October, before it had been transmitted through diplomatic channels, in order to prevent any possibility of its being blocked or modified by intermediaries in Havana. The crisis ended not because Kennedy had won a game of nerve against Khrushchev, but because Khrushchev had discovered, in the space of the preceding seventy-two hours, that the ally on whose behalf he was risking a general war had passed beyond his operational control and was actively lobbying for the extinction of both countries.

The conventional account of the Cuban Missile Crisis, which was fixed in Anglo-American historiography by Graham Allison's *Essence of Decision* in 1971 and has been elaborated but not fundamentally displaced in the fifty-five years since, treats the resolution as a rational bargain between two nuclear-armed states negotiating over a well-defined stake. The account is not wrong at the level of the exchange of messages between Kennedy and Khrushchev on 26–28 October. It is wrong at the level of causation. **The decisive event of the crisis was not the American blockade proclaimed on 22 October, nor the shooting down of Rudolf Anderson's U-2 over Banes on 27 October, nor the ExComm meetings in the White House Cabinet Room, nor Robert Kennedy's back-channel conversation with Anatoly Dobrynin at the Soviet Embassy on the evening of 27 October. The decisive event was Castro's cable, which arrived in Moscow at the moment when Khrushchev was still contemplating the possibility of holding his position, and which converted the strategic problem from a question of American resolve into a question of Soviet control over its own client.** The Soviet Union had inserted the missiles into Cuba on the assumption that Cuba was a Soviet operational asset. The cable of 27 October demonstrated that this assumption was false. Cuba was a state with its own strategic preferences, which included the acceptance of national annihilation as a rational cost of ideological victory. This was not a preference structure with which the Soviet leadership could operate. The withdrawal followed within twenty-four hours.

The mechanism by which Castro arrived at his position is worth reconstructing. He was not a lunatic, and he was not — despite the persistent legend cultivated by his enemies and by some of his admirers — an ideologue indifferent to the survival of his country. He was a revolutionary of a specific generation, formed by the failure of the 1953 attack on the Moncada barracks and the success of the December 1956 landing from the *Granma*, who had absorbed from Che Guevara and from his own reading of Lenin the doctrine that revolutions succeed by refusing to compromise at the moment when compromise appears prudent, and that the correct posture at the moment of maximum external pressure is not de-escalation but escalation. **Castro's cable was not a lapse of judgement. It was the operational application of the doctrine that had brought him to power, applied to a strategic situation in which the doctrine was catastrophically wrong.** He believed, on the evidence of the American mobilisation in Florida and of the U-2 reconnaissance overflights and of the intercepts of American radio traffic that Cuban intelligence was reading in real time, that an invasion was inevitable within days. He believed that the invasion would succeed — not because the Cuban armed forces could not fight, but because the American conventional superiority was of such magnitude that resistance would be crushed within weeks. He believed that in the event of Cuban defeat, the Soviet position in the Caribbean would collapse, the credibility of Soviet extended deterrence would be destroyed, and the international revolutionary movement would suffer a defeat from which it might not recover. He concluded, from these premises, that the rational course was to preempt: to force the Soviet Union to use its strategic arsenal while the arsenal was still in place and while the political conditions for its use still obtained. The reasoning was internally consistent. Its premises were partly correct. Its conclusion was insane.

Khrushchev's response to the cable, when it is read alongside the transcripts of the Presidium sessions of 27 and 28 October released by the Volkogonov archive in 1994 and by the Bukharin Collection at the Russian State Archive of Contemporary History in 2001, reveals a shift in his conception of the crisis that the American negotiators did not perceive at the time and that Allison's account did not capture. **Until the morning of 27 October, Khrushchev had been managing a bilateral confrontation with Kennedy in which the Cuban government was a stake, a symbol, and a base of operations, but not an independent actor. From the moment he read Castro's cable, he was managing a trilateral crisis in which the third party had preferences that were not merely different from his own but were incompatible with the continued existence of the human species.** The withdrawal of the missiles was not a concession to Kennedy. It was the removal of an instrument that Castro might have been able to trigger through provocation, sabotage of the command chain, or direct action against Soviet personnel in Cuba. The Soviet nuclear warheads in Cuba on 27 October 1962 numbered ninety-eight, of which forty-two were mated to intermediate-range missiles and fifty-six were tactical devices — cruise missiles, short-range Luna rockets, and free-fall bombs — distributed among Soviet units that were operating under a chain of command running through General Issa Pliyev at Soviet headquarters at El Chico, but that were exposed to Cuban intelligence, Cuban logistics, and Cuban territory. The tactical devices, in particular, were pre-delegated for use against invading forces at the discretion of Soviet local commanders under conditions of loss of communications with Moscow. Loss of communications with Moscow, in a theatre in which the communications infrastructure ran through Cuban territory, was a condition that Cuban action could create. Khrushchev's cable of 28 October, in which he described his decision as motivated by the need to prevent "an incalculable catastrophe," was not rhetorical. It was a precise description of what he understood to have become possible in the seventy-two hours before he sent it.

The consequences propagated through the strategic culture of the Cold War in ways that were not fully understood until the archival openings of the 1990s. **The Soviet leadership drew from the crisis the conclusion that reliance on ideologically committed clients as forward bases for strategic weapons was operationally impossible, because the clients could not be trusted to accept the escalation calculus of the metropolitan power. The doctrine that emerged — the doctrine of nuclear parity through direct central-strategic-force build-up, pursued through the 1960s and 1970s under the direction of Dmitri Ustinov — was a doctrine designed to make the Soviet Union independent of clients like Castro.** The Chinese leadership, which had received an account of the crisis through its own intelligence channels and had been openly contemptuous of Khrushchev's withdrawal, drew the opposite conclusion: that the Soviet Union had proved itself unwilling to fight for socialist internationalism at the decisive moment, and that the Chinese revolution would henceforth pursue an independent nuclear capability on the assumption that the Soviet umbrella was worthless. The Sino-Soviet split, which had begun in 1960 as an ideological dispute, became after October 1962 a strategic breach. The American leadership, which never learned of Castro's cable until the Havana conferences of 1989 and 1992, drew from the crisis the conclusion that graduated pressure and communication of resolve had produced Soviet capitulation, and generalised this conclusion into the theory of coercive diplomacy that would be applied disastrously in Vietnam between 1965 and 1968. The Cuban leadership, which never forgave Khrushchev for the withdrawal, entered into a period of alienation from Moscow that lasted until the direct Soviet intervention in Cuban economic planning in 1972 and that shaped Cuban foreign policy — the deployments to Algeria, to Angola, to Ethiopia, to Grenada — into a distinctively independent internationalism that operated in parallel to Soviet policy rather than under its direction.

The instructive feature of the episode is that the crisis was resolved by a mechanism that neither of the two principal parties fully understood at the time and that the dominant historical account has continued to obscure. **Kennedy did not force Khrushchev to withdraw. Castro forced Khrushchev to withdraw, by demonstrating that the alternative to withdrawal was not a controlled confrontation with the United States but an uncontrolled cascade toward general nuclear war driven by a third party.** The lesson, which has application to every subsequent case of extended deterrence — from the American nuclear commitments to South Korea and to Taiwan, to the Russian nuclear commitments to Belarus and to Kazakhstan in the 1990s, to the contemporary question of whether North Korea's strategic behaviour can be predicted from Chinese preferences — is that the extension of nuclear guarantees to ideologically committed clients creates a class of risks that cannot be managed by the metropolitan power alone, because the client's escalation calculus is not identical to the metropolitan power's escalation calculus and cannot be brought into identity by any mechanism short of the removal of the client's independent capacity to act. The Cuban Missile Crisis ended because Khrushchev discovered this fact about Castro in seventy-two hours and drew the correct conclusion from it. The subsequent history of nuclear extended deterrence is largely the history of powers that did not draw the conclusion, or drew it and then forgot it.

The cable of 27 October 1962 is preserved in the Presidential Archive of the Russian Federation, Fond 3, Opis 65, Delo 903, Listy 65–67. It was declassified in 1990 and published in Russian in the collection *Prezidium TsK KPSS 1954–1964* (Rosspen, 2003). Castro, when confronted with the cable at the Havana conference of January 1992, in the presence of Robert McNamara and Arthur Schlesinger, acknowledged its authenticity and defended its contents. He was eighty-five years old when he died in November 2016. He never retracted the argument.
